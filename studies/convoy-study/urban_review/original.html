Sandtable Urban Resupply

A research planning page: what the Army does, what Sandtable must simulate, and how we would test it

“Can essential supplies reach the right recipients, in the required quantities, before they are needed?”

Sandtable will evaluate how vehicle capabilities, logistics organization and human supervision affect the reliable delivery of essential supplies through disrupted urban networks. This page does not assume that autonomy or reinforcement learning is the answer. A useful result could show that autonomy helps, that a simpler planning method performs just as well, or that some other constraint dominates both.

Part 1

Define the mission

Who decidesSustainment planners and distribution leaders: the staff who match supplies to requests, and the platoons that load, move and hand them over.
Who depends on itThe receiving units. They consume water, fuel and other supplies continuously, and they run out if deliveries arrive late or short.
What counts as a completed deliveryThe right supply type, in the right quantity, handed over to and confirmed by the intended recipient before its deadline.
Why arrival is not successA vehicle can reach the right street while the recipient has moved, the unloading space is blocked, or the load is wrong. Sandtable's current convoy scenario carries no cargo at all, so today it measures movement, not resupply.

The decision Sandtable should improve. Given limited vehicles, personnel, energy and communications, which delivery concept meets demand most reliably, and which capabilities are actually necessary? The decision-maker is a sustainment planner or capability developer who wants to reduce uncertainty before expensive vehicle trials or investments.

1. Identify demand and prepare supplies

Recipients report stock and request what they need. Supplies are counted and loaded.

Analogy: a store packs grocery orders

2. Allocate vehicles and plan movement

Planners assign vehicles, loads and routes, and coordinate when each recipient can receive.

Analogy: deciding which van takes which orders

3. Move through a disrupted network

Streets close, radios drop, vehicles break and must be recovered. Plans must adapt.

Analogy: road works and a dead phone battery

4. Hand over, report, prepare again

The recipient confirms receipt, stock is updated, and the vehicle returns for the next mission.

Analogy: the customer signs for the delivery

Where military logistics gets harder than grocery delivery. Streets can close without warning, communications fail exactly when they are needed, broken vehicles must be recovered under time pressure, recipients move, every crew member on the road is exposed, and running out of water or fuel is dangerous rather than inconvenient.

Logistics packages and vehicle-based distribution

Observed exercise practice
Documented practiceDistribution platoons move the logistics package (LOGPAC) convoy to, from and during logistics release point (LRP) and rearm/refuel/resupply point (R3P) operations. Palletized Load System trucks appear in the account.
Reported difficultyPlatoon leaders are often junior lieutenants with limited training. Ammunition delays push the convoy off schedule, and a missed turn on the main supply route follows.
Implication for SandtableA trip is a package with contents and a destination, not just a vehicle reaching a node.

Source: “Stewards of Kings: Preparing Distribution Platoon for the National Training Center”, CPT John M. LeClair, NTC observer/coach-trainer, Field Artillery, June 2, 2025.

Cargo accountability and recipient coordination

Observed exercise practiceProfessional recommendation
Documented practiceSoldiers must know which combat configured loads are on hand and where they are. The company commander and platoon leader coordinate with the battalion S-4 for updates, and an ammunition tracker is kept in the logistics operations center.
Reported difficultyWithout current updates, leaders cannot tell what has been delivered or what is still needed.
Implication for SandtableModel supply type, quantity, requests and receipt confirmation. Count what the recipient received, not what left the depot.

Source: Stewards of Kings, June 2025.

Temporary resupply points and site changes

Observed exercise practiceProfessional recommendation
Documented practiceGround units rehearse LRP and R3P execution crawl-walk-run, and the author recommends finishing bulk fuel and ammunition resupply within two hours. In an aviation exercise in Korea (Operation Saber Shield), a forward support troop set up temporary arming and refueling sites. By the final mission it could arrive, operate and leave in under 30 minutes. When one site was compromised, aircraft were redirected to an alternate site in real time.
Reported difficultySetup, service and departure time is exposure time. Moving loads was the main bottleneck. A load-handling system with a flat rack cut transport time about in half.
Implication for SandtableRepresent setup time, service time, teardown and relocation, plus the interruption a move causes. The aviation case is not an urban ground procedure.

Sources: Stewards of Kings, June 2025; “Sustainment Forces and Survivability in Large-Scale Combat Operations”, CPT Peter P. Cho and CPT Reed T. Marshall, Aviation Digest Winter 2026, published March 10, 2026. Opening two sites at once is the authors' proposal for the future.

Movement control and route planning

Observed exercise practice
Documented practiceA 396-vehicle formation covered more than 1,400 km in four days. Convoy support centers every two hours handled driver swaps and vehicle checks. Movement control teams reported to the regimental operations cell and kept host-nation traffic agencies informed so leaders could adjust movement windows. Distribution platoons navigate main and alternate supply routes, including at night.
Reported difficultyNavigation errors and missed turns. Equipment readiness fell from 88% to about 79% once the convoy started moving.
Implication for SandtableA road graph with travel times, closures, alternate routes and movement windows. This was a road march across open country, not urban combat evidence.

Sources: “Going the Distance: Planning, Sustaining, and Executing a Multi-Day Tactical Road March”, MAJ Austin Faulkner and CPT Elijah Lapid, July 15, 2026; Stewards of Kings.

Maintenance, recovery and cargo transfer

Observed exercise practiceProfessional recommendation
Documented practiceMost breakdowns happened before the first support center. Recovery used organic assets where possible, and trailers following the convoy carried vehicles that could not move. A dashboard forecast which vehicles could be repaired within 24 to 48 hours. At the National Training Center, maintenance collection points must keep moving with the support area.
Reported difficultyUnits that forced maintenance points to move together with the trains command post suffered “significant maintenance shortfalls” and lower readiness.
Implication for SandtableBreakdown, recovery and repair take time and resources. A relocation costs service time. Maintenance is never an instant reset.

Sources: Going the Distance, July 2026; “Practice Makes Perfect: Training Sustainment in the Era of Contested Logistics”, LTC Boyce J. Newton III, Army Sustainment, July 16, 2025.

Communications fallback and delayed reporting

Observed exercise practiceProfessional recommendation
Documented practiceDuring the road march, faults and work orders were recorded offline and synchronized once a unit reached a support center with connectivity. NTC observers recommend redundant digital and analog tracking because “digital-based products inevitably experience outages.” In the aviation exercise, SINCGARS radios were unreliable beyond line of sight, so the team used JBC-P and a mesh radio network.
Reported difficultyRadios that are not fully mission capable mean orders may not reach the convoy. Poor beyond-line-of-sight proficiency leads to poor battle tracking.
Implication for SandtableDelayed messages, stale inventory reports, information age and explicit fallback procedures, not just “connected” or “disconnected.”

Sources: Going the Distance; Practice Makes Perfect; Stewards of Kings; Sustainment Forces and Survivability.

Experimental remote and autonomous delivery

Experimental demonstrationSandtable project proposal
Documented practiceAt Project Convergence Capstone 5 (Pearl Harbor, April 2025), uncrewed surface vessels controlled from about 5,000 miles away carried a supply-laden ground robot toward a simulated contested port. Control passed to a local harbormaster. The robot, remotely operated from about 3,000 miles away, drove to a Humvee and delivered its payload.
Reported difficultyThe scenario was simulated, and the article reports no timing, payload or failure data. It is a public affairs account, and some claims look ahead to capabilities not shown.
Implication for SandtableRemote operation, supervision and control handover are legitimate things to model. They do not establish that autonomous urban delivery fleets are routine. The Sandtable one-pager separately proposes an autonomous march unit: one crewed control vehicle leading four autonomous trucks.

Sources: “Redefining logistics: Army demonstrates breakthrough in autonomous ship-to-shore resupply”, army.mil, April 29, 2025; Sandtable contested logistics one-pager.

Motor transport doctrine

Published doctrineSandtable project proposal
Documented practiceThe Sandtable one-pager builds its reference convoy from ATP 4-11, Army Motor Transport Operations. A march unit has up to 25 vehicles, a serial has 2 to 5 march units, and a column has 2 to 5 serials, with head, main body and trail roles.
Reported difficultyDoctrine describes how convoys are organized. It does not define one standard vehicle arrangement for every contested urban mission.
Implication for SandtableThe 15-vehicle reference convoy is a declared experimental configuration. Results should be read against it, not presented as the Army's standard urban convoy.

Source: Sandtable one-pager. We have not independently reviewed the ATP 4-11 text for this page.

The urban evidence gap. None of these reports studies urban resupply. Narrow streets, blocked access, limited unloading space, unreliable positioning and buildings that block radio signals change the operation in ways these reports do not settle. Every urban feature on this page is labelled Proposed urban adaptation and needs its own validation.

Where Sandtable stands today. According to the project one-pager, Sandtable already has formations, leader-follow behavior, a C0 to C5 communications ladder, detection and attrition, and replay traces. The existing convoy scenario carries and delivers nothing. Cargo and delivery are proposed (issue #9), along with a street graph, route stationing and an autonomous march unit. The current success measure is the fraction of vehicles that survive.

Later extensions that need a justification first

Add these only after showing that leaving them out changes the logistics decision.

Radio propagation. A building that blocks line of sight does not necessarily block radio. Model RF separately from optical visibility if outage windows prove too crude.
Adaptive adversary. The literature note warns that static adversaries can invert recommendations. Introduce an ensemble of doctrinal templates, not one fixed red.
Combat mechanics and attrition. Detailed engagement models add weight that resupply questions may not need.
Building interiors and detailed terrain. Useful for access questions only once street-level access is validated.
Learned (RL) dispatch policies. Only if rolling-horizon optimization leaves a material, repeatable gap.
Deep surrogates. The literature note warns of surrogate bias. Report policy-equivalence tests, not fit statistics.

    Part 5

    Compare baselines before adding AI

    All three approaches see the same scenario, the same demand, the same vehicles and the same information. None of them is learned. Approach A is not called optimization because it does not optimize. Approach B is a real optimizer. Approach C is a clearly specified candidate, not reinforcement learning. Any of them is allowed to win.

    A · Conventional dispatch declared procedure

    Rule
    Every 2 hours, trucks leave in a fixed rotation of recipients, each carrying a package sized from that recipient's last report. Between runs, urgent requests are served first-come, first-served by any idle vehicle.
    Information
    Last stock reports and requests the dispatcher has received.
    Resources
    3 trucks (6 pallets each), optional UGV for urgent requests, 1 dispatcher.

    B · Optimization with periodic replanning traditional OR

    Rule
    Every 30 minutes, solve the next-trip assignment for all idle vehicles exactly, by enumerating every combination. The objective is predicted pallet-minutes below required stock over the next 4 hours, plus a small cost per trip.
    Information
    Same reports and requests as A, plus nominal consumption rates and estimated arrival times of loads already on the road.
    Resources
    Same as A.

    C · Candidate adaptive approach hypothesis, not RL

    Rule
    The same optimizer as B, but re-solved immediately whenever new information arrives (a request, a closure report, a breakdown report, a returning vehicle, restored communications) and at least every 15 minutes. Loads are capped at half capacity, giving smaller, more frequent deliveries.
    Information
    Same as B, used sooner.
    Resources
    Same as A.

    Control mode is a separate factor with equal vehicle capability. Crewed trucks carry two soldiers each and reroute themselves. Supervised autonomous trucks have no one on board, but they ask the single operator for help when they meet an unexpected closure, when their perception is uncertain, and to confirm each handover. Requests wait in a queue, and they cannot reach the operator during a communications outage. Speed, capacity and street access are the same in both modes. The small UGV carries 2 pallets, is slower, can use narrow streets, and is always supervised.

    Keep calibration and validation apart. Use some runs to estimate parameters such as loading time, travel speed and intervention rate. Then test predictions on different runs, with different layouts, demand and disruption sequences. Agree on acceptable prediction error before testing. Agreement with ProjectGL is useful evidence that two models agree. It is not validation against reality, and an operator queue model cannot by itself establish real trust or workload.

    Part 7

    The research decision: a one-page charter

    Problem
    Supply continuity in an urban network with uncertain access, information and transport capacity. Today Sandtable measures movement and survival, not whether supplies arrive.
    Decision-maker
    A sustainment planner or capability developer choosing a delivery concept and the capabilities to invest in.
    Research question
    Under uncertain demand, intermittent communications and temporary road closures, when does supervised autonomous transport improve on-time delivery or reduce personnel requirements, compared with a strong conventional planning baseline?
    Conventional baseline
    An expert-reviewed, doctrine-informed logistics-package procedure (approach A), plus rolling-horizon vehicle-routing optimization (approach B) as the strong traditional competitor.
    Candidate improvement
    Supervised autonomous control with the same vehicle capability. Separately, event-triggered replanning with smaller batches (approach C).
    Primary outcome
    The proportion of required supplies received by the required time, reported by supply type and recipient. Supporting outcomes are time below required stock, backlog, vehicle downtime, crew and operator hours, interventions and energy, kept separate rather than merged into one score.
    Registered hypotheses
    1. Better conventional replanning captures much of the benefit attributed to autonomy.
    2. Autonomy reduces onboard staffing while increasing intervention or recovery demands elsewhere.
    3. Smaller batches improve some resilience measures while increasing total transport effort.
    Each is allowed to fail.
    Assumptions and evidence gaps
    • No public Army report studies urban resupply directly.
    • Consumption, loading, intervention and repair parameters are placeholders until measured.
    • Communications are modelled as outage windows, not radio propagation.
    • The adversary is abstract (closures, outages, breakdowns), not adaptive.
    Physical validation plan
    A closed-course trial with inert cargo, scripted closures, software-injected outages and a declared breakdown. Calibrate on some runs and test predictions on held-out runs with a new layout and disruption sequence.
    When the improvement is not worthwhile
    • Approach A or B comes within a pre-agreed margin on the primary outcome.
    • Intervention demand exceeds one operator's capacity, or gains vanish during communications loss.
    • Crew savings are offset by recovery and operator effort.
    • Vehicle availability or loading capacity, not control mode, turns out to be the binding constraint.
    Questions for the professor
    1. Who will use the result, and what decision will it change?
    2. Which supply mission and operating horizon are we studying?
    3. Which conventional procedure would an experienced sustainer accept as credible?
    4. What improvement would justify more autonomy or equipment?
    5. What people, vehicle data and test access can we realistically obtain?
