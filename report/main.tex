\begin{abstract}
Designing human-machine teams for contested ground operations means choosing how operators control robotic assets, which routes formations take, and how much reconnaissance to field, under communications an adversary can degrade. ProjectGL, the Army's Government-furnished high-fidelity ground simulation framework, is the authoritative engine for that work, but each mission runs at or near real time, so broad design searches are impractical. We use a two-tier workflow in which a fast model explores many designs and ProjectGL confirms only the ones that matter, and this paper builds the fast tier. \sandtable{} keeps the effects that change teaming decisions (movement, sensing, engagement, operator attention, and contested communications) and drops the vehicle physics and rendering that make ProjectGL expensive, so a full mission finishes in about a tenth of a second. It builds on agent-based combat models and military operations simulation: effect-based engagement, multi-replication designed experiments, and validation against aggregate attrition laws, and the same loop supports Bayesian sequential design of experiments and Bayesian test and evaluation. Its central finding is a sufficiency result. An operator that never errs, never slows, and never tires is still enough to produce the span pathologies usually attributed to human attention, because the pathologies come from the queue its clients form rather than from the operator. Attention arguments alone therefore cannot set span doctrine, and the effective span moves under levers that touch no operator at all. The operator is a server whose clients abandon it, and one that never learns it has been abandoned spends service time producing answers nobody is waiting for, so what defeats direct human control at high span is congestion, not attention dilution. Two closed forms locate the failure, a collapse span and a ceiling on serviceable latency, and the simulator obeys both; a third splits the collapse span into a band inside which a team has two self-sustaining states, so that at the single span the band covers, mission success moves by \qBandSuccess{} on the starting condition alone. Because effective span is a queueing outcome, it moves under software, and three levers move it without adding an operator or a radio. Explicit admission control takes mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qAdmitClear\% at the centerpiece operating point and from \qTeleBlindClear\% to \qTeleAdmitClear\% under teleoperation; of that gain, \qRefundShare\% comes from discarding work that cannot be finished in time rather than from telling the requester, and the same discard closes the bistable band. A vehicle that halves its own request rate when the console refuses it, and eases back up when served, cuts the console's offered load \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} at twelve assets over admission control alone while raising mission success \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten, and costs nothing below the collapse span. It does this by having the console serve \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} rather than more, which is worth stating plainly because the ratio metric the field reports, serviced over requested, moves in the opposite direction: without the response rule, refusing a vehicle sends it back to autonomy early and it returns to the queue early, so the console is asked \rtAskedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} more often while serving \rtServedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{}. Every span-of-control number measured as a ratio of served to requested is inflatable by any policy that changes the asking, and the policies worth recommending all do. And doubling the hold after each unanswered request, which is Ethernet's binary exponential backoff, reaches the band-closing floor of \qBandFloor{} steps. Taken together the two console-side levers raise mission success by \aiStackClearEight{} percentage points at the centerpiece span against a console running no policy at all, and cut the fraction of the blue force lost by \rtLossStackOverBlindClearEight{} points over the same runs; both are fractions of the force fielded rather than of the requests made, so neither can be moved by a policy that changes the asking. None of the three levers needs the console to know the span it will be flown at, the delivery rate, or the service rate. The account also says what a span-of-control experiment must declare before its numbers can be compared with anyone else's. Sweeping span with the defending force fixed raises mission success \atFixDir{}; sweeping the identical span with the force ratio held reverses it to \atHeldDir{}, a difference in differences of \atDid{} ($t = \atDidT{}$) over \atDidN{} seed-matched blocks, and the reversal repeats on the continuous arrival fraction that mission success thresholds. Removing the console bounds about \atConsoleShare\% of that collapse to the operator's queue and the rest to the scenario. The best control mode therefore shifts with conditions: direct human control retains a narrow edge only at small span on a nearly clear link, while at high span on-platform autonomy is better at every communications level. The same mechanism makes jamming \emph{help} an unaware operator, so an electronic-warfare ladder that raises latency and message loss together confounds two effects of opposite sign. Route choice traces a speed-survivability tradeoff, with covered routes far more successful but much slower than exposed ones, and a reconnaissance swarm that cues ground shooters raises mission success sharply until jamming weakens the chain. Drawing on the Russo-Ukrainian war, we add opt-in electronic-warfare-immune command links and short-range air defense against the swarm, the latter giving swarm size a survivability cost that turns its otherwise monotone benefit into an interior optimum. \sandtable{} is the fast search companion to ProjectGL for Army mission engineering.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. Army's shift toward human-machine teams, dismounted squads working with robotic combat vehicles, uncrewed aircraft, and sensor nodes, turns tactics into a design problem. A mission engineer must decide how one operator's attention is divided across autonomous assets, whether control should be direct or supervisory, which route a formation takes, and how large a recon swarm should be, all under communications that an adversary actively degrades. Each of these is a knob, and the knobs interact: the best control modality depends on the communications state, and the value of a recon swarm depends on whether its picture survives jamming. Answering such questions demands experiments that sweep the knobs and measure outcomes against an engineered ground truth, not one-off demonstrations.

ProjectGL, the Army's Government-furnished simulation framework, is the authoritative, high-fidelity environment for this work \citep{stanko2025projectgl}. It models vehicle dynamics, sensing, and synthetic terrain in real time. That fidelity is exactly what makes it unsuitable for the \emph{inner loop} of a design study: each mission runs at or near real time, so a modest experiment of a few hundred design points, each averaged over a few hundred stochastic replications, would take weeks of wall-clock time. Bayesian optimization and sensitivity analysis need the objective evaluated thousands of times, quickly and reproducibly. The fidelity that makes ProjectGL the right tool for crew-station development and final validation makes it the wrong tool for searching a design space.

We resolve this with a two-tier workflow. A fast, low-fidelity, mission-level model searches the design space and locates the decision-relevant regions; the high-fidelity engine then validates the handful of designs that matter. This paper builds and exercises the fast tier. \sandtable{} is a pure-Python simulator that keeps the mission-level effects that drive design decisions, movement over trafficable terrain, probabilistic sensing and engagement, a two-tier command model, and a communications/EW degradation ladder, while discarding the physics and rendering that dominate high-fidelity cost. It follows the ``accurate enough, not high fidelity'' principle: the model is kept only as detailed as it must be to produce the right \emph{ordering} of design alternatives.

A model built for a search loop must satisfy two properties that ordinary simulators often do not. It must be a \emph{pure function} of its inputs, with no global or hidden state, so that a run is reproducible from its seed, cacheable, and safe to evaluate in parallel. It must also carry an \emph{engineered ground truth}, a known correct answer for each trial, so that outcomes are scored against truth rather than against agreement with the model's own agents. \sandtable{} is designed around both.

The contributions of this paper are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \sandtable{}, a fast, reproducible, pure-Python mission-level simulator whose
\texttt{run\_mission} contract is a pure function of (scenario, seed, parameters), wired into a Bayesian-optimization and design of experiment (DOE) orchestrator and an HPC batch runner as the fast inner-loop companion to ProjectGL (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item A control-modality model, direct human-in-the-loop versus supervisory autonomy, coupled to
span of control and a C0--C5 communications/EW ladder, that reproduces a \emph{moving optimum}: the communications level at which autonomy overtakes human control falls as span rises (Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}).
\item The mechanism behind that optimum, stated in closed form and then measured: the operator is
a server with client abandonment, and one that does not detect abandonment sustains its own overload above a collapse span $n^{\star} = c(P+\tau+1)\mu/d$ and a serviceable-latency ceiling $\ell^{\star} = (P - 1/\mu)/2$. Both thresholds are confirmed against staffing and patience sweeps; explicit admission control is worth \qAdmitClear\% against \qBlindClear\% mission success at the centerpiece operating point, and the same mechanism makes message loss beneficial to an undetecting operator, which separates the two axes an EW ladder normally conflates (Section~\ref{subsec:queue}).
\item An attribution of that span effect, which reverses its own sign under the control every
span-of-control study needs and few report: sweeping span with the defending force scaled so the ratio never moves turns a \atFixDir{} gain into a \atHeldDir{} loss, a difference in differences of \atDid{} at $t = \atDidT{}$ over \atDidN{} seed-matched blocks. Bounding the console away with supervisory control puts about \atConsoleShare\% of the collapse at the operator's queue and \atScenarioShare\% at the scenario, and the same decomposition holds on the continuous arrival fraction that mission success thresholds, which rules out the metric's own sharpening as its cause (Section~\ref{subsec:attrib}).
\item A bistability in span of control, derived and then gated: when a vehicle that went
unanswered asks again sooner than one that was served, offered load depends on the state the queue
is already in, and the collapse threshold splits into a band $(n_c, n_h)$ of width
$c\mu\pi(\tau-\rho)(1-a_h)/d$ inside which a team has two self-sustaining states. At the one span
the band covers, mission success moves by \qBandSuccess{} on the initial condition alone; a console
that discards work it cannot finish shrinks that to \qBandRefund{}, and a backoff floor of
\qBandFloor{} steps closes the band outright (Section~\ref{subsec:band}).
\item A measurement result that applies to any span-of-control study reporting a service ratio, and which cost this paper a headline before it was found. Operator goodput, serviced over requested, has a denominator that every policy worth recommending moves: explicit admission control returns a refused vehicle to autonomy after one round trip instead of after its full patience, so it is asked \rtAskedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} more often at twelve assets while serving \rtServedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{}, and a client-side rate rule suppresses requests by construction and so flatters itself by the same arithmetic in reverse. Read on absolute rates the rate rule serves \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} rather than more, and it is the offered load that falls, by \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{}. A contrast between arms whose request rates differ cannot be reported on a ratio whose denominator is the request count (Section~\ref{subsec:queue}).
\item Three decision-relevant findings for Army human-machine teaming, the moving control optimum,
the speed-survivability frontier of route choice (Section~\ref{subsec:uc3}), and the EW-induced collapse of a sensor-to-shooter chain (Section~\ref{subsec:uc5}), each generated from \nRep{}-replication Monte-Carlo studies with confidence intervals.
\item An analytic validation: \sandtable{}'s emergent attrition matches Lanchester's square law to
within \lanchMaxErr\%, with the residual explained by known discretization effects (Section~\ref{subsec:validation}).
\item A first-class kill-web extension, opt-in and byte-identical when off, in which each entity
carries suppression, a finite basic load of munitions, and a persistent belief/track state, and fires resolve against belief rather than truth; suppression, munitions, and deception each move mission outcomes as first-order effects, with the fixed-Pk baseline recovered as their degenerate limit (Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}).
\item A drone-war extension motivated by the Russo-Ukrainian war, also opt-in and byte-identical when
off: an electronic-warfare-immune command link that holds direct control under jamming, a counter-UAS air-defense layer that gives reconnaissance-swarm size a survivability cost and hence an interior optimum, and a cost-exchange metric for attritable mass (Section~\ref{subsec:drone}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\sandtable{} draws on four bodies of work: mission engineering and the ProjectGL environment it serves, human-machine teaming and supervisory control, agent-based combat simulation, and design of experiments with optimization.

\emph{Mission engineering and ProjectGL.} Mission engineering analyzes how systems combine to accomplish a mission and traces measures of effectiveness back to design choices \citep{raz2024mission,raz2024foundations}. A formal treatment casts the mission-engineering design space in a domain-specific language with set-based and interval-algebra constraints, and evaluates feasible system-of-systems configurations with graph theory and multiattribute utility, demonstrated on a notional sense-decide-effect command-and-control problem \citep{raz2024foundations}; \sandtable{} is an executable counterpart to that analysis, running a sense-decide-act loop forward under contested communications to score a configuration rather than enumerate it. ProjectGL provides the Army's shared, modular basis for such analysis in the ground domain \citep{stanko2025projectgl}. \sandtable{} does not replace it; it occupies the fast end of a fidelity spectrum, producing the response surfaces that tell an engineer which designs are worth running at high fidelity.

\emph{Human-machine teaming and supervisory control.} The distinction between direct and supervisory control dates to early telerobotics \citep{sheridan1978supervisory} and is set within the levels-of-automation framework, which places manual control and full autonomy at the ends of a continuum whose middle our direct-versus-supervisory axis samples \citep{parasuraman2000types}. The limits of a single operator's situation awareness \citep{endsley1995awareness} and attention set how many assets that operator can command: fan-out, the ratio of an asset's neglect time to the operator's interaction time, bounds the span \citep{olsen2004fanout}, and operator-capacity models estimate it from task timing \citep{cummings2007capacity}. Reviews of human-agent teaming and of one operator controlling many robots catalog the resulting interface and workload issues \citep{chen2014teaming,chen2011supervisory,lewis2013human}. The closest analytic precedent for our command model treats the operator as a dynamical queue serving asset requests \citep{savla2012queue}; \sandtable{} adopts that capacity-limited-server abstraction of operator attention (Section~\ref{sec:model}) and maps its behavior across the span-by-communications design space. What that line of work leaves out, and what Section~\ref{subsec:queue} turns on, is that the clients are impatient: an asset that waits past its patience stops waiting. Queues with abandonment are the Erlang-A family developed for call centers, where the quality-and-efficiency-driven regime and the effect of customer impatience on staffing are worked out in detail \citep{garnett2002impatient,gans2003callcenters}. The supervisory-control literature imports the server but not the abandonment, and fan-out \citep{olsen2004fanout}, even with the wait-time correction of \citet{cummings2008predicting}, has no queue in it at all, so it cannot express the failure mode we find. That literature also names the remedy we test: telling a waiting customer what its delay will be is a control on abandonment, and staffing and announcement are complementary levers on the same congestion \citep{ibrahim2018announcements}. On the supervisory side, the closest simulation precedent is a multirobot, multioperator surveillance model with explicit human submodels, used to trade the number and mix of operators and vehicles \citep{humann2023multioperator}; our multi-console staffing curve asks the narrower question of where the queue itself breaks. That the best automation level is itself condition-dependent \citep{kaber2004effects} is the moving optimum our centerpiece locates, and the over-reliance and rejection that flank it are the poles of automation trust \citep{parasuraman1997humans} the program's trust-calibration objective targets. For uncrewed-aircraft swarms specifically, a meta-analysis of swarm-management interfaces documents how operator performance degrades as swarm size and tempo grow \citep{hocraffer2017meta}, which motivates our sensor-swarm study.

\emph{Agent-based combat simulation.} Low-fidelity, effect-based combat models have a long lineage in the combat-distillation tradition: small agent-based models built to expose what drives an outcome rather than to reproduce physics. ISAAC and its successor EINSTein move agents by a weighted sum of attraction and repulsion propensities toward goals, friendly forces, and enemies \citep{ilachinski2000isaac,ilachinski2004artificial}, and MANA added terrain awareness and trigger-driven state changes to that scheme \citep{lauren2002mana}; \sandtable{}'s effect-based agents sit in this line, and we make the inheritance concrete with an opt-in personality-movement mode (Section~\ref{subsec:personality}). Such models are usually built in general agent frameworks, the Java MASON engine behind many defense studies \citep{luke2005mason} or, in Python, Mesa \citep{masad2015mesa}, whose per-agent object pattern we follow in spirit but replace with a bespoke vectorized state for a tight optimization loop. Flocking rules supply formation movement from local interactions \citep{reynolds1987flocks}, and operation-time frameworks schedule swarm tasks against a mission clock \citep{wei2013operation}. A recurring lesson in this literature is to validate an emergent model against a closed-form law; we adopt Lanchester's square law \citep{lanchester1916aircraft}, in the modern treatment of \citet{kress2020lanchester}, for that purpose.

\emph{Design of experiments and optimization.} The studies here are batch DOE sweeps in the data-farming tradition, running a fast model across a designed parameter space to reveal response structure and outliers \citep{horne2004datafarming}; space-filling designs keep that practical as the factor count grows \citep{cioppa2007nolh}, and the statistical basis for designing simulation experiments and fitting metamodels to their output is well established \citep{kleijnen2015doe}. The same plumbing supports Bayesian optimization via Monte-Carlo acquisition functions \citep{balandat2020botorch} when a design space is too large to grid, one instance of the broader simulation-optimization family \citep{amaran2016simulation} in which a cheap surrogate is fit to a simulator and optimized in its place \citep{barton2006metamodel}. The core is built on vectorized array programming \citep{harris2020numpy} and follows standard Monte-Carlo practice, independent seeded replications summarized with confidence intervals \citep{law2015simulation}.

\section{The \sandtable{} Model}
\label{sec:model}

\sandtable{} is a layered mission-level model: a pure-function evaluation contract wraps a vectorized state and a fixed-step loop over movement, sensing, engagement, command, and communications (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The subsections below describe each layer and the two properties that make the model fit for a search loop.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture.pdf}
\caption{\sandtable{} structure. A pure \texttt{run\_mission} builds the world (terrain rasters) and the structure-of-arrays entity state from a scenario, seed, and design parameters, then advances a fixed-step pipeline, command tasking, planning, motion, sensing, engagement, that reads and writes that state each step, and returns scalar mission KPIs. \texttt{evaluate} averages the KPIs over seeded replications, and a design-of-experiments and Bayesian-optimization orchestrator drives batches on the HPC cluster.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The top-level interface is a pure function
\[
\texttt{run\_mission}(\text{scenario}, \text{seed}, \text{params}) \;\rightarrow\; \text{metrics},
\]
with no module-level state, no global random number generator, and no file or wall-clock access in the hot path. A companion \texttt{evaluate} averages the metrics across a requested number of seeded replications. This boundary is what makes a run reproducible from its seed, cacheable, and safe to evaluate concurrently, and it is what lets the optimizer treat the simulator as a black-box objective. All randomness is drawn from a single generator seeded per replication, so identical seeds reproduce identical metrics bit for bit, and matched seed blocks across paired studies implement common random numbers for low-variance comparison.


Entities (ground vehicles, uncrewed aircraft, sensor and command nodes, and defenders) are stored as a structure of arrays: position, heading, health, sensor and weapon parameters, and side live in parallel NumPy arrays rather than in per-agent objects \citep{harris2020numpy}. The mission advances on a fixed time step (1~s here). Each step updates, in order, command tasking, planning, motion, sensing, and engagement (the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). This fixed-step pipeline is a deliberate reduction of the discrete-event executive that full engagement engines such as AFSIM use, whose components self-schedule on their own update intervals \citep{rainey2024afsim}; the discrete-event-system formalism makes that model-simplification relation precise \citep{zeigler2019theory}. This vectorized design runs a full mission of up to 1800 steps in roughly 0.1~s on a single core, about 7 to 11 replications per second depending on scenario, so the 44,100 replications behind this paper are one to two core-hours of compute, trivially parallel across a cluster.

\subsection{Movement, sensing, and engagement}
\emph{Movement.} Ground platforms follow a unicycle kinematic model: each turns its heading toward its current waypoint at a turn-rate limit and advances at
\[
v = v_{\max}\;\tau\;g(x,y)\;(0.35 + 0.65\,q),
\]
where $\tau$ is a scenario tempo factor, $g(x,y)\in[0,1]$ is the terrain trafficability sampled from a precomputed raster (air platforms ignore it), and $q$ is the platform's control quality (Section~\ref{subsec:command}), so a poorly controlled or stalled vehicle also loses tempo. Speed is capped so a platform never oversteps its waypoint in one step. The waypoint comes from a \texttt{route\_bias} parameter that sets a lateral lane between a fast exposed corridor and a slow covered route, holds that lane through the threat, and converges onto the objective only over the final stretch, so a covered route stays in defilade past the defenders.

\emph{Sensing and shared awareness.} Sensing is a cookie-cutter range gate feeding a per-side shared picture. A target is placed on the opposing side's picture if any living observer holds it within sensor range with clear line of sight, and then only with probability $1-c_{\text{conceal}}$ at the target's location, so concealment lowers detectability. The picture is \emph{shared}: a contact any friendly sensor holds is visible to every friendly shooter, which is what lets recon aircraft cue ground vehicles that have not detected the threat themselves. A contact held \emph{only} by an air sensor must be relayed, so under electronic warfare it is dropped from the shared picture with the link's message-drop probability (Section~\ref{subsec:comms}); a contact also held organically by a ground sensor is unaffected. Line of sight is a flat-terrain stub in the present model.

\emph{Engagement.} Engagement is aimed fire. Every living shooter engages its nearest detected enemy within weapon range, and a kill is a Bernoulli draw with probability
\[
p_k = p_k^{\text{base}}\,(1 - c_{\text{cover}})\,q_{\text{shooter}}\,(2 - q_{\text{target}}),
\]
clipped to $[0,1]$, where $c_{\text{cover}}$ is terrain cover at the target: cover reduces incoming lethality, and control quality couples both ways, since a well-controlled shooter fires more effectively and a poorly controlled target exposes itself. Both sides fire against the same pre-step state and damage is applied simultaneously, so there is no turn-order bias; recon aircraft carry no weapon and are not targeted, as the baseline models no short-range air defense (Section~\ref{subsec:drone} adds it as an opt-in layer). This effect-based abstraction is deliberately coarse; Section~\ref{subsec:validation} shows it still obeys the right aggregate law.

\subsection{Command model: direct versus supervisory}
\label{subsec:command}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{command_model.pdf}
\caption{The command model, the mechanism behind the moving optimum. Under \emph{direct} control an asset requests a single shared operator over the comms/EW link and waits: the reply is high quality when it arrives in time, low while the asset waits, and poor when the link drops it. Under \emph{supervisory} control each asset decides locally at a steady autonomous quality, independent of the link. More supervised assets both dilute the operator's per-decision quality and lengthen its queue.}
\label{fig:command}
\end{figure}

The command layer is where the human-machine teaming questions live (Figure~\ref{fig:command}). In \emph{supervisory} mode each asset runs on-platform autonomy at a steady control quality $q_{\text{auto}}$, independent of communications. In \emph{direct} mode an asset requests the operator over the modeled link, paying a round trip of $2\ell$ steps at one-way latency $\ell$ with per-message drop probability $p_{\text{drop}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), and the single operator serves queued requests at a fixed rate $\mu$. A request answered within the asset's patience returns the high operator quality $q_{\text{operator}}$; while it waits the asset holds a low stall quality $q_{\text{stall}}$; and a dropped or timed-out request drops the asset to a poor autonomous fallback $q_{\text{fallback}}$. Crucially, the operator is a single server shared across all supervised assets. Attention dilutes with span: the effective operator quality is
\[
q_{\text{operator}}^{\text{eff}} = q_{\text{auto}} + \big(q_{\text{operator}} - q_{\text{auto}}\big)\, \min\!\Big(1, \tfrac{\kappa}{n}\Big),
\]
where $n$ is the number of supervised assets and $\kappa$ the operator's attention (span) capacity (here $q_{\text{auto}}=0.70$, $q_{\text{operator}}=0.95$, $q_{\text{stall}}=0.30$, $q_{\text{fallback}}=0.50$, $\kappa=4$). Below capacity the operator adds full value; above it, requests queue and the human-in-the-loop advantage erodes. Control quality feeds engagement (a well-controlled shooter is more effective; a poorly controlled or stalled asset exposes itself), so the abstract quality translates into mission outcomes. This model, an operator as a capacity-limited server whose value is gated by both span and link quality, is the mechanism behind the moving optimum of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}, and it is grounded in the operator-capacity and supervisory-control literature \citep{cummings2007capacity,chen2011supervisory}.

\paragraph{Abandonment, and what the operator does about it.} The queue has one further property that turns out to drive the results of Section~\ref{subsec:queue}: an asset that has waited longer than its patience $P$ stops waiting. That makes the operator an $M/D/1$ server with abandonment, the Erlang-A family studied in the call-center literature \citep{garnett2002impatient,gans2003callcenters}, and it raises a design question that the fan-out tradition does not ask, because fan-out has no queue in it \citep{olsen2004fanout,cummings2008predicting}. The question is whether the server discovers the abandonment, and \sandtable{} carries all three answers as the parameter \texttt{queue\_policy}. Under \texttt{blind}, the default and the behavior of the model as first published, the operator works every request it receives, including requests whose answer will arrive after the requester has given up, so the service slot is spent either way. Under \texttt{refund} the operator silently drops work it cannot finish in time. Under \texttt{admit} it also tells the requester, which goes autonomous after one round trip instead of after $P$ steps: explicit admission control. Naming the regime this produces is worth doing precisely, because it is neither new nor a queueing result. A server that spends its capacity producing work discarded before it reaches anyone is \emph{congestion collapse from undelivered packets}, the failure Floyd and Fall describe for a network busy carrying traffic that a downstream link will drop \citep{floyd1999congestion}. The correspondence is exact in mechanism and inexact in what is wasted: there the waste is upstream bandwidth on a path whose far end discards, here it is the server itself and the discard is the requester's own patience running out. Each of the two literatures supplies the half the other lacks. Erlang-A has the abandonment but reports no collapse, because a call-center agent learns of a hang-up at once, so wasted service is a footnote rather than a regime; the networking literature has the collapse but no human, its senders being protocol state machines whose patience is a timeout constant someone chose. A supervisory console inherits both at the same time, which is why the two remedies that work in Section~\ref{subsec:queue} and Section~\ref{subsec:band}, an explicit congestion signal and a retransmission backoff, are the two that literature already fields \citep{jacobson1988congestion}.

A console cannot discard work it cannot finish in time unless it knows when the time runs out, and that is an interface requirement rather than a modeling convenience. The requester's deadline has to travel with the request. That is the ordinary construction in protocols that face the same problem, where a message carries its own expiry and any server along the path may drop it once expired, and it is the assumption under which every \texttt{refund} and \texttt{admit} number in this paper should be read: a console attached to a request format that does not carry a deadline can implement neither, and the levers are worth nothing to it until the format changes. Whether a fielded console has that field is an empirical question, and it is a cheaper one to answer than any of the ones this simulator asks.

Two thresholds follow in closed form, and Section~\ref{subsec:queue} shows the simulator obeying both. A reply fits inside the requester's patience only while $2\ell + 1/\mu \le P$, so above a one-way latency of
\[
\ell^{\star} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(P - 1/\mu\big) = \qLatCut{}\text{ steps}
\]
no request can be answered at all and the drop probability becomes inert. And in the collapsed regime every request abandons, so each asset runs a fixed cycle between requests: $P$ steps waiting the request out, $\tau$ steps of cooldown, and one step on which the next decision event fires. A \texttt{blind} operator still pays $1/\mu$ for every one of them. With $c$ consoles and two-way delivery probability $d = (1-p_{\text{drop}})^2$ the queue therefore sustains its own overload once the span exceeds
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nstar}
n^{\star} = \frac{c\,(P + \tau + 1)\,\mu}{d},
\end{equation}
which at the centerpiece's operating point ($\mu = \qServiceRate{}$, $P = \qPatience{}$, $\tau = \qDecisionInterval{}$) is $\qNStar{}$ assets on one console and $\qNStarTwo{}$ on two. The trailing step is not cosmetic. The cooldown counter is decremented on each of $\tau$ steps and the event fires on the step after it reaches zero, so the cycle is $P + \tau + 1$; dropping it understates the threshold by about two and a half percent. Measured directly in the collapsed regime the cycle is \qCycleMeasured{} steps against the predicted \qCyclePredicted{}. Note what is absent from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}: the link enters only through $d$, and jamming \emph{raises} the threshold, because a dropped request is one the operator never has to work.

\subsection{Communications and EW ladder}
\label{subsec:comms}
A single \texttt{comms\_level} parameter indexes a C0--C5 degradation ladder, from clear (C0) to severe (C5), that raises tasking latency and message-drop probability and thins the shared picture by dropping relayed contacts (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The six rungs raise one-way tasking latency from 0 to 16 time steps and message-drop probability from 0 to 0.55, spanning a benign link to a heavily contested one; the rung values are set parameters, not measured link data. Because the ladder raises both together, it is one path through a two-dimensional space, and Section~\ref{subsec:queue} shows that latency and loss do not act alike along it: latency only ever hurts direct control, while loss can help it. The ladder also has fewer distinguishable rungs than levels for direct control, since C4 and C5 both sit above $\ell^{\star}$ and are therefore the same rung. The same latency $\ell$ and drop probability $p_{\text{drop}}$ gate both the direct-control round trip (Section~\ref{subsec:command}) and the air-relay path in sensing, so a single dial degrades both tasking and the shared picture. Because supervisory autonomy does not depend on the link while direct control does, the ladder is the axis along which the two modalities separate.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Every scenario carries a correct answer for the trial (which threats exist, which route survives, what outcome a design should produce), and every agent exposes dial-able error and confidence. This is what lets a study score a design against truth rather than against agreement with the model's own agents, and it is a prerequisite for the pattern-detection objectives the broader program pursues. Because each agent's error rate, confidence behavior, and version state are knobs rather than emergent artifacts, a study can hold the ground truth fixed and vary only the teaming design under test.

We study three scenarios drawn from the program's use-case library (Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios}; platform parameters in Table~\ref{tab:platforms}). All three share a 6~km $\times$ 3~km area in which a blue force spawns on the west edge and must move a surviving majority to an objective on the east edge, held by static anti-tank (AT) teams. UC-3 (route choice) sweeps a ground formation's \texttt{route\_bias} from a fast corridor to a covered defilade route. The centerpiece crosses control modality (direct, supervisory) with span of control (one operator to $\{2,3,4,5,6,8\}$ assets) and the C0--C5 communications ladder, in a fair fight where red strength scales with blue. UC-5 (sensor swarm) sweeps recon swarm size ($0$ to $\ucFiveSwarmMax{}$ aircraft) against the C0--C5 ladder, with the swarm cueing near-blind ground shooters against a defense in depth.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first set out the model's verification and validation, then report the three studies: route choice, the span-by-communications centerpiece, and the sensor swarm under EW.

\subsection{Verification and validation}
\label{subsec:validation}

\sandtable{} is low-fidelity, so it earns trust the way a combat-distillation model does, through a small battery of verification and validation checks rather than a single appeal to realism \citep{sargent2013verification}. Four apply here. \emph{Verification:} the evaluation is a pure function of its seed, so a run reproduces bit for bit and paired designs can share random numbers, the property that keeps the response surfaces smooth enough to optimize. \emph{Extreme-condition and degenerate-limit tests:} every added mechanic reduces to a known limit when switched off, and we check each numerically in Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}; suppression strength zero recovers the baseline exactly, an unbounded defender magazine converges onto the fixed-Pk curve, and the baseline is the degenerate kill web with no suppression, unlimited munitions, and perfect knowledge. \emph{Face validity:} the mission KPIs move in the direction and with the monotonicity a subject-matter expert expects as each design knob turns, the structure our sensitivity criterion demands and the studies below display. \emph{Analytic cross-check:} the attrition kernel matches a recognized closed-form combat law, developed next.

For that cross-check, \sandtable{} resolves fire as aimed fire, so when fire is distributed the kill rate on a side is proportional to the number of opposing shooters, which is the hypothesis of Lanchester's square law \citep{lanchester1916aircraft}; area or unaimed fire would instead follow a linear law \citep{deitchman1962guerrilla}, and the operations-research treatment of both laws with the search and detection that gate them is standard \citep{washburn2009combat}. Our per-shot kill probability and rate of fire are the primitives that map onto the Lanchester attrition-rate coefficients \citep{bonder1967attrition}, so the check is quantitative. For initial blue and red strengths $B_0 \ge R_0$ of equal per-unit effectiveness, the stronger (blue) side annihilates the weaker and its survivors are $\sqrt{B_0^2 - R_0^2}$. We isolate the kernel (no movement, no cover, mutual detection, forces intermixed so fire distributes) and compare the Monte-Carlo mean survivors to that closed form (Table~\ref{tab:lanchester}). Agreement is within \lanchMaxErr\%, biased slightly low. The bias is expected and conservative: simultaneous discrete-time resolution lets a unit about to die still return fire (an effect that vanishes as the per-step kill probability shrinks), and nearest-target assignment produces occasional overkill. Lanchester's laws are not a historical curiosity: they remain the standard closed form for aggregate attrition in current operations research, from irregular-warfare models \citep{kress2020lanchester} to heterogeneous-force extensions fitted to present-day equipment mixes \citep{hausken2025modeling}, and they have been checked directly against historical battle data \citep{lucas2004fitting}. Reproducing the square law to within \lanchMaxErr\% is the analytic leg of the checks above, not the whole of them.

\subsection{The moving optimum: span of control under EW}
\label{subsec:centerpiece}

The centerpiece study crosses control modality with span of control and the communications ladder. Figure~\ref{fig:cp_curves} shows the mechanism directly. Supervisory autonomy is nearly communications-invariant: at a span of one operator to two assets its success stays between \cpSupLoSpanMin\% and \cpSupLoSpanMax\% across the entire ladder (a spread of \cpSupLoSpanSpread{} percentage points). Direct control degrades along two axes at once. It falls with communications, from \cpDirClearLoSpan\% at C0 to \cpDirSevereLoSpan\% at C5 for the one-to-two span, and it falls with span, because the single operator saturates: at one-to-eight, direct control is already floored at \cpDirClearHiSpan\% even in clear communications, against \cpSupClearHiSpan\% for supervisory.

Figure~\ref{fig:cp_phase} collapses the full design space into a single decision aid. Each cell is the supervisory-minus-direct success difference; blue cells (bottom-left, low span and clear communications) are where a human in the loop wins, red cells everywhere else are where on-platform autonomy wins, the black contour is the measured crossover, and the green dashed contour is the queue's own unfitted prediction of it (below). The optimum \emph{moves}. At the smallest span the crossover sits near C$\cpStarLoSpan{}$: direct control leads at C0 and is otherwise within Monte-Carlo noise of parity across the near-clear cells, so its exact location is approximate. As span grows the crossover slides left until, at one-to-eight, supervisory autonomy dominates at every communications level (crossover C$\cpStarHiSpan{}$), by \cpDeltaHiSpanClear{}~percentage points even in the clear. Across the whole grid the supervisory advantage reaches \cpDeltaMax{}~percentage points, at span one-to-six under the heaviest degradation. The single most decision-relevant reading is the boundary itself: the more assets one operator must supervise, the less communications degradation it takes before control should be handed to the machines. Table~\ref{tab:centerpiece} gives the full grid.

\paragraph{The moving optimum is the collapse threshold moving.} The phase diagram and the queue mechanism of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} were produced by separate studies, and the second predicts the first. Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and the serviceable-latency ceiling $\ell^{\star}$ are given the ladder's latency and drop rate and the three queue parameters, and nothing else: no success rate, no fitted coefficient, no view of this grid. Their predicted operator goodput correlates with the measured supervisory-minus-direct advantage at $r = \cpQueueR{}$ across all \cpQueueCells{} cells, and its half-goodput contour is drawn on Figure~\ref{fig:cp_phase} beside the measured crossover. The discriminant matters as much as the correlation. The same prediction tracks the direct arm at $r = \cpQueueRDirect{}$ and the supervisory arm at only $r = \cpQueueRSup{}$, so what it has found is the operator's queue and not the scenario's difficulty, which both arms share. Two mechanisms carry the whole boundary. Direct control loses at high span because the queue collapses once $n$ passes $n^{\star}(d)$, and it loses at C4 and C5 at every span because one-way latency there is 8 and 16 steps against a ceiling of \qLatCut{}, so no answer can return inside the requester's patience whatever the span. The prediction is conservative: it keeps direct control viable about one span higher than the measurement does across C0 to C2, which is the queueing approximation of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} overstating the server's tolerance, and it agrees on the sign of the advantage in \cpQueueSign{}\% of cells. The practical reading is that a program does not need this simulator to locate its own crossover. It needs its operators' service rate, patience and consultation cadence, and its radio's round-trip delivery.

\subsection{Inside the optimum: an operator queue that pays for work nobody waits for}
\label{subsec:queue}

The moving optimum of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} is usually read as attention dilution: one operator divided among $n$ assets gives each of them less. That reading is incomplete. Holding the attention term fixed and changing only what the operator does with requests its clients have already abandoned moves mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qAdmitClear\% at the centerpiece's own operating point. The dominant term is not how thin the operator's attention is spread. It is that a \texttt{blind} operator spends a service slot on every request it receives, including the ones whose answer will arrive after the requester has given up and gone autonomous. Work that reaches nobody still occupies the server, which lengthens the queue, which makes the next answer later still. Past a threshold the queue sustains its own overload. Table~\ref{tab:queue_gate} lists the contrasts that establish this, each a seed-matched paired comparison at the full tier of 32 blocks by 64 replications.

\paragraph{The operator in this model is an oracle, and that is the point.} It never errs, never slows, and never tires; the ten-thousandth answer is exactly as good as the first, and the only thing that degrades under load is when the answer arrives. That is not a claim about people. It is what makes the mechanism argument work in one direction only: since the model has no attention term to spend, every pathology in this section is produced by the queue alone, so queueing is \emph{sufficient} for the span behavior usually attributed to operator overload. It says nothing about whether attention also matters, and the workload literature that measures a performance ceiling under high utilization is measuring something this model does not contain \citep{cummings2008predicting,schneider2011workload}.

The direction of the omission is worth stating because it is not neutral, and it favors the levers rather than the argument. An operator whose answers degrade as it saturates is an operator producing rework, and rework is offered load, which is the same quantity Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} thresholds. A quality droop should therefore move every threshold in this section to the \emph{left} and make admission control worth more, not less. We test that rather than assert it. Taking the quality of a serviced decision down toward the autonomous baseline in proportion to the console's running utilization, across \drLevels{} settings from the shipped model to an operator whose answers decay all the way to the autonomous baseline at full utilization, \drVerdict{} of the \drChecked{} orderings this paper ships reverses sign. What the droop does instead is worth reporting, because the three families move differently and the differences are the mechanism. The mission-metric gains SHRINK: admission control over a blind console falls from \drAdmitSuccZero{} to \drAdmitSuccFull{}, a loss of \drAdmitSuccShrink\% of its value, and refunding and the no-retry ordering lose \drRefundSuccShrink\% and \drNoRetryShrink\% the same way. The queue-metric gain does not: admission control's goodput advantage runs \drAdmitGoodZero{} at zero droop and \drAdmitGoodFull{} at full. And the supervisory-over-direct ordering GROWS, from \drSupOverDirZero{} to \drSupOverDirFull{}. A degrading operator costs the policies that work by serving more, leaves the congestion effect they act on untouched, and strengthens the case for taking the operator out of the loop. Every conclusion here is therefore conservative with respect to the oracle assumption rather than dependent on it. That check is a sensitivity analysis of an assumption, not a model of an operator, and it is reported as one.

\paragraph{Two thresholds, predicted then measured.} Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} puts the collapse span at $\qNStar{}$ assets on one console and $\qNStarTwo{}$ on two, and $\ell^{\star} = \qLatCut{}$ steps puts a ceiling on serviceable latency. Figure~\ref{fig:queue}(a) and Table~\ref{tab:staffing} are the staffing curve those numbers predict. On one console the operator's goodput, the fraction of its service time that reaches a requester still waiting, is \qGoodOneSix\% at six assets and \qGoodOneEight\% at eight, so the transition brackets $\qNStar{}$. On two consoles it is \qGoodTwoTwelve\% at twelve and \qGoodTwoSixteen\% at sixteen, bracketing $\qNStarTwo{}$. On three consoles, whose predicted threshold is $\qNStarThree{}$, the queue is still healthy at sixteen (\qGoodThreeSixteen\%). Adding a console does not raise goodput a little at every span; it moves a cliff. Staffing and admission control are the two levers the call-center literature puts on the same congestion \citep{ibrahim2018announcements}, and they act differently here: a second console doubles the span the queue can carry, while admission control on a single console converts the cliff into a slope, holding \qGoodOneEightAdmit\% goodput at eight assets where the blind operator has fallen to \qGoodOneEight\%. The latency ceiling shows the same discipline. Because C4 and C5 both sit above $\ell^{\star}$ at the default patience, they are one rung, not two: goodput is \qPatShortCfour\% at both. Raising patience to 30 steps lifts $\ell^{\star}$ past C4's latency and C4 separates (\qPatMidCfour\%) while C5 stays dead (\qPatMidCfive\%); at patience 45 both resolve (\qPatLongCfive\% at C5). The cut-off is a property of the requester's patience, not of the link.

\paragraph{Jamming can help, and admission control reverses that.} The counter-intuitive consequence of unrefunded abandonment is that dropping messages relieves the server. A request the link never delivers is a request the operator never works, so $d = (1-p_{\text{drop}})^2$ appears in the denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and jamming raises the collapse span. \sandtable{} shows exactly that: at eight assets under \texttt{blind}, moving from a clear link to C2 raises mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qBlindJammed\% (Table~\ref{tab:queue_gate}). Under teleoperation physics, where every asset consults the operator on every decision, the same move takes success from \qTeleBlindClear\% to \qTeleBlindJam\%. Turn on admission control and the sign flips: \texttt{admit} scores \qAdmitClear\% clear against \qAdmitJammed\% at C2, a small but gated advantage for the better link. This matters for how the ladder itself is read. C0 through C5 raise latency and loss together, so a curve drawn against it confounds two mechanisms that do not act alike. Separating them, in Figure~\ref{fig:queue}(b), shows latency monotone harmful, from \qDecoupClean\% goodput at zero latency and zero loss down to \qDecoupLatSix\% at six steps and \qDecoupLatEight\% at eight, where nothing is serviceable at any loss rate. Loss is hump-shaped: goodput rises from \qDecoupClean\% to a peak of \qDecoupPeak\% at a drop probability of \qDecoupPeakDrop{}, then falls to \qDecoupHiLoss\% at 0.55, because past the peak the link is destroying requests faster than the relief is worth. An EW dial that moves both at once cannot show this.

\paragraph{Which half of admission control does the work.} \texttt{admit} does two things at once: it frees the service slot, and it tells the requester. \texttt{refund} does only the first. Separating them at full tier says the slot is almost the whole story. Freeing it alone is worth \qRefundClear\% against \qBlindClear\% at the published operating point, which is \qRefundShare\% of what full admission control delivers, and \qTeleRefundShare\% of it under teleoperation. The notification is a second and much smaller lever, and its value is not constant: on top of a freed slot it adds \qNotifyPub{}~percentage points at the published request rate, which the gate rejects ($t = \qNotifyPubT{}$, 21 of 32 blocks), against \qNotifyTele{} under teleoperation, which it accepts ($t = \qNotifyTeleT{}$, 25 of 32). The asymmetry has a mechanism, and the operator's own goodput shows it. Freeing the slot helps the \emph{operator}, and the operator is the scarce resource in both settings. Telling the requester helps the \emph{requester}, by returning it to autonomy after one round trip instead of after its full patience. But a requester returned early also asks again sooner, so \texttt{admit} offers the operator more load than \texttt{refund} does, and its goodput is accordingly \emph{lower} at every span: \qGoodAdmitEight\% against \qGoodRefundEight\% at eight assets, and \qGoodAdmitTwelve\% against \qGoodRefundTwelve\% at twelve. That ordering is a ratio effect and should not be read as an efficiency loss. Goodput is serviced requests over requests made, and counting completions instead of fractions reverses it: neither policy ever claims a console for work it cannot finish, and \texttt{admit}'s operator completes \qServedAdmit{} useful decisions per step against \texttt{refund}'s \qServedRefund{} at the same span. Notification raises both the numerator and, by more, the denominator. What it buys is requester tempo, so its value should be the price of waiting, and that is directly testable: \texttt{q\_stall} is the control quality of an asset awaiting a decision, and raising it to \texttt{q\_fallback} makes waiting free. Sweeping it does not abolish the increment, it halves it. Under teleoperation the notification is worth \qNotifyTeleLow{}~points at \texttt{q\_stall} $=$ \qStallLow{} and \qNotifyTeleFree{} at \qStallFree{}, gated throughout ($t = \qNotifyTeleFreeT{}$ at the top of the sweep); under the published physics it is \qNotifyPubFree{} even at the cheapest wait and the gate rejects it there too ($t = \qNotifyPubFreeT{}$). So waiting is expensive in more than one way and \texttt{q\_stall} prices only part of it: it prices what a waiting asset can \emph{do}, not the fact that a teleoperated asset awaiting a decision does not \emph{move}. Crossing the two factors separates them without any arithmetic between response surfaces. Holding the behavior layer fixed and varying only whether a waiting asset stops (\texttt{teleop\_stop}) and only what it is worth while it waits (\texttt{q\_stall}) gives four cells, and the gate accepts the contrast in exactly the two where the asset is stopped: \qFacStopPub{}~points at \texttt{q\_stall} $=$ \qStallPub{} ($t = \qFacStopPubT{}$) and \qFacStopFree{} at \qStallFree{} ($t = \qFacStopFreeT{}$), against \qFacGoPub{} ($t = \qFacGoPubT{}$) and \qFacGoFree{} ($t = \qFacGoFreeT{}$) when it keeps driving. Splitting the effect further into main effects and an interaction is not supported at this tier: the point estimates order as the cells imply, but each term individually falls below the minimum practical effect the contract will report, so we give the cells and not the decomposition. The conditional they establish is the usable one. Notification earns its place where an asset that is waiting is an asset that is stopped, which is a property of the tasking loop rather than of the radio. The design consequence is that the load-bearing requirement is a console that does not spend service time on requests that will expire, which needs request timestamps and a discard rule rather than a better radio. Notifying the requester is worth building too, but it should be justified by the tempo of the tasking loop, not by the headline number. There is also a structural reason the notification is the small half, and it is not about tempo at all. A refusal is a congestion signal in the sense of Floyd and Fall \citep{floyd1999congestion}, and in this model nothing consumes it: a refused asset returns to autonomy sooner and then consults again on exactly the same cadence, so the signal changes one wait and never once changes the offered load. In a congestion-controlled network the signal changes the \emph{sender's rate}, and that is the whole of why it works there \citep{jacobson1988congestion}. What the measurement above bounds, then, is the worth of a signal that no policy on the receiving end responds to, which is what a console that merely displays ``operator unavailable'' would deliver. Whether a requester that also asked less often would reorder the two halves is a question about tasking doctrine rather than about the console, and we leave it open.

\paragraph{Fan-out cannot warn of the collapse, whichever way the wait is booked.} The standard supervisory-control statistic for operator capacity is fan-out, $1 + NT/IT$, the ratio of neglect time to interaction time \citep{olsen2004fanout,cummings2008predicting}. Measuring it inside \sandtable{} while the queue collapses gives a negative result, and the negative is sharper than a bare failure to track capacity. Fan-out is load-dependent rather than a platform constant: it falls from \qFanTwo{} at two assets to \qFanTwelve{} at twelve. Across the interval where delivery actually fails it is nearly flat, \qFanEight{} at eight assets against \qFanTen{} at ten, while vehicles delivered over the same interval fall from \qArrivedEight{} to \qArrivedTwelve{} at twelve.

That floor has a closed form, and it explains why. Counting the whole held period as interaction, measured neglect is pinned at $\tau + 1$ across the entire span sweep while every bit of the load dependence sits in the interaction term, which is not interaction at all but the request sojourn $W(n)$, saturating at $P$. So the statistic is $1 + (\tau+1)/W(n)$, and past the cliff, where $W \to P$, it converges to
\[
1 + \frac{\tau + 1}{P} = \qFanFloor{},
\]
a ratio of two \emph{client} timing constants. The service rate and the console count have vanished. It is not that fan-out fails to see this cliff; at the cliff the quantity has stopped being about the operator at all.

Booking the wait differently does not rescue it, and we report the alternatives because a referee can compute them from the same run. Excluding the wait entirely, the classic reading, gives \qFanClassic{} at the collapsed operating point, wrong in the other direction. Counting the wait as neglect instead gives \qFanUtil{}, which does land near $n^{\star} = \qNStar{}$, but only at saturation: it reads the capacity correctly at the moment capacity has already run out, and below the cliff it reads low. No booking of the duty cycle predicts the cliff in advance. Fan-out measures how long an asset can be neglected before it needs help; it does not measure whether help arrives, which is exactly the failure unrefunded abandonment produces. An operator-capacity number computed from neglect and interaction times should not be used to size a span without a queueing check alongside it, and the closed form of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} is the only forward-looking instrument here.

\paragraph{The queue reaches into terrain and route choice.} Operator demand is not exogenous. When the request rate is tied to trafficability, so that an asset in difficult ground asks for help more often (\texttt{terrain\_demand}), the collapse threshold moves left: at six assets, goodput falls from \qTerrainFlatSix\% on easy ground to \qTerrainHardSix\% under the strongest coupling, which is the behavior the flat-ground model shows only at eight. The route-choice study of Section~\ref{subsec:uc3} changes character as a result. There, more cover was monotonically better, and the best route bias was full cover. Under direct control with terrain-correlated demand the covered lane is both slower and harder ground, so it costs attention the operator does not have, and success peaks at a bias of \qRouteDirectArgmax{} (\qRouteDirectBest\%) before falling to \qRouteDirectFull\% at full cover. The interior optimum needs both halves of the coupling: on flat ground the same direct arm still prefers full cover (\qRouteFlatFull\%), and on the same difficult terrain so do supervisory control (\qRouteSupFull\%) and admission control (\qRouteAdmFull\%). Route planning under direct control is therefore an operator-load decision as much as a survivability one, and the interior optimum is an artifact of the human in the loop rather than of the ground.

\paragraph{What the deadline does, and does not, do.} One correction belongs in the record because a cheap robustness check overturned a stronger reading. At the centerpiece's \qDeadline{}~s mission window, \texttt{blind} direct control delivers \qArrivedSix{} vehicles at six assets, \qArrivedEight{} at eight, and \qArrivedTwelve{} at twelve, which reads as a capacity cliff at the predicted span. Re-running the same configurations at a \qLongWindow{}~s window removes the cliff: the same twelve-asset case then delivers \qArrivedTwelveLong{} vehicles and ten delivers \qArrivedTenLong{}, and delivery becomes monotone increasing in span again. What the queue actually imposes is a tempo penalty, and the deadline is what converts tempo into failure. The \texttt{admit} arm is identical at all three windows tested, which is the cleaner statement of the result: admission control removes the dependence on how long the mission is allowed to run. The claim the model supports is about tempo under a deadline, not about a throughput ceiling, and the window has to be named whenever the delivered-vehicle numbers are quoted.

\subsection{What the span result is about: the sign reverses when the enemy scales too}
\label{subsec:attrib}

Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} sweeps span against a fixed defending force, which is how span-of-control studies are usually run and which confounds two things. Adding assets to one operator loads the console, and it also improves the force ratio. A result read as teaming may be a result about arithmetic. The test is to sweep the same span with the defenders scaled so the ratio never moves.

The sign reverses. From \atSpanLo{} to \atSpanHi{} assets at a fixed defending force, mission success rises \atFixDir{} ($t = \atFixDirT$). Over the identical span with the ratio held, it falls \atHeldDir{} ($t = \atHeldDirT$), from \atDirLo{} to \atDirHi{}. The difference in differences is \atDid{} at $t = \atDidT$ with \atDidBlocks{} of \atDidN{} seed-matched blocks agreeing. A reversal of this size is not a confound to be controlled away and reported in a limitations paragraph. It is the identification: whatever the free force ratio contributes, the held-ratio arm has none of it, and what remains is a team that gets worse as it grows.

\paragraph{Three channels, and two of them can be bounded.} The reversal admits three readings, and they carry different consequences for a program office. The console may be congesting, which is the mechanism of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} and is a teaming result. Holding the ratio scales the defending force, which deepens the defending column on one lane rather than widening the engagement, and that is a scenario fact. Or \texttt{success} may be moving without anything underneath it moving, because it is the single bit $\text{arrived} \geq \tfrac{1}{2}n$ and a binomial gets sharper as $n$ grows, so a step in the reported metric can be a step across the cut rather than a step in the fraction.

Supervisory control has no console at all, so its held-ratio step bounds the second and third channels together: whatever it still loses cannot be the queue. It loses \atHeldSup{} ($t = \atHeldSupT$) against direct control's \atHeldDir{}. The console therefore owns about \atConsoleShare\% of the collapse and everything else about \atScenarioShare\%. Keeping the console but refunding the slot of a requester who has already abandoned recovers \atRefundShare\% of the direct step, which places admission policy inside the console's share rather than beside it.

The third channel is ruled out directly rather than bounded. The same runs report \texttt{arrived\_frac}, the fraction of the assault force that reaches the objective, which is the continuous quantity \texttt{success} thresholds. On the direct held-ratio arm it falls monotonically at every rung, \atArrHeldDir{} in total ($t = \atArrHeldDirT$, \atArrBlocks{} of \atArrBlocksN{} blocks), and the difference in differences on it is \atArrDid{} at $t = \atArrDidT$. Nothing was sitting still while the threshold moved. The attribution repeats on the mediator at \atArrConsoleShare/\atArrScenarioShare{} against the \atConsoleShare/\atScenarioShare{} above. The agreement should be read for what it is: \texttt{success} is a deterministic function of \texttt{arrived\_frac} on the same missions, so the two are not independent measurements, and what the second establishes is that the threshold amplifies a real move rather than manufacturing one. Refunding is the one place they disagree, recovering \atRefundShare\% of the direct step on \texttt{success} and \atArrRefundShare\% on the mediator, which is the signature of a policy that acts on the tail: it moves vehicles onto the objective more readily than it moves whole missions across the half-force line.

\paragraph{An independent model found this interaction and could not explain it.} The reversal is not an artifact of our scenario, and the strongest evidence for that is a study that reported it before us without a mechanism. \citet{giachetti2012agent} build an agent-based model of an Army human-robot team searching an urban area, unrelated to this one in scenario, implementation, and metric, and cross team size with how centralized the team's decision authority is. They find a significant interaction ($F(1,12) = 9.18$, $p = 0.010$): under high centralization small teams outperform large ones, while under low centralization the difference disappears. They call the result counterintuitive, since more robots search more sectors, and they resolve it from their simulation logs, reporting that large centralized teams spend more time waiting and that the subteams are waiting on a commander who has become a bottleneck. Their reported operator capacity under those conditions is a single robot, which they note matches an experimental finding that a second ground vehicle is at best marginally useful.

What agrees is the sign, and it is worth being precise about how little else does. Their commander allocates search sectors a few times per subteam per mission; our console answers per-vehicle control requests on a \qDecisionInterval{}-step cadence. Those are different queues with different arrival processes, and a two-level contrast of centralized against decentralized cannot separate the two ways a shared decision-maker produces waiting: simple serialization, which is linear in team size at any utilization, from congestion, which is nonlinear and has a threshold. Their design was not built to, and they do not claim it. So we take this as an independent observation of the interaction's sign in an Army team model that shares none of our choices, and take no quantitative weight from it. What the closed forms add is the part a two-level design cannot supply: which of the two mechanisms is operating, and where it begins, as a quantity computable before the experiment rather than recovered afterward from output logs.

\paragraph{A human experiment recorded the interior optimum and read it as a question about subtasks.} The closest external test of the span claim is not a simulation. \citet{lewis2010choosing} had operators run a multirobot foraging task at 4, 8 and 12 robots, either performing the whole task or one of its two subtasks with the other automated. Full-task yield was \lwFullFour{} victims at four robots, \lwFullEight{} at eight, and \lwFullTwelve{} at twelve: an interior optimum, with an $N \times$ task interaction at $F(\lwInterDf) = \lwInterF$, $p = \lwInterP$. The pairwise comparisons are what make it a threshold rather than a trend. Four against eight is $p \lwFourEightP$ and eight against twelve is $p = \lwEightTwelveP$, but four against twelve is $p = \lwFourTwelveP$ and does not reach significance. Twelve robots performed like four.

We registered that shape as a prediction before obtaining their Results section, because it is where the two accounts of span separate. An operator whose finite attention is divided among competing robots should lose per-robot quality as soon as the robots compete, giving a monotone decline from four. A server whose clients abandon and re-ask should give away nothing until it saturates, and then give away a great deal. What their data shows is a return, not a decline.

Their own coverage numbers make the mechanism harder to attribute to the operator's attention than the framing suggests. Area explored in the full-task condition rose from \lwAreaFullFour{} to \lwAreaFullTwelve{} square metres between four robots and twelve, a factor of \lwAreaRatio{}, while victims found over the same range moved \lwYieldDelta{}. The fleet more than doubled the ground it covered and the mission output did not move. Whatever the operator lost at twelve robots, and they did lose something, since exploration-only operators covered \lwAreaExplTwelve{} against the full task's \lwAreaFullTwelve{} at $p = \lwAreaTwelveP{}$, it was not enough to stop the robots covering twice the ground. None of that extra ground converted. The loss is in the conversion, and conversion happens one interaction at a time at a shared server.

The condition that removes one class of console interaction removes the collapse with it. Operators doing perceptual search alone, with navigation automated, found \lwPercFour{}, \lwPercEight{} and \lwPercTwelve{} victims: monotone in team size, no interior optimum, $F(\lwPercDf) = \lwPercF$ against the full task. That is the same intervention as this paper's supervisory arm, run on human operators, and it produces the same qualitative result. We take the shape and not a capacity number, since the study reports no interaction or neglect time and no $n^\star$ is computable for their setup. What it establishes is that the interior optimum is not an artifact of simulating an operator, because it appears when the operator is a person.

\paragraph{Why reported spans disagree.} Single-operator capacities in the literature are not close to each other. Fan-out arithmetic at \sandtable{}'s own interaction time puts the ceiling near \provMagicFanout{} platforms (Section~\ref{subsec:validation}); workload modelling of multi-aircraft control puts it near three, and says so explicitly of \emph{benign} operations \citep{schneider2011workload}; and wait time alone has been measured to consume up to two thirds of an operator's capacity \citep{cummings2008predicting}. Some of that spread is task and interface. But a capacity measured with no opposing force cannot have a threat-scaling rule, because there is no threat to scale, so the question this section asks does not arise for most of the studies that report a number. It arises for every contested one, and the measurement above says the answer's sign depends on it. A span-of-control figure quoted without the rule that governed the opposition is not a quantity another study can be compared against.

The practical reading is narrower than the headline and more useful. Growing a team helps when the opposition does not grow with it, and that part is arithmetic that needs no simulator. Hold the opposition proportional and growth hurts, three quarters of it at the console. Span of control is not a property of the operator's attention alone; it is a property of the queue the operator's clients form, and it is the part of the problem a program can actually staff, schedule, or automate away.

\paragraph{Why the backoff has to be random, not merely long.} The floor of Section~\ref{subsec:band} sets how long a rebuffed vehicle should wait. It says nothing about whether every vehicle should wait the \emph{same} long time, and the two questions have different answers. Holding the mean hold fixed and widening only its jitter, so that the arms differ in nothing but the spread of their return times, is worth \jiGeGoodWideFive{} of operator goodput at five assets ($t = \jiGeGoodWideFiveT{}$, \jiGeGoodWideFiveBlocks{} of \jiBlocks{} blocks) and \jiGeGoodWideSix{} at six. Same cadence, same offered rate, different phase.

We had expected that to be a fact about the link. Vehicles knocked offline by a common fade come back when it lifts, so a fixed hold should march them into the console together at the recovery edge, and a bursty channel should therefore be where jitter pays. The prediction was that jitter would lower the burstiness of the delivered request stream, measured as its Fano factor. We first measured it in windows the size of the channel's own dwell, found no pair with a gated reduction, the largest being \jiGeFanoWideSix{} at $t = \jiGeFanoWideSixT{}$, and reported that as a refutation. It is not one. The window had been chosen by an argument about where the channel's structure lives rather than by measuring where the statistic works, and those give opposite answers. Benched against an injected cohort of known size, the estimator recovers \fbBurstWDwell{} of it at the dwell window at $t$ near \fbBurstWDwellT{}, against \fbBurstWOne{} at $t$ above \fbMemWOneT{} at a window one service time wide. A null read through a fifth of the available power is not evidence of absence. We therefore re-ran the same arms with the window taken from the bench's own power curve rather than from the channel, and registered the prediction again before looking at any number the re-run produced. It is confirmed in \frGeRed{} of \frGePairs{} mean-matched pairs on the bursty link, by \frGeMin{} to \frGeMax{} against fixed-hold Fano factors of up to \frGeBase{}, which is \frGeMinPct{}\% to \frGeMaxPct{}\% of the burstiness the fixed hold produces, at $|t|$ from \frGeMinT{} to \frGeMaxT{} with \frGeBlocks{} of \frGeBlocksN{} blocks agreeing in every pair. The multiplicity statistic, which unlike a variance can separate many small clusters from few large ones, agrees in \frExcRed{} of \frExcPairs{}. The published null was the window and nothing else.

The control we had included to be discarded is the one that carries the result, though not in the way we first read it. On a memoryless link there is no dwell to synchronize to, so jitter should have bought nothing. It buys \jiBernGoodSix{} at six assets ($t = \jiBernGoodSixT{}$, \jiBernGoodSixBlocks{} of \jiBlocks{}), as much as on the bursty link or more, and the Fano reduction appears there too, in \frMemRed{} of \frMemPairs{} pairs, smaller in absolute and relative terms than on the bursty link (\frMemMin{} to \frMemMax{}, \frMemMinPct{}\% to \frMemMaxPct{}\%). What the control establishes is therefore not that the channel is uninvolved. It is that a retry cohort forms, and costs the console real throughput, in a link with no structure at all for it to form around. A common outage puts several vehicles into the queue together; a deterministic hold is what keeps them together on the next attempt, and the one after, and that second part needs no channel. The bursty link has more clustering to dissolve because its gate adds its own on top of the retry's, which is why the same intervention removes more of it there. Four statistics now say the same thing from different directions: the raw collision multiplicity, the multiplicity net of the arrival rate that would produce it under Poisson, the Fano factor at the mechanism's own timescale, and the residual that survives jitter at matched delivered rate. All four make the bursty link the more clustered one and jitter the larger correction on it.

Separating the two components costs no new computation, because the two links are sections of one battery run and block $i$ carries the same seed in each, so the cross-channel difference in differences is itself paired. Measured where no dwell exists to confound it, self-collision is worth \dcGoodSelfFive{} of operator goodput at five assets and \dcGoodSelfSix{} at six ($t = \dcGoodSelfFiveT{}$ and $\dcGoodSelfSixT{}$, \dcGoodSelfSixBlocks{} of \dcBlocks{} blocks in both). What the dwell contributes on top is \dcGoodDwellFive{} at five and \dcGoodDwellSix{} at six: neither reaches the minimum practical effect, and the two disagree in sign. The whole of the benefit that survives the gate is vehicles desynchronizing from each other, and the recommendation is correspondingly indifferent to what the link is doing.

That collapses two recommendations into one mechanism. Contending senders that retransmit on a fixed schedule collide, back off identically, and collide again, which is why Ethernet randomizes its backoff rather than merely lengthening it \citep{metcalfe1976ethernet}. Section~\ref{subsec:band} borrowed that literature for the length of the hold. It turns out to govern the spread as well, and for its original reason rather than a new one. The operational statement is a single sentence: a vehicle that has gone unanswered should wait at least \qBandFloor{} steps, and should not wait exactly \qBandFloor{} steps.

\paragraph{A congestion signal is worth nothing until something consumes it.} The notification analysis above has a gap that the networking literature makes obvious once the analogy is drawn. A refusal \emph{is} a congestion signal in the sense of \citet{floyd1999congestion}, and nothing in the model consumes it. A refused vehicle returns to autonomy sooner and then comes back on exactly the same standing cadence, so the signal changes one wait and never once changes the offered load. In TCP the signal changes the sender's rate \citep{jacobson1988congestion}. That is the disanalogy, and it is a missing mechanism rather than a finding.

Adding the missing half changes the answer. Let a refused vehicle halve its own request rate and add back an eighth on each answered request, floored at a quarter of the standing cadence, which is additive-increase multiplicative-decrease on the vehicle rather than on the console. At twelve assets on a clear link the operator's goodput runs \aiGoodClearBlindTwelve{} under \texttt{blind}, \aiGoodClearRefundTwelve{} under \texttt{refund}, \aiGoodClearAdmitTwelve{} under \texttt{admit}, and \aiGoodClearAimdTwelve{} when the vehicle responds. On that metric notification alone appears to cost \aiGoodClearTellTwelve{} there ($t = \aiGoodClearTellTwelveT{}$), and adding a response rule appears to be worth \aiGoodClearGainTwelve{} over \texttt{admit} ($t = \aiGoodClearGainTwelveT{}$, \aiGoodClearGainTwelveBlocks{} of \aiBlocks{} blocks). The gain is \aiGoodClearGainEight{} at eight assets and \aiGoodClearGainTen{} at ten, and on mission success it reaches \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten ($t = \aiSuccClearGainTenT{}$).

That reading is contaminated, and the direction of the contamination is knowable in advance. \texttt{operator\_goodput} is serviced over requested, and every policy here changes the requesting: a refusal returns a vehicle to autonomy sooner so it re-consults sooner, and a rate rule suppresses re-consultation by construction. Read on \texttt{served\_per\_step}, which has no denominator to move, the rule serves \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} at twelve assets on a clear link ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveT{}$) and \rtServedAimdOverAdmitJamTwelve{} under jamming ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitJamTwelveT{}$). The console does \rtAimdWorkVerdict{} work, not more. What collapses is the asking: requests fall \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} ($t = \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveT{}$, \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveBlocks{} of \rtBlocks{} blocks).

The finding survives the correction and is better stated after it. Mission success under the rule still gates positive, \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten assets on a clear link and \aiSuccJamGainEight{} at eight under jamming. So the console is asked forty percent less, does a few percent less work, and wins more missions. Stating that as a throughput gain both misdescribes the mechanism and undersells it, because a lever that improves outcomes while REDUCING operator load is a different kind of recommendation from one that improves outcomes by extracting more work from the same operator. The same correction rehabilitates plain \texttt{admit}, whose goodput appears to collapse against \texttt{refund} at wide spans: it in fact serves \rtServedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} at twelve assets, ungated, while being asked \rtAskedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} more often. The console was never overloaded by it. The metric was.

The two console-side levers together are worth stating on the two metrics no policy can inflate, and they agree. Against a console running no policy at all, admission control plus the response rule raises mission success by \aiStackClearEight{} percentage points at eight assets on a clear link ($t = \aiStackClearEightT{}$, \aiStackClearEightBlocks{} of \aiBlocks{} blocks), \aiStackClearTen{} at ten and \aiStackClearTwelve{} at twelve, and \aiStackJamEight{}, \aiStackJamTen{} and \aiStackJamTwelve{} at the same spans under jamming. Over the identical runs it cuts the fraction of the blue force lost by \rtLossStackOverBlindClearEight{} points at eight assets ($t = \rtLossStackOverBlindClearEightT{}$), \rtLossStackOverBlindClearTen{} at ten and \rtLossStackOverBlindClearTwelve{} at twelve. The two are separate measurements rather than one restated: \texttt{success} thresholds the assault, \texttt{blue\_loss\_frac} counts the force that does not come back, and both are fractions of what was fielded rather than of what was requested, so neither can be moved by a policy that changes the asking. They peak at the same span, gate at the same spans, and are null at the same one. Two changes to software, neither adding an operator, a console, or a radio.

Below the collapse span it costs nothing, which is the property that makes it a recommendation rather than a tuning parameter. At four assets the difference from plain \texttt{admit} gates on nothing: \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearFour{} on the rate served ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearFourT{}$), \rtLossAimdOverAdmitClearFour{} points of blue force lost ($t = \rtLossAimdOverAdmitClearFourT{}$), and \aiSuccClearGainFour{} on mission success ($t = \aiSuccClearGainFourT{}$). A vehicle that is never refused never halves anything, so a fleet operating inside its console's capacity pays nothing for carrying the rule, and that now rests on three metrics rather than on the one a policy can move. Like the backoff floor of Section~\ref{subsec:band}, and unlike a staffing decision, it needs no estimate of the span, the delivery rate, or the service rate, because the console's own refusals carry that information already. What it does need is the console to emit them, so this lever sits on top of admission control rather than beside it.

We had predicted something weaker and got it wrong in an instructive direction: that notification would become the majority share of admission control's gain once consumed. It does not, and the share statistic was the wrong instrument, because past the collapse span the unconsumed notification is negative and a ratio whose numerator changes sign cannot measure a share. The finding that replaces it is sharper. The value of telling a requester it has been refused is not small; it is negative, until the requester has a rule for what to do about it.

\subsection{When reliance closes the loop: a span at which the team has two answers}
\label{subsec:band}

Every queue result above assumes a vehicle's request cycle does not depend on whether its last request was answered. That assumption is what gives the model one threshold. It is also not obviously true of an operator-reliant vehicle, which may ask again sooner after being left unanswered than after being served, and the difference matters more than it looks: it makes offered load a function of the state the queue is already in. We add the smallest mechanism that does this. A fraction $\pi$ of abandoned requests return after a short backoff $\rho$ instead of the standing decision interval $\tau$, and everything else is unchanged.

The threshold then splits. Deep in collapse every request abandons, so the mean cycle is $P + 1 + \tau - \pi(\tau - \rho)$ and the collapsed state sustains itself above $n_c$. In the healthy state only a fraction $a_h$ abandons, so only that fraction returns early, and the healthy state survives up to $n_h$:
\begin{equation}
n_c = \frac{c\,\mu\,(P + 1 + \tau - \pi(\tau-\rho))}{d}, \qquad
n_h = \frac{c\,\mu\,(P + 1 + \tau - a_h\,\pi(\tau-\rho))}{d},
\label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
n_h - n_c = \frac{c\,\mu\,\pi\,(\tau-\rho)\,(1 - a_h)}{d}.
\label{eq:bandwidth}
\end{equation}
Between $n_c$ and $n_h$ both states are self-sustaining. The fold itself is old: a retrial queue, in which a blocked customer returns from orbit rather than leaving, is a standard object \citep{falin1990retrial}, and the same feedback makes slotted ALOHA bistable, where a collision provokes the retransmission that provokes the next collision \citep{carleial1975bistable}. What is new here is where it sits. The quantity that turns out to be bistable is span of control, which is a thing a program actually chooses, and the retry that closes the loop is a human-machine interaction rather than a protocol. Three things follow, and none of them is specific to this particular retry rule. The band is empty exactly when the return does not shorten the cycle, so any reliance loop that closes faster after a failure opens one. At the abandonment floor $a_h = 1-d$ the link cancels out of Equation~\ref{eq:bandwidth} entirely, so jamming relocates the band without widening it. And the width inverts into a design rule: holding a failed requester off for at least $\tau - 1/(c\mu\pi)$ steps, which is \qBandFloor{} at this operating point, leaves a band narrower than one vehicle, which is a band no staffing decision can land inside. The floor is a number, and a console cannot use a number: it does not know the span it will be flown at, still less the delivery rate. The rule that reaches the floor without one is older than the analysis. Doubling the hold after each unanswered request and resetting it on service is Ethernet's binary exponential backoff \citep{metcalfe1976ethernet} and the retransmit rule of TCP congestion control \citep{jacobson1988congestion}, and it pays nothing while the system is healthy because a requester that gets answered never accumulates a streak. The published caveat runs the other way and belongs here: unbounded exponential backoff is unstable in infinite-population models \citep{aldous1987instability,hastad1996backoff}. What makes it safe on a console is the bounded vehicle count and the cap at the standing decision interval, not the doubling. That rule is measured and not merely derived. Sweeping the backoff at five vehicles, the initial-condition gap runs \qFloorFour{} at a backoff of four steps and \qFloorTwelve{} at twelve, both of which the gate accepts, and falls to \qFloorSixteen{} at sixteen and \qFloorTwenty{} at twenty, neither of which it does. The predicted floor lies inside that interval.

The floor $a_h = 1-d$ is the wrong number to use on a clear link, where it is zero and Equation~\ref{eq:band} collapses back onto the single threshold. A request can also abandon because the queue was busy, and at C0 that is the only way it can. Reading the healthy abandonment off the retry-free arm instead (\qBandAbandon{} at six vehicles) puts the band at $(\qBandLo{}, \qBandHi{})$, so five vehicles sit inside it and six do not.

Whether an operator's load actually arrives in the clustered way this mechanism needs is an empirical question, and the field record says it does: across the MAGIC 2010 multi-robot reconnaissance competition the task operator averaged 4.25 interactions per minute but went as long as two minutes without interacting at all, intervening in prolonged bursts rather than in small steady adjustments \citep{olson2012magic}. That is the demand shape a fold band lives in, and it is burstier than anything this model generates: the request arrivals here are mildly \emph{under}-dispersed, because the jittered decision interval regularizes them. The band therefore appears under arrivals smoother than the ones a real operator faces, which makes the operating conditions reported below conservative rather than favorable. That is a testable prediction about initial conditions rather than about means, so we test it that way. Staggering the vehicles' first decision events is a healthy start and the synchronized default is not, and the two are compared at each span. Staggering also perturbs the random stream and has an effect of its own, so the quantity that isolates the loop is the difference of differences against the retry-free arm. It is null everywhere except at five vehicles (Figure~\ref{fig:band}a), where mission success moves by \qBandSuccess{} ($t = \qBandSuccessT{}$, 32 of 32 blocks) and operator goodput by \qBandGood{} ($t = \qBandGoodT{}$, 32 of 32). One span below it gives \qBandBelow{} and one span above it \qBandAbove{}. Filling the sweep in at full resolution leaves that reading intact: seven vehicles, a span the first sweep skipped and which had only been interpolated between six and eight, measures \qBandSeven{} on success and \qBandSevenGood{} on goodput, and nine measures \qBandNine{}. At five vehicles the model has two answers, and which one it gives depends on how the vehicles happened to start.

The operational content is in what closes the band. Freeing the slot does: the same contrast at five vehicles falls from \qBandBlind{} under \texttt{blind} to \qBandRefund{} under \texttt{refund} and \qBandAdmit{} under \texttt{admit}, a factor of forty-four and twenty. A console that discards work it cannot finish in time removes the bistability, not merely the average loss, which is a stronger reason to build admission control than the mean effect of Section~\ref{subsec:queue}. That \texttt{admit} closes the band less completely than \texttt{refund} is consistent rather than anomalous: notification returns a requester to service sooner, which raises the request rate, which is what the retry loop does. Admission control's second half is itself a small reliance loop.

Three limitations belong with this. The upper edge is not a constant: $a_h$ is itself a function of span, so Equation~\ref{eq:band} with a frozen $a_h$ brackets the edge rather than locating it, and the honest reading of $\qBandHi{}$ is an estimate at one operating point. Four vehicles sits inside the predicted band and shows nothing, which is a question about basins rather than about stability: the collapsed state is self-sustaining there, but a synchronized start is not a large enough disturbance to reach it, and we have not mapped the basin boundary. Nor does the band survive load arriving from another direction. Tying the request rate to trafficability shortens the decision interval, which lowers both edges, and the upper edge falls below the operating span first: the same contrast decays monotonically from \qBandGood{} on flat ground to \qBandGoodHard{} under the strongest terrain coupling, which is a threshold moving past a fixed span rather than a band widening around it. And $\pi$ and $\rho$ are declared rather than measured. We could find no published re-request rate for a vehicle whose request went unanswered, which is the number this section would need to become a prediction rather than a demonstration. What the field record does establish is that the retry itself is not an exotic assumption: the Demo~III experimental unmanned vehicles called for help only after several attempts to back up and replan had failed, and terrain was the typical trigger \citep{schlenoff2006rcs}, so retry-before-escalation is already how fielded ground autonomy behaves. Whether the same vehicle also retries \emph{after} escalating, and how soon, is the open measurement. Until it is made, the claim here is conditional: if a crew or a vehicle closes that loop faster than it would otherwise, a span exists at which the team's behavior is not determined by its parameters.

The sensor-to-shooter chain is the whole story: recon is what lets blind shooters engage, and swarm size buys the coverage that cues them.

Jamming erodes that chain. As jamming intensifies it thins the shared picture, and both coverage and success fall sharply at every swarm size, though they stay well above the no-recon baseline. The design implication is that swarm size and communications resilience are complements, not substitutes: buying more aircraft raises the ceiling in clear conditions but does not protect the picture once the link is contested.

\subsection{Kill-web mechanics: suppression, munitions, and contested belief}
\label{subsec:killweb}

A review of the model raised three related points about its engagement kernel. Attrition should emerge from the stream of individual engagements rather than being posited as the primitive; each entity should carry the state that governs a modern kill web, at least suppression, a finite basic load of munitions, and a perception state; and fires should be allocated against what a shooter believes rather than against ground truth, so that jamming, spoofing, and decoys become first-order effects instead of probability-of-kill modifiers. The first point already holds: \sandtable{}'s engagement is event-based, each living shooter draws a per-shot Bernoulli kill against its nearest detected target every tick, so attrition is emergent and the Lanchester match of Section~\ref{subsec:validation} is an aggregate cross-check, not the mechanism. The other two we address by adding the missing state.

We add three mechanics, each opt-in and off by default. Suppression is a decaying state in $[0,1]$ that incoming fire raises on its target and that scales down a suppressed shooter's kill probability. Munitions are a finite basic load carried per shooter, decremented on fire, that gates engagement once a shooter runs dry. Belief replaces the single detected-or-not bit with a persistent \emph{track} per opposing entity, a believed position refreshed on detection and going stale with decaying confidence when detection lapses, plus optional false tracks for decoys and spoofing; fires resolve against the believed position and confidence, not truth. All three are byte-identical when off: each is gated so the disabled path draws the identical random stream, and every number in Sections~\ref{subsec:validation}--\ref{subsec:uc5} is unchanged. The baseline is their degenerate limit, a force with no suppression, unlimited magazines, and perfect knowledge. Figure~\ref{fig:tick} shows the per-step pipeline these mechanics plug into and the entity state they add.

\paragraph{Suppression.} Suppression makes a base of fire worth having. On UC-3, raising the
suppression strength from off to full lowers mean blue losses from \suppBaseLoss{} to \suppMaxLoss{} of eight and lifts mission success from \suppBaseSucc\% to \suppMaxSucc\% (Figure~\ref{fig:killweb}a). The attacking echelon out-guns the six defenders, so putting fire on them degrades their return fire faster than they degrade the assault, and the maneuver force crosses at lower cost. A fixed probability-of-kill table cannot express this, because the benefit is contingent on who is firing on whom rather than a property of the weapon. The off setting recovers the baseline exactly.

\paragraph{Munitions.} A finite basic load turns rate of fire into a sustainment ceiling. Sweeping
the defenders' load on UC-3 traces a monotonic curve (Figure~\ref{fig:killweb}b): with \muniScarceAmmo{} rounds each the six defenders cannot service every vehicle crossing their engagement area, so blue loses \muniScarceLoss{} platforms at \muniScarceSucc\% success, whereas at an effectively unlimited load blue loses \muniPlentyLoss{} at \muniPlentySucc\%, reproducing the fixed-Pk baseline. The fixed-Pk model is the infinite-magazine limit; between the two, the defender's magazine depth sets the attacker's loss rate, which is exactly the quantity a logistics-aware plan trades against.

\paragraph{Contested belief.} With fires resolving against tracks, deception and electronic warfare
act on the belief state directly. On UC-3, mean blue losses over \beliefNRep{} replications are \beliefBaseLoss{} for the baseline seen-bit model and \beliefCleanLoss{} for belief tracks in clear communications, so with fresh tracks the two nearly coincide. Adding decoys drops losses to \beliefDecoyLoss{}, because spoofed tracks pull fire off the real force, and severe jamming drops them to \beliefJamLoss{} by a different route, stale tracks whose believed positions drift from the moving truth. Neither effect is reachable by a modifier table, because each needs a belief that deception can corrupt, which is how engagement-level frameworks such as AFSIM allocate fires against imperfect tracks rather than truth \citep{tryhorn2021afsim}.

\subsection{Emergent maneuver: personality-movement propensities}
\label{subsec:personality}

The route-choice study of Section~\ref{subsec:uc3} steers the assault force along a scripted lane set by one \texttt{route\_bias} knob. That knob names the route; it does not let the route respond to where the threat actually is. The combat-distillation models we build on take the opposite view: in ISAAC, EINSTein, and MANA a unit's next move is a weighted sum of local propensities, and the route is whatever those propensities produce \citep{ilachinski2000isaac,ilachinski2004artificial,lauren2002mana}. We make that inheritance concrete with an opt-in movement mode that replaces the scripted lane for blue ground with such a propensity sum: attraction toward the objective, repulsion from each detected enemy that falls off with distance, a pull along the local cover gradient, and a separation term that keeps the formation from stacking up. A single weight $w_e$ scales the enemy-repulsion term. The mode is off by default, and the disabled path is byte-identical to the scripted planner.

Table~\ref{tab:personality} runs the two movement models on UC-3 over \personalityNRep{} replications. With the enemy term off ($w_e{=}0$) the propensity sum is pure goal-seeking and recovers the scripted fast corridor exactly, \persStraightLoss{} losses at \persStraightSucc\% success: the reduction checks that the mode adds a behavior rather than a bias. Turning the enemy term on bends the route. At $w_e{=}1$ the force bows around the detected defenders and reaches the objective with \persAvoidLoss{} losses at \persAvoidSucc\% success in \persAvoidTime{}~s. The scripted planner has no setting that reaches that point. Its safe extreme, the covered route, also crosses at \persScriptCoverSucc\% but costs \persScriptCoverLoss{} losses and takes \persScriptCoverTime{}~s; the emergent route is as survivable and about \persTimeSavePct\% faster, because it commits to no fixed lane and instead skirts each threat as it comes into view, cutting back toward the objective once past. The route is chosen by the model, not by us.

Higher weight is not uniformly better. At $w_e{=}2$ the same force still crosses unharmed but takes \persOverTime{}~s, an emergent over-caution: the repulsion field now holds vehicles off approaches that were survivable at lower weight. The weight has an interior operating point, the kind of structure the sensitivity loop of Section~\ref{sec:results} is built to expose, reached here from local movement rules rather than a scripted route. This is one demonstration on one scenario, not a claim that emergent movement dominates a planner in general; what it shows is that \sandtable{} carries the combat-distillation movement model as a first-class, byte-identical-off option, and that on UC-3 it already finds a route the single scripted knob cannot.

\subsection{Drone-war mechanisms: EW-immune links and counter-UAS}
\label{subsec:drone}

The Russo-Ukrainian war has pushed small uncrewed aircraft and the electronic-warfare fight over their control links to the center of land combat, but its clearest lesson is that the effect is contingent: the same drone is decisive or inert depending on the countermeasures it meets, so that the game-changing effect ``depends on the game'' \citep{kunertova2023depends}, and air defense keeps pace with the drone in an enduring hider-finder competition rather than being overturned by it \citep{calcara2022drones}. Two of those countermeasures bear directly on the command and sensing axes \sandtable{} already models, so we add each as an opt-in mechanic that is byte-identical when off. The first is the answer to link jamming, of which the war shows two: shifting control onboard to autonomy so the drone completes with the radio link severed, and hardening the link itself, most visibly the fiber-optic tethers that jamming cannot touch \citep{kunertova2023boots,bondar2025autonomy}. The autonomy answer is already the supervisory mode of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}, so here we model the other one: on that same command model we add an EW-immune command link that rides a fixed low latency off the C0--C5 ladder and ignores its drops, a fiber tether that keeps the operator in \emph{direct} control while removing the jamming penalty. The single operator remains a shared server, so span-of-control contention is untouched and only the link penalty is removed.

Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(a) contrasts the two links for direct (human-in-the-loop) control across the ladder, over \droneNRep{} replications. On the jammable link, direct control degrades from \fiberClearJam\% success in clear conditions to \fiberHeavyJam\% under heavy jamming (\fiberHeavyLabel{}), the collapse the centerpiece already documents. The EW-immune link instead holds near \fiberHeavyImm\% across the whole ladder, because jamming no longer touches the control path; its advantage is within Monte-Carlo noise when comms are clear (the fixed tether latency and unpaired seeds leave it indistinguishable from a perfect radio there) and grows to about \fiberHeavyGain{} percentage points at heavy jamming. The tether is the complement of the centerpiece's other jamming remedy: there, handing control to autonomy let the force keep functioning as the link degraded; here, an unjammable link lets it keep the human in \emph{direct} control instead. Both realize the same lesson from the war, that jamming-resistant completion is what restores effectiveness under EW; \citet{bondar2025autonomy} report the autonomy variant raising engagement success from roughly 10--20\% to 70--80\%, which we read as directional support for that lesson rather than a calibration of our direct-control rates.

The war's second lesson cuts the other way. Massed cheap drones are themselves attrited by layered air defense and counter-UAS fires, so swarm size is not a free good. The baseline UC-5 of Section~\ref{subsec:uc5} models no air defense, so recon aircraft cannot be engaged and swarm size carries no survivability cost; the unopposed response only climbs and saturates with swarm size, from \cuasOffSmallSucc\% at a single aircraft to \cuasOffLargeSucc\% at the largest, with no interior penalty for a bigger swarm. We add an opt-in counter-UAS/SHORAD layer in which red air defense engages exposed aircraft with a per-step hazard that rises with the committed swarm size, because a larger, more detectable formation is acquired and serviced faster; the hazard keys on the committed size, so it does not slacken as the swarm thins. The layer is deliberately coarse and should be read as a stand-in, not a modeled weapon system: the red units already in the scenario double as the shooters (on UC-5 those are the anti-tank teams, not a dedicated SHORAD element), an aircraft is exposed simply by coming within reach of any of them, and the per-drone hazard is set by the committed swarm size through a calibrated superlinear signature rather than by a radar, magazine, or defender-count model. Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(b) and Table~\ref{tab:drone} give the result. Under counter-UAS that climbing curve becomes an interior optimum: success peaks at \cuasPeakSize{} aircraft (\cuasOnPeakSucc\%) and falls to \cuasOnLargeSucc\% at the largest swarm, a \cuasOnDrop-point drop, because past the optimum the swarm is attrited faster than it can cue the ground force. Too few aircraft give thin coverage; too many are shot down before they deliver it. At one or two aircraft the opposed and unopposed curves differ only within Monte-Carlo noise, since the hazard is negligible for so small a force and the two runs desynchronize once counter-UAS draws its own random numbers. This is the survivability cost the earlier UC-5 result explicitly lacked, and it turns the sensor-swarm study from an upper bound into a tradeoff.

That optimum has a cost dimension, which is the third mechanic. Recon drones are cheap and attritable, a signature economic fact of the war \citep{kunertova2023boots}, so losing many is tolerable in isolation; what makes an oversized swarm expensive is the mission it fails. We attach a cost-exchange KPI, the stipulated value of red destroyed divided by the blue platform-cost lost (a recon drone costed at 1, an assault vehicle at 20, a destroyed defender valued at 15, illustrative relative units, not measured prices), reported on the same swarm sweep so that a value above one means the exchange favored blue. With the default unit costs this KPI reduces exactly to the loss-exchange ratio, so the weights above are a study-level stipulation, and the series in Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(b) and Table~\ref{tab:drone} is the one under counter-UAS; air defense is a cost the unopposed force never pays, so for oversized swarms the unopposed exchange sits higher, and the interior peak describes the shape of the opposed series rather than any gain from counter-UAS. The cost-exchange peaks at \cuasCostPeakSize{} aircraft (\cuasCostPeak{}) and collapses to \cuasCostLarge{} for the largest swarm: the oversized swarm not only bleeds \cuasLargeAirLoss{} drones but, by failing to cue the assault, spends the expensive ground force for no gain. Cheap mass pays only up to the size the defense can be made to over-commit against; past it, more drones buy a negative return. Both mechanics are opt-in and byte-identical off: the jammable-link rows reproduce the command model of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} and the counter-UAS-off rows reproduce the unopposed swarm of Section~\ref{subsec:uc5}, exactly.

\subsection{Bayesian test and evaluation of a design}
\label{subsec:bayeste}

Each study above returns a mission-success rate per design, but a test-and-evaluation decision needs more than a point estimate: the uncertainty on that rate, the probability a requirement is met, and a rule for when enough has been tested. That is the Bayesian Decision Theory paradigm for test and evaluation, which keeps a posterior over a system's performance and uses a utility to weigh information against the cost of testing \citep{ferry2023edou,ferry2024paradigm}. \sandtable{}'s per-replication binary outcomes are exactly what the paradigm consumes, with the simulator standing in as the likelihood, so we demonstrate it on the centerpiece scenario with no new mechanics (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}, Table~\ref{tab:bayeste}).

\paragraph{Credible intervals over point estimates.} A design's success draws give a conjugate Beta
posterior on its success probability, starting from a neutral Jeffreys $\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ prior. For a direct-control design at span 1:2 over \bteN{} replications (\bteHits{} successes) the posterior 95\% credible interval is [\bteBetaLo{}, \bteBetaHi{}] and the probability the design clears an 80\% requirement is \bteComplyEighty{}, a statement a design gate can act on rather than a $p$-value. The frequentist Wald interval nearly coincides at this sample size, but it misbehaves where decisions are often made, on little data: from the first \bteSmallN{} replications (\bteSmallHits{} successes) its upper limit is \bteWaldHiSmall{}, above one, while the Beta interval stays inside the unit interval at \bteBetaHiSmall{}.

\paragraph{A decision chart and when to stop.} Bayesian Decision Theory turns that posterior into an
accept, reject, or keep-testing rule. We set a compliance gate at 60\% mission success (below the 80\% bar used above only to illustrate the compliance probability) and a utility that scores a confident accept at $+20$, a mistaken accept at $-10$, and a reject at $0$, where ``confident'' means the posterior places at least 90\% of its mass above the gate; each trial then costs a fixed amount. A backward recursion labels every (successes, trials) state (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}a). Run against the sim at a per-trial cost of $0.02$, the rule accepts the compliant design (success rate $0.80$) after \bteStopAccept{} replications, rejects a clearly failing one ($0.05$) after \bteStopReject{}, and spends the most evidence, \bteStopBorder{}, on the design nearest the gate ($0.45$), against the \bteN{} replications a fixed-budget study spends on every design. Raising the per-trial cost from $0.02$ to $0.20$ shrinks the keep-testing region, from \bteContinueCheap{} states to \bteContinueDear{}, so the rule stops sooner, the cost-aware behavior a test planner wants.

\paragraph{Comparing two designs.} The same posterior compares designs. At the high-span crossover
the supervisory control mode beats direct with posterior probability \bteProbSupBetter{}, a success-rate advantage of \bteContrastMean{} (95\% CrI [\bteContrastLo{}, \bteContrastHi{}]). One caveat we report rather than bury: common random numbers, which should sharpen a paired comparison, buy almost nothing here (paired outcome correlation \bteCrnCorr{}), because \sandtable{}'s single per-run random stream aligns two designs' initial conditions but desynchronizes once their trajectories diverge; realizing the variance reduction would need the stream split into per-purpose substreams.

\paragraph{The fast model as a prior for the slow one.} The paradigm's most useful move for a
two-tier program is to treat cheap model-and-simulation output as a discounted prior that sparse, expensive high-fidelity runs then update \citep{ferry2024adaptive}. We weight \sandtable{}'s \bteSimN{}-run estimate for the compliant design at \bteLam{} of its nominal sample and confirm it against an emulated high-fidelity truth, a bias-perturbed \sandtable{} standing in for a real high-fidelity model. The informed test reaches a confident compliance decision in about \bteRunsSaved{} fewer expensive runs than testing from scratch (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}b), because the prior starts the posterior already tight. The discount is the guardrail against a wrong prior: when the emulated truth instead sits below the gate (\bteConflictP{}), overturning the optimistic prior to a confident reject takes about \bteOverrideMain{} high-fidelity runs at \bteLam{} but only about \bteOverrideHalf{} at the lighter discount \bteLamHalf{}, so trusting the cheap model less overturns it sooner. This is model-and-simulation-informed operational testing, and it is the quantitative form of the claim that the fast tier feeds, not replaces, the slow one.

We stipulate the utilities here (mission value against per-run cost) rather than eliciting them from stakeholders, which the source work flags as the hard practical step, and framing \sandtable{} as the system under test is a stand-in for a real one. The discounted-prior demonstration is precisely what keeps that honest: the model informs the prior, it is not the truth.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three of this paper's findings are levers rather than observations, and they share a property worth stating before the rest of the discussion qualifies them: each is a change to software, none adds an operator, a console, or a radio, and all three act on the same mechanism. A console that refuses work it cannot finish in time takes mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qAdmitClear\% at the centerpiece operating point, and the same discard removes the bistable span outright, taking the initial-condition gap from \qBandBlind{} to \qBandRefund{}. A vehicle that halves its own request rate when refused and eases back up when served cuts the console's offered load \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} at twelve assets over admission control alone while raising mission success \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten, and at four assets, where nothing is being refused, moves neither the rate served, the force lost, nor mission success by a gated margin. And where the console cannot be changed at all, doubling the hold after each unanswered request reaches the band-closing floor from the vehicle side.

The second and third share the property that makes them deployable, and it is the same property that makes exponential backoff deployable in a network: neither needs to know the span it will be flown at, the delivery rate, or the service rate, because the console's own refusals and silences carry that information already. A staffing decision needs all three estimated in advance and is wrong whenever the estimate is.

The first of the two has an interaction worth reporting separately, because it runs the opposite way from how a mitigation usually behaves: admission control is worth more, not less, as the link gets worse. Refunding is worth \buGoodBernEight{} of operator goodput at eight assets on a memoryless link and \buGoodGeEight{} on a link that fades in bursts of the same average delivery, a difference of \buDidGoodEight{} ($t = \buDidGoodEightT{}$, \buDidGoodEightBlocks{} of \buDidGoodEightN{} blocks). Both channels deliver at the same rate by construction, so what separates them is the arrival pattern: a fading link hands the console a cluster at each recovery edge, the back of the cluster waits past its requester's patience, and a console that cannot detect abandonment serves it anyway. On mission success the same difference is smaller and gates only at the span where the queue is already collapsing (\buDidSuccEight{}, $t = \buDidSuccEightT{}$ at eight assets), so the claim we make is about the console's throughput, with the mission-level consequence established at one span rather than across the sweep. The location of that span is itself a prediction rather than an embarrassment: a throughput gain converts into a mission gain only where the operating point sits near the threshold, and eight assets is where the bursty channel's shifted $n^{\star}$ falls, while at ten both channels are already past their own. We have two spans in the success sweep and cannot separate that account from a noise floor, so it is stated as the reading we would test rather than as one we have tested.

The route, control, and swarm studies make the same point in different settings: the best design is not fixed but \emph{contingent} on the contested-conditions axis, and \sandtable{} locates where it flips. Route choice prices tempo against survivability so a planner can pick a point on the frontier to match the mission. The centerpiece turns span of control and communications state into a single rule: hand control to autonomy sooner as either span or degradation grows, and the phase diagram says exactly when. The swarm study shows that sensing capacity and link resilience must be bought together, because EW attacks the chain that connects them. None of these is a claim about a single design; each shows how the right choice shifts as conditions change, which is the question a mission engineer actually has to answer. The drone-war mechanics of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} carry the same contingency into the countermeasure environment: the EW-immune link changes the outcome only once the link is contested, and the recon swarm's payoff turns from monotone to interior only once an air defense can engage it, so a drone's value is set by the countermeasures it meets rather than fixed \citep{kunertova2023depends}.

The queue result of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} changes what the centerpiece recommends. Read as attention dilution, the moving optimum says to buy autonomy, which is new capability on the platform. Read as congestion produced by undetected abandonment, it says a cheaper fix comes first: a console that does not spend service time on requests whose answers will arrive after the requester has given up. At the centerpiece operating point that is worth \qAdmitClear\% against \qBlindClear\% mission success, and under teleoperation it is the difference between a system that works and one that does not (\qTeleAdmitClear\% against \qTeleBlindClear\%). The decomposition matters for what to build. Almost all of it, \qRefundShare\% at the published operating point, comes from discarding the expired work rather than from telling the requester about it, and the notification earns its keep only when tasking is fast enough that an asset's wasted patience is a large share of its duty cycle. So the requirement is a discard rule and request timestamps, which is doctrine and software; the notification is a second increment, justified by tempo rather than by the headline. The guidance is therefore ordered: fix the admission policy, then staff the consoles to the collapse span of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}, and only then buy autonomy. What does \emph{not} belong on that list is the order the operator works its queue in. Prioritizing requests by how difficult the ground under the requester is, against a randomized-order control and an inverted-priority control, moves mission success by \qTriageSpread{} percentage points at the span and terrain coupling where the effect would be largest. The reason is structural rather than incidental: reordering a queue whose clients all carry the same patience changes which vehicle is helped, never how many are, and a single formation moving down a single route presents almost no dispersion in need to exploit. Refusal is the lever; ordering is not. The same reading changes what an EW dial means. Because loss relieves an unaware server while latency never does, a single ladder that raises both cannot say which one an adversary is actually buying, and the two need separate treatment in requirements and in test design. Finally, it puts a caution on fan-out. Fan-out is the standard number for sizing an operator's span, and across the interval where this model's delivery fails it barely moves; a capacity number computed from neglect and interaction times says nothing about whether help arrives.

Methodologically, the results argue for the two-tier workflow. \sandtable{} ran 44,100 replications in one to two core-hours and returned smooth, well-ordered response surfaces with tight confidence intervals, precisely the regions and boundaries a high-fidelity ProjectGL study should then confirm. The fast tier is not a replacement for fidelity; it is what makes fidelity affordable, by spending the expensive engine only on the designs the cheap one has flagged.


\sandtable{} is deliberately low-fidelity, and its conclusions inherit that. Movement is 2.5D kinematic, not physics-based; sensing and engagement are effect-based draws, not signal or signature models; terrain is a scalar trafficability-and-cover field, not a rendered environment; and line of sight is a flat-terrain stub. Tactical behavior is simplified too: red forces are static defenders that fire but neither maneuver nor react, so every finding is conditioned on a non-adaptive threat. The baseline scenarios model no air defense against the recon aircraft, so in them swarm size carries no survivability cost and the UC-5 upside is an upper bound; the opt-in counter-UAS layer of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} supplies that cost and the interior swarm optimum it implies, but its air defense is itself static, an addressed limit rather than a fully lifted one. Because that layer keys attrition to swarm size rather than to a finite-magazine defense, the interior optimum it produces should be read as a demonstrated mechanism, not a robust prediction of an optimal swarm size: a saturable defense that a large enough swarm can overwhelm would push the balance back toward mass, and which effect dominates in a given engagement is exactly the kind of question the high-fidelity tier exists to settle.

The bistability of Section~\ref{subsec:band} sharpens the same recommendation and adds a
second, cheaper one. A console that discards work it cannot finish does not merely raise the average;
it removes a span at which the team has two possible outcomes and the parameters do not say which,
which is the difference between a system that performs worse and a system that is not predictable
from its design. Where that console cannot be built, the backoff floor is a software change on the
vehicle rather than a change to the operator's station: hold a requester that went unanswered off
for at least $\tau - 1/(c\mu\pi)$ steps, \qBandFloor{} here, and the band narrows below one vehicle.
On difficult ground, where the model ties call-for-help rate to trafficability, lengthening the backoff past that floor cuts the terrain penalty from \qDefileShort{} to \qDefileLong{}, and it does so for a reason the band explains: a longer backoff shortens the request cycle less, which raises both edges, which puts the healthy state back above the operating span on ground that had removed it. Neither
recommendation asks for more operators. Adding a console scales both edges of the band by $c$, which
relocates the problem at the cost of people; the discipline removes it at the cost of software.

The admission-control recommendation is also narrower than it may read. It says the operator should refuse work it cannot finish in time, not that it should be clever about what it accepts. We tested the obvious next step, prioritizing by which requester needs help most, and found nothing, but the test was run on an operator supervising one formation on one route, where the vehicles stand on nearly the same ground by construction: the terrain difficulty spread across the supervised vehicles is \qTriageSeen{} on average against \qTriageMap{} available across the map, because the terrain's variation is almost entirely lateral and a formation samples one lane of it. Whether ordering pays for an operator whose clients are in genuinely different situations, on different routes or against different threats, is untested here and is a property of the command architecture rather than of the queue.

Two of the queue findings are conditioned on choices worth naming. Abandonment semantics are a modeling choice rather than an observation: \texttt{blind}, \texttt{refund}, and \texttt{admit} bracket what a console might plausibly do, \texttt{blind} is the pessimistic end and the behavior of the model as first published, and which of the three a fielded system resembles is an empirical question this model cannot settle. Second, the delivered-vehicle counts that make the collapse look like a throughput ceiling are specific to the \qDeadline{}~s mission window. At \qLongWindow{}~s the same blind configuration delivers \qArrivedTwelveLong{} vehicles at twelve assets rather than \qArrivedTwelve{}, and delivery is again monotone in span. What the queue imposes is a tempo penalty; the deadline is what converts tempo into failure. Success rates under the published window are joint statements about the queue and the clock, and the window belongs with the number whenever it is quoted.

The model's parameters are set, not measured. The command model is a capacity-limited-server abstraction of operator attention, calibrated to the qualitative findings of the supervisory-control literature rather than to human-subject data; and the lethality probabilities and the C0--C5 latency and drop values are hand-set, so they, not measured link or weapon data, fix where the crossover falls and how fast the sensor-to-shooter chain degrades. The studies also use fixed heuristic behaviors rather than learned or reinforcement-learning policies, a single operator with no handoff or dynamic span reallocation, and three scenarios with small teams; extrapolation to larger forces, multi-operator command, or adaptive policies is untested.

The absolute success rates should therefore be read as comparative, not predictive: the value is in the \emph{ordering} of designs and the \emph{location} of crossovers, which is what a search loop consumes, not in any single percentage. The moving optimum's exact crossover cells near the contour are within Monte-Carlo noise, so what the study establishes is the direction of the boundary, not the precise cell. The Lanchester check validates the aggregate attrition law but not the spatial or command dynamics. The stronger forms of validation this class of model admits are left open: docking the emergent behavior against an independently built model \citep{axtell1996aligning}, pattern-oriented validation that asks the model to reproduce several observed tactical patterns at once \citep{grimm2005pattern}, and empirical comparison against exercise or high-fidelity ProjectGL traces. This is why the intended role of the model is to feed, not replace, high-fidelity validation.

We built \sandtable{}, a fast, reproducible, pure-Python mission-level testbed, as the inner-loop companion to the Army's high-fidelity ProjectGL, and used it to produce three decision-relevant maps for human-machine teaming under contested communications: a moving direct-to-supervisory control optimum, a speed-survivability frontier for route choice, and the EW-induced collapse of a sensor-to-shooter chain. Tracing the first of those maps to its cause gave the sharpest result: the operator is a queue with impatient clients, an operator blind to abandonment sustains its own overload past a collapse span given in closed form, and admission control at the console recovers most of what high span costs direct control. On the same core we added an opt-in kill web, suppression, finite munitions, and a belief state that jamming and decoys corrupt, so attrition emerges from the engagement stream and deception and sustainment are first-order effects, with the fixed-Pk baseline recovered exactly when the mechanics are off. Drawing on the Russo-Ukrainian war, we further added an EW-immune command link that holds direct control under jamming, a counter-UAS layer that turns the sensor swarm's monotone upside into an interior optimum in swarm size, and a cost-exchange metric that prices attritable mass, again recovering the prior model exactly when off. The tool obeys a recognized attrition law, runs a full mission in about a tenth of a second, and plugs directly into a Bayesian-optimization and DOE orchestrator, so the next step is to search these design spaces with optimization rather than grids, and to confirm the flagged designs in ProjectGL.

\subsection{The registered-claim ledger}
\label{subsec:ledger}

Every quantitative claim in Sections~\ref{subsec:queue} through \ref{subsec:attrib} was produced by an unattended search loop under one discipline: a conjecture and a falsifier are written down before the run that tests them, the gate is a paired blockwise contrast at a fixed tier, and a claim that meets its falsifier is retracted rather than salvaged. A reader has no way to tell that loop from a post-hoc one by looking at the results that survived, because both produce surviving results. What distinguishes them is the denominator. Table~\ref{tab:ledger} is the denominator.

Of the \ldClaims{} registered claims, three thresholds held against falsifiers that could have fired and did not. One mechanism was retracted on its own falsifier while the shape it predicted survived, and is reported that way rather than restated to fit. One rule was refuted on both the metric it was derived for and the metric a sponsor reads, and its revival clause was written in advance and then closed. One dominance claim died and ships as the weaker claim it can support. One prediction was refuted while something better survived it, because the statistic chosen to test it could not measure what it was pointed at. One was refuted, and then unrefuted: the burstiness reduction it predicted was absent at the window we first read it in and present in every pair at the window a bench says the statistic works in, so what the falsifier caught was our instrument rather than our mechanism, and the entry is in the table with both readings because the first one was published. One conjecture was cut before it ran at all, on the ground that a null on a knob we wrote ourselves, with a range we chose, is not evidence. The last \ldInstr{} rows are instruments rather than claims. All were found by adversarial review, one of them in a guard we had written specifically to prevent the error it failed to catch, and they are in the table because an instrument caught by review is the strongest available evidence that the review has teeth.

The point of the table is not that the loop was mostly right. It is that the loop was sometimes wrong, in public, on record, and before the numbers were seen. Nothing in Sections~\ref{subsec:queue} through \ref{subsec:attrib} would look different if we had quietly dropped the \ldFailed{} entries that failed.

\subsection{Extensions}
\label{subsec:extensions}

The cheapest gains come from hardening the findings the model already produces. A global sensitivity analysis over the operator parameters (service rate, attention capacity, and patience) and the C0--C5 latency and drop values, using Morris screening or Sobol indices, would report each crossover as a band with a variance decomposition that says which inputs actually move it. That converts the moving optimum from a point estimate, whose exact cells sit within Monte-Carlo noise, into a boundary with stated confidence, and it names the few parameters worth measuring. Two calibration steps would supply those measurements: fitting the command model to human-subject supervisory-control data rather than the qualitative anchor used here \citep{cummings2007capacity,chen2011supervisory,hocraffer2017meta}, and Bayesian calibration of the lethality and link parameters against high-fidelity ProjectGL runs \citep{stanko2025projectgl}, so the fast tier inherits the engine's numbers where they set the crossover.

Richer mechanisms would close the fidelity gaps that bound the present conclusions. Replacing the binary range gate and the flat line-of-sight stub with a range-dependent detection probability, true line-of-sight on the trafficability grid, and a false-alarm rate would sharpen the recon-cueing chain that drives the swarm study and the shared picture in the centerpiece. A reactive red force that maneuvers, seeks cover, and actively hunts the reconnaissance would lift every finding off the assumption of a static defender; the counter-UAS layer of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} already gives swarm size a survivability cost, and the natural next step is to let that air defense maneuver and deplete a finite magazine of interceptors rather than fire from fixed sites. A further contested-conditions axis the war has made central is satellite-navigation denial: modeling GNSS jamming and spoofing as a drift-and-bias process on each platform's own position estimate, degrading navigation and geolocation the way the C0--C5 ladder degrades the link, would let position uncertainty act as a first-order effect and compose with the EW-immune link and counter-UAS mechanics already in place. The single operator is now a bank of $c$ interchangeable consoles, which is what makes the staffing curve of Table~\ref{tab:staffing} and the $c$ in Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} meaningful, but the consoles are identical and the assignment is to whichever frees first. What remains is the part that makes a command post a command post: heterogeneous operators, dynamic span reallocation, explicit handoff, and a triage discipline that serves the request most likely to still be waited on rather than the one at the head of the line. Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} sizes the bank; it does not tell it how to work.

The largest extensions point the testbed at the program's learning and analytics objectives. Swapping the scripted heuristics for reinforcement-learning and imitation policies would test the direct-versus-supervisory tradeoff on adaptive teaming behavior rather than fixed rules, and would let the same policies transfer to the Learning Agents runtime inside ProjectGL. A deep-learning surrogate with uncertainty quantification, fitted to the response surfaces and queried by the Bayesian-optimization loop \citep{balandat2020botorch} with an acquisition rule that targets high-variance regions, would cut the number of simulator calls each search needs. Building on the kill-web mechanics of Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}, the entity state a full kill web still lacks, a command and connectivity graph and fuel-and-resupply sustainment, would let command-post disruption and logistics act as first-order effects the way suppression and munitions now do. Broadening the use-case library beyond the UC-7 deception case toward the remaining trust cases in the design, confident-but-wrong recommendations and thermal decoys, would exercise the engineered-ground-truth machinery for the trust-calibration metric and let pattern-mining over the run logs surface the success and failure signatures the program set out to detect.
