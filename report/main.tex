\begin{abstract}
Designing human-machine teams for contested ground operations means choosing how operators control
robotic assets, which routes formations take, and how much reconnaissance to field, under
communications an adversary can degrade. ProjectGL, the Army's Government-furnished high-fidelity
ground simulation framework, is authoritative for that work but runs at or near real time, so broad
design searches are impractical. This paper builds the fast tier of a two-tier workflow in which a
cheap model explores many designs and ProjectGL confirms only the ones that matter. \sandtable{}
keeps the effects that change teaming decisions, which are movement, sensing, engagement, operator
attention and contested communications, and drops the vehicle physics and rendering, so a full
mission finishes in about a tenth of a second.

Its central finding is a sufficiency result. An operator that never errs, never slows and never
tires still produces the span-of-control pathologies usually attributed to human attention, because
they come from the queue its clients form: a server whose clients abandon it, and which never learns
it has been abandoned, spends service time producing answers nobody is waiting for. Attention
arguments alone therefore cannot set span doctrine, and effective span moves under levers that touch
no operator at all. Two closed forms locate the failure, a collapse span and a ceiling on serviceable
latency, and the simulator obeys both with nothing fitted.

Because effective span is a queueing outcome it moves under software, and we price a ladder of
console policies that differ in what the message must carry rather than in mechanism: bound the
backlog with a counter, cap the request's age, or refuse on the requester's own deadline. The
recommendation is conditional on the link and inverts inside the ladder this study sweeps, because a
length bound is a proxy for a request's age and the proxy fails as latency eats the deadline. The
console's total effort is the same under every one of these policies; what they change is its
composition, so working a saturated operator harder is not available as a lever.

Knowledge sets that ceiling, but at fixed knowledge the service ORDER decides who pays, and no
average can see it. Serving requests oldest-first answers the patient half of a mixed fleet
\FrLvFcfsRatio{} times as often as the impatient half and leaves \FrLvFcfsStarv{} of it with no
answer at all; working the identical queue in deadline order takes those to \FrLvEdfRatio{} and
\FrLvEdfStarv{} while raising throughput and lowering abandonment, so it is a strict improvement and
not a fairness-for-efficiency trade. Random order recovers most of the same ground, which makes
oldest-first actively regressive rather than merely suboptimal. The same deadline field read as a
service-level budget instead of an order gates the other way and excludes a quarter of the fleet, so
what a console is told does not fix the sign of what it does.

The account also says what a span-of-control experiment must declare before its numbers can be
compared with anyone else's. Sweeping span with the defending force fixed raises mission success;
sweeping the identical span with the force ratio held reverses the sign. A more portable version of that failure recurs
across four subsystems: a metric measured against a reference the treatment itself moves cannot
audit that treatment. Mean backlog and console utilization rank two scheduling disciplines as
equivalent or backwards where the share of a vehicle's own requests ever answered separates them by
a factor of \KpClearAnsRatio{}; station-keeping error cannot see whether the commanded geometry it
is measured against is survivable; and packet delivery ratio is identical across a change in burst
structure that moves tracking error by two orders of magnitude. In each case a metric referenced to
something the treatment does not set was already available and cheaper than a new sensor. Route choice under fire, reconnaissance cueing under
jamming, and counter-swarm air defense are reported as further studies on the same engine.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The U.S. Army's shift toward human-machine teams, dismounted squads working with robotic combat vehicles, uncrewed aircraft, and sensor nodes, turns tactics into a design problem. A mission engineer must decide how one operator's attention is divided across autonomous assets, whether control should be direct or supervisory, which route a formation takes, and how large a recon swarm should be, all under communications that an adversary actively degrades. Each of these is a knob, and the knobs interact: the best control modality depends on the communications state, and the value of a recon swarm depends on whether its picture survives jamming. Answering such questions demands experiments that sweep the knobs and measure outcomes against an engineered ground truth, not one-off demonstrations.

ProjectGL, the Army's Government-furnished simulation framework, is the authoritative, high-fidelity environment for this work \citep{stanko2025projectgl}. It models vehicle dynamics, sensing, and synthetic terrain in real time. That fidelity is exactly what makes it unsuitable for the \emph{inner loop} of a design study: each mission runs at or near real time, so a modest experiment of a few hundred design points, each averaged over a few hundred stochastic replications, would take weeks of wall-clock time. Bayesian optimization and sensitivity analysis need the objective evaluated thousands of times, quickly and reproducibly. The fidelity that makes ProjectGL the right tool for crew-station development and final validation makes it the wrong tool for searching a design space.

We resolve this with a two-tier workflow. A fast, low-fidelity, mission-level model searches the design space and locates the decision-relevant regions; the high-fidelity engine then validates the handful of designs that matter. This paper builds and exercises the fast tier. \sandtable{} is a pure-Python simulator that keeps the mission-level effects that drive design decisions, movement over trafficable terrain, probabilistic sensing and engagement, a two-tier command model, and a communications/EW degradation ladder, while discarding the physics and rendering that dominate high-fidelity cost. It follows the ``accurate enough, not high fidelity'' principle: the model is kept only as detailed as it must be to produce the right \emph{ordering} of design alternatives.

A model built for a search loop must satisfy two properties that ordinary simulators often do not. It must be a \emph{pure function} of its inputs, with no global or hidden state, so that a run is reproducible from its seed, cacheable, and safe to evaluate in parallel. It must also carry an \emph{engineered ground truth}, a known correct answer for each trial, so that outcomes are scored against truth rather than against agreement with the model's own agents. \sandtable{} is designed around both.

The contributions of this paper are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \sandtable{}, a fast, reproducible, pure-Python mission-level simulator whose
\texttt{run\_mission} contract is a pure function of (scenario, seed, parameters), wired into a Bayesian-optimization and design of experiment (DOE) orchestrator and an HPC batch runner as the fast inner-loop companion to ProjectGL (Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item A control-modality model, direct human-in-the-loop versus supervisory autonomy, coupled to
span of control and a C0--C5 communications/EW ladder, that reproduces a \emph{moving optimum}: the communications level at which autonomy overtakes human control falls as span rises (Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}).
\item The mechanism behind that optimum, stated in closed form and then measured: the operator is
a server with client abandonment, and one that does not detect abandonment sustains its own overload above a collapse span $n^{\star} = c(P+\tau+1)\mu/d$ and a serviceable-latency ceiling $\ell^{\star} = (P - 1/\mu)/2$. Both thresholds are confirmed against staffing and patience sweeps; explicit admission control is worth \qAdmitClear\% against \qBlindClear\% mission success at the centerpiece operating point, and the same mechanism makes message loss beneficial to an undetecting operator, which separates the two axes an EW ladder normally conflates (Section~\ref{subsec:queue}).
\item A requirements ladder for the console, priced rather than argued, whose recommendation is
conditional on the link. Three rungs differ in what the message must carry and not in mechanism:
bounding the backlog needs the patience, the latency and the service rate and no change to the
message; capping the request's age needs a send timestamp; refusing on the requester's own deadline
needs a semantic field. On a clear link the cheapest rung is free, delivering \KpClearSrvBound{}
served per step against admission control's \KpClearSrvAdmit{}, a gap the gate rejects as
immaterial; at C3 it is not, giving up \KpJamBoundVsAdmitSixteen{} at both twelve and sixteen
assets, because a length bound is a proxy for a request's age and the proxy fails as latency eats
the deadline. Underneath all three the console's total effort is invariant: the four ways a claimed
service slot can end sum to within \KpClearEffortSpread{} across every policy while delivered
service spans a factor of \KpClearRatio{}, so these levers change the composition of a fixed
quantity and working a saturated operator harder is not among them (Section~\ref{subsec:queue}).
\item A second console axis that crosses the ladder rather than extending it, and that no aggregate
in this paper could see. Knowledge sets the ceiling; the rule decides who pays. At fixed information,
service ORDER moves who gets served by more than any capability lever priced here: first come, first
served answers the patient half of a mixed fleet \FrLvFcfsRatio{} times as often as the impatient
half and leaves \FrLvFcfsStarv{} of it with no answer at all, and working the identical queue in
deadline order takes those to \FrLvEdfRatio{} and \FrLvEdfStarv{} while also raising mean answer rate
\FrEdfRate{} and throughput \FrEdfSrv{} and lowering abandonment \FrEdfAband{}. It is a strict
improvement rather than a trade, subject to one welded precondition: a console that cannot see the
job in front of it pays \FqEdfBRate{} of answer rate for the same compression. The same deadline
field read as a service-level budget instead of an order gates the other way, excluding
\FqLvQNinetyfiveStarv{} of the fleet at a ninety-fifth percentile against \FqLvEdfBStarv{} for
ordering, so what a console is told does not determine the sign of what it does
(Section~\ref{subsec:queue}, Table~\ref{tab:fair}).
\item An attribution of that span effect, which reverses its own sign under the control every
span-of-control study needs and few report: sweeping span with the defending force scaled so the ratio never moves turns a \atFixDir{} gain into a \atHeldDir{} loss, a difference in differences of \atDid{} at $t = \atDidT{}$ over \atDidN{} seed-matched blocks. Bounding the console away with supervisory control puts about \atConsoleShare\% of the collapse at the operator's queue and \atScenarioShare\% at the scenario, and the same decomposition holds on the continuous arrival fraction that mission success thresholds, which rules out the metric's own sharpening as its cause (Section~\ref{subsec:attrib}).
\item A bistability in span of control, derived and then gated: when a vehicle that went
unanswered asks again sooner than one that was served, offered load depends on the state the queue
is already in, and the collapse threshold splits into a band $(n_c, n_h)$ of width
$c\mu\pi(\tau-\rho)(1-a_h)/d$ inside which a team has two self-sustaining states. At the one span
the band covers, mission success moves by \qBandSuccess{} on the initial condition alone; a console
that discards work it cannot finish shrinks that to \qBandRefund{}, and a backoff floor of
\qBandFloor{} steps closes the band outright (Section~\ref{subsec:band}).
\item A measurement result that applies to any span-of-control study reporting a service ratio, and which cost this paper a headline before it was found. Operator goodput, serviced over requested, has a denominator that every policy worth recommending moves: explicit admission control returns a refused vehicle to autonomy after one round trip instead of after its full patience, so it is asked \rtAskedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} more often at twelve assets while serving \rtServedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{}, and a client-side rate rule suppresses requests by construction and so flatters itself by the same arithmetic in reverse. Read on absolute rates the rate rule serves \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} rather than more, and it is the offered load that falls, by \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{}. A contrast between arms whose request rates differ cannot be reported on a ratio whose denominator is the request count (Section~\ref{subsec:queue}).
\item That failure turned out not to be about ratios, and the general form is the paper's most portable result because it costs nothing to apply. A metric measured against a reference that the treatment itself moves cannot audit that treatment. Five instances are gated here, in four different subsystems, and in each the metric is correct about what it measures. Operator goodput is referenced to the asking, which every policy worth recommending changes. Mean backlog is referenced to the queue, which a scheduling discipline reshapes: it ranks newest-first \emph{worse} than oldest-first, \KpClearBackLifo{} against \KpClearBackFifo{}, while the share of its own requests that a vehicle ever gets answered ranks it \KpClearAnsRatio{} times better. Operator utilization is referenced to the console's clock and puts those same two policies at \KpClearUtilLifo{} against \KpClearUtilFifo{}, which is the same console to four figures. Station-keeping error is referenced to the commanded station, so it cannot see whether the commanded geometry is survivable, and it orders two failure modes backward: \FoDisCmdErr{}~m of error scores \FoDisCmdSuc{} and \FoDisEstErr{}~m scores \FoDisEstSuc{}. Packet delivery ratio is referenced to the packet stream and is identical by construction across a change in burst structure that moves mean track error by a factor of \FoBurstRatio{}. The constructive half is that the metrics which survive are the ones referenced to something the treatment does not set, and in every case here one was already available and cheaper than a new sensor (Sections~\ref{subsec:queue} and \ref{subsec:attrib}).
\item A third console axis, verification, posed as a detection problem rather than as a dial, with
the finding that the axis a program office would tune is not the one that decides the answer.
Discriminability is a capability and the check rate is a policy, and the model separates them:
moving the criterion alone moves the fraction of answers checked by \TdFracSpread{} and the
recovered discriminability by \TdCritSpread{}. On that instrument, an operator who never verifies is
WORSE than no operator at low span (\ToNeverSuccLow{}, \ToNeverSuccLowV{}), and the arm with an
operator loses more of the force than the arm without one in \ToLossUpCells{} of twenty cells. What
verification costs is the asset's time and not the console's, so its affordability is a requirement
on what a vehicle may do while it waits rather than on the console: removing the waiting penalty
takes an always-verifying operator from \ToAlwaysSuccLow{} to \ToFreeAlwaysSuccLow{} against no
operator at all. We registered that the best rate is interior and rises with the automation's error
rate, and retract it on its own falsifier at \DrInterior{} of \DrCells{} cells. What survives is a
rule with a mechanism: verification pays when a wrong answer COMES BACK, and not when it is
harmful, so it gains where the requester keeps its problem and loses at every error rate measured in
the channel that leaves a vehicle below its own autonomy (Section~\ref{subsec:trust}).

\item Three decision-relevant findings for Army human-machine teaming, the moving control optimum,
the speed-survivability frontier of route choice (Section~\ref{subsec:uc3}), and the EW-induced collapse of a sensor-to-shooter chain (Section~\ref{subsec:uc5}), each generated from \nRep{}-replication Monte-Carlo studies with confidence intervals.
\item An analytic validation: \sandtable{}'s emergent attrition matches Lanchester's square law to
within \lanchMaxErr\%, with the residual explained by known discretization effects (Section~\ref{subsec:validation}).
\item A first-class kill-web extension, opt-in and byte-identical when off, in which each entity
carries suppression, a finite basic load of munitions, and a persistent belief/track state, and fires resolve against belief rather than truth; suppression, munitions, and deception each move mission outcomes as first-order effects, with the fixed-Pk baseline recovered as their degenerate limit (Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}).
\item A drone-war extension motivated by the Russo-Ukrainian war, also opt-in and byte-identical when
off: an electronic-warfare-immune command link that holds direct control under jamming, a counter-UAS air-defense layer that gives reconnaissance-swarm size a survivability cost and hence an interior optimum, and a cost-exchange metric for attritable mass (Section~\ref{subsec:drone}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\sandtable{} draws on four bodies of work: mission engineering and the ProjectGL environment it serves, human-machine teaming and supervisory control, agent-based combat simulation, and design of experiments with optimization.

\emph{Mission engineering and ProjectGL.} Mission engineering analyzes how systems combine to accomplish a mission and traces measures of effectiveness back to design choices \citep{raz2024mission,raz2024foundations}. A formal treatment casts the mission-engineering design space in a domain-specific language with set-based and interval-algebra constraints, and evaluates feasible system-of-systems configurations with graph theory and multiattribute utility, demonstrated on a notional sense-decide-effect command-and-control problem \citep{raz2024foundations}; \sandtable{} is an executable counterpart to that analysis, running a sense-decide-act loop forward under contested communications to score a configuration rather than enumerate it. ProjectGL provides the Army's shared, modular basis for such analysis in the ground domain \citep{stanko2025projectgl}. \sandtable{} does not replace it; it occupies the fast end of a fidelity spectrum, producing the response surfaces that tell an engineer which designs are worth running at high fidelity.

\emph{Human-machine teaming and supervisory control.} The distinction between direct and supervisory control dates to early telerobotics \citep{sheridan1978supervisory} and is set within the levels-of-automation framework, which places manual control and full autonomy at the ends of a continuum whose middle our direct-versus-supervisory axis samples \citep{parasuraman2000types}. The limits of a single operator's situation awareness \citep{endsley1995awareness} and attention set how many assets that operator can command: fan-out, the ratio of an asset's neglect time to the operator's interaction time, bounds the span \citep{olsen2004fanout}, and operator-capacity models estimate it from task timing \citep{cummings2007capacity}. Reviews of human-agent teaming and of one operator controlling many robots catalog the resulting interface and workload issues \citep{chen2014teaming,chen2011supervisory,lewis2013human}. The closest analytic precedent for our command model treats the operator as a dynamical queue serving asset requests \citep{savla2012queue}; \sandtable{} adopts that capacity-limited-server abstraction of operator attention (Section~\ref{sec:model}) and maps its behavior across the span-by-communications design space. What that line of work leaves out, and what Section~\ref{subsec:queue} turns on, is that the clients are impatient: an asset that waits past its patience stops waiting. Queues with abandonment are the Erlang-A family developed for call centers, where the quality-and-efficiency-driven regime and the effect of customer impatience on staffing are worked out in detail \citep{garnett2002impatient,gans2003callcenters}. The supervisory-control literature imports the server but not the abandonment, and fan-out \citep{olsen2004fanout}, even with the wait-time correction of \citet{cummings2008predicting}, has no queue in it at all, so it cannot express the failure mode we find. That literature also names the remedy we test: telling a waiting customer what its delay will be is a control on abandonment, and staffing and announcement are complementary levers on the same congestion \citep{ibrahim2018announcements}. On the supervisory side, the closest simulation precedent is a multirobot, multioperator surveillance model with explicit human submodels, used to trade the number and mix of operators and vehicles \citep{humann2023multioperator}; our multi-console staffing curve asks the narrower question of where the queue itself breaks. That the best automation level is itself condition-dependent \citep{kaber2004effects} is the moving optimum our centerpiece locates, and the over-reliance and rejection that flank it are the poles of automation trust \citep{parasuraman1997humans} the program's trust-calibration objective targets. For uncrewed-aircraft swarms specifically, a meta-analysis of swarm-management interfaces documents how operator performance degrades as swarm size and tempo grow \citep{hocraffer2017meta}, which motivates our sensor-swarm study.

\emph{Agent-based combat simulation.} Low-fidelity, effect-based combat models have a long lineage in the combat-distillation tradition: small agent-based models built to expose what drives an outcome rather than to reproduce physics. ISAAC and its successor EINSTein move agents by a weighted sum of attraction and repulsion propensities toward goals, friendly forces, and enemies \citep{ilachinski2000isaac,ilachinski2004artificial}, and MANA added terrain awareness and trigger-driven state changes to that scheme \citep{lauren2002mana}; \sandtable{}'s effect-based agents sit in this line, and we make the inheritance concrete with an opt-in personality-movement mode (Section~\ref{subsec:personality}). Such models are usually built in general agent frameworks, the Java MASON engine behind many defense studies \citep{luke2005mason} or, in Python, Mesa \citep{masad2015mesa}, whose per-agent object pattern we follow in spirit but replace with a bespoke vectorized state for a tight optimization loop. Flocking rules supply formation movement from local interactions \citep{reynolds1987flocks}, and operation-time frameworks schedule swarm tasks against a mission clock \citep{wei2013operation}. A recurring lesson in this literature is to validate an emergent model against a closed-form law; we adopt Lanchester's square law \citep{lanchester1916aircraft}, in the modern treatment of \citet{kress2020lanchester}, for that purpose.

\emph{Design of experiments and optimization.} The studies here are batch DOE sweeps in the data-farming tradition, running a fast model across a designed parameter space to reveal response structure and outliers \citep{horne2004datafarming}; space-filling designs keep that practical as the factor count grows \citep{cioppa2007nolh}, and the statistical basis for designing simulation experiments and fitting metamodels to their output is well established \citep{kleijnen2015doe}. The same plumbing supports Bayesian optimization via Monte-Carlo acquisition functions \citep{balandat2020botorch} when a design space is too large to grid, one instance of the broader simulation-optimization family \citep{amaran2016simulation} in which a cheap surrogate is fit to a simulator and optimized in its place \citep{barton2006metamodel}. The core is built on vectorized array programming \citep{harris2020numpy} and follows standard Monte-Carlo practice, independent seeded replications summarized with confidence intervals \citep{law2015simulation}.

\section{The \sandtable{} Model}
\label{sec:model}

\sandtable{} is a layered mission-level model: a pure-function evaluation contract wraps a vectorized state and a fixed-step loop over movement, sensing, engagement, command, and communications (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The subsections below describe each layer and the two properties that make the model fit for a search loop.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture.pdf}
\caption{\sandtable{} structure. A pure \texttt{run\_mission} builds the world (terrain rasters) and the structure-of-arrays entity state from a scenario, seed, and design parameters, then advances a fixed-step pipeline, command tasking, planning, motion, sensing, engagement, that reads and writes that state each step, and returns scalar mission KPIs. \texttt{evaluate} averages the KPIs over seeded replications, and a design-of-experiments and Bayesian-optimization orchestrator drives batches on the HPC cluster.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The top-level interface is a pure function
\[
\texttt{run\_mission}(\text{scenario}, \text{seed}, \text{params}) \;\rightarrow\; \text{metrics},
\]
with no module-level state, no global random number generator, and no file or wall-clock access in the hot path. A companion \texttt{evaluate} averages the metrics across a requested number of seeded replications. This boundary is what makes a run reproducible from its seed, cacheable, and safe to evaluate concurrently, and it is what lets the optimizer treat the simulator as a black-box objective. All randomness is drawn from a single generator seeded per replication, so identical seeds reproduce identical metrics bit for bit, and matched seed blocks across paired studies implement common random numbers for low-variance comparison.


Entities (ground vehicles, uncrewed aircraft, sensor and command nodes, and defenders) are stored as a structure of arrays: position, heading, health, sensor and weapon parameters, and side live in parallel NumPy arrays rather than in per-agent objects \citep{harris2020numpy}. The mission advances on a fixed time step (1~s here). Each step updates, in order, command tasking, planning, motion, sensing, and engagement (the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). This fixed-step pipeline is a deliberate reduction of the discrete-event executive that full engagement engines such as AFSIM use, whose components self-schedule on their own update intervals \citep{rainey2024afsim}; the discrete-event-system formalism makes that model-simplification relation precise \citep{zeigler2019theory}. This vectorized design runs a full mission of up to 1800 steps in roughly 0.1~s on a single core, about 7 to 11 replications per second depending on scenario, so the 44,100 replications behind this paper are one to two core-hours of compute, trivially parallel across a cluster.

\subsection{Movement, sensing, and engagement}
\emph{Movement.} Ground platforms follow a unicycle kinematic model: each turns its heading toward its current waypoint at a turn-rate limit and advances at
\[
v = v_{\max}\;\tau\;g(x,y)\;(0.35 + 0.65\,q),
\]
where $\tau$ is a scenario tempo factor, $g(x,y)\in[0,1]$ is the terrain trafficability sampled from a precomputed raster (air platforms ignore it), and $q$ is the platform's control quality (Section~\ref{subsec:command}), so a poorly controlled or stalled vehicle also loses tempo. Speed is capped so a platform never oversteps its waypoint in one step. The waypoint comes from a \texttt{route\_bias} parameter that sets a lateral lane between a fast exposed corridor and a slow covered route, holds that lane through the threat, and converges onto the objective only over the final stretch, so a covered route stays in defilade past the defenders.

\emph{Sensing and shared awareness.} Sensing is a cookie-cutter range gate feeding a per-side shared picture. A target is placed on the opposing side's picture if any living observer holds it within sensor range with clear line of sight, and then only with probability $1-c_{\text{conceal}}$ at the target's location, so concealment lowers detectability. The picture is \emph{shared}: a contact any friendly sensor holds is visible to every friendly shooter, which is what lets recon aircraft cue ground vehicles that have not detected the threat themselves. A contact held \emph{only} by an air sensor must be relayed, so under electronic warfare it is dropped from the shared picture with the link's message-drop probability (Section~\ref{subsec:comms}); a contact also held organically by a ground sensor is unaffected. Line of sight is a flat-terrain stub in the present model.

\emph{Engagement.} Engagement is aimed fire. Every living shooter engages its nearest detected enemy within weapon range, and a kill is a Bernoulli draw with probability
\[
p_k = p_k^{\text{base}}\,(1 - c_{\text{cover}})\,q_{\text{shooter}}\,(2 - q_{\text{target}}),
\]
clipped to $[0,1]$, where $c_{\text{cover}}$ is terrain cover at the target: cover reduces incoming lethality, and control quality couples both ways, since a well-controlled shooter fires more effectively and a poorly controlled target exposes itself. Both sides fire against the same pre-step state and damage is applied simultaneously, so there is no turn-order bias; recon aircraft carry no weapon and are not targeted, as the baseline models no short-range air defense (Section~\ref{subsec:drone} adds it as an opt-in layer). This effect-based abstraction is deliberately coarse; Section~\ref{subsec:validation} shows it still obeys the right aggregate law.

\subsection{Command model: direct versus supervisory}
\label{subsec:command}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{command_model.pdf}
\caption{The command model, the mechanism behind the moving optimum. Under \emph{direct} control an asset requests a single shared operator over the comms/EW link and waits: the reply is high quality when it arrives in time, low while the asset waits, and poor when the link drops it. Under \emph{supervisory} control each asset decides locally at a steady autonomous quality, independent of the link. More supervised assets both dilute the operator's per-decision quality and lengthen its queue.}
\label{fig:command}
\end{figure}

The command layer is where the human-machine teaming questions live (Figure~\ref{fig:command}). In \emph{supervisory} mode each asset runs on-platform autonomy at a steady control quality $q_{\text{auto}}$, independent of communications. In \emph{direct} mode an asset requests the operator over the modeled link, paying a round trip of $2\ell$ steps at one-way latency $\ell$ with per-message drop probability $p_{\text{drop}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), and the single operator serves queued requests at a fixed rate $\mu$. A request answered within the asset's patience returns the high operator quality $q_{\text{operator}}$; while it waits the asset holds a low stall quality $q_{\text{stall}}$; and a dropped or timed-out request drops the asset to a poor autonomous fallback $q_{\text{fallback}}$. Crucially, the operator is a single server shared across all supervised assets. Attention dilutes with span: the effective operator quality is
\[
q_{\text{operator}}^{\text{eff}} = q_{\text{auto}} + \big(q_{\text{operator}} - q_{\text{auto}}\big)\, \min\!\Big(1, \tfrac{\kappa}{n}\Big),
\]
where $n$ is the number of supervised assets and $\kappa$ the operator's attention (span) capacity (here $q_{\text{auto}}=0.70$, $q_{\text{operator}}=0.95$, $q_{\text{stall}}=0.30$, $q_{\text{fallback}}=0.50$, $\kappa=4$). Below capacity the operator adds full value; above it, requests queue and the human-in-the-loop advantage erodes. Control quality feeds engagement (a well-controlled shooter is more effective; a poorly controlled or stalled asset exposes itself), so the abstract quality translates into mission outcomes. This model, an operator as a capacity-limited server whose value is gated by both span and link quality, is the mechanism behind the moving optimum of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}, and it is grounded in the operator-capacity and supervisory-control literature \citep{cummings2007capacity,chen2011supervisory}.

\paragraph{Abandonment, and what the operator does about it.} The queue has one further property that turns out to drive the results of Section~\ref{subsec:queue}: an asset that has waited longer than its patience $P$ stops waiting. That makes the operator an $M/D/1$ server with abandonment, the Erlang-A family studied in the call-center literature \citep{garnett2002impatient,gans2003callcenters}, and it raises a design question that the fan-out tradition does not ask, because fan-out has no queue in it \citep{olsen2004fanout,cummings2008predicting}. The question is whether the server discovers the abandonment, and \sandtable{} carries all three answers as the parameter \texttt{queue\_policy}. Under \texttt{blind}, the default and the behavior of the model as first published, the operator works every request it receives, including requests whose answer will arrive after the requester has given up, so the service slot is spent either way. Under \texttt{refund} the operator silently drops work it cannot finish in time. Under \texttt{admit} it also tells the requester, which goes autonomous after one round trip instead of after $P$ steps: explicit admission control. Naming the regime this produces is worth doing precisely, because it is neither new nor a queueing result. A server that spends its capacity producing work discarded before it reaches anyone is \emph{congestion collapse from undelivered packets}, the failure Floyd and Fall describe for a network busy carrying traffic that a downstream link will drop \citep{floyd1999congestion}. The correspondence is exact in mechanism and inexact in what is wasted: there the waste is upstream bandwidth on a path whose far end discards, here it is the server itself and the discard is the requester's own patience running out. Each of the two literatures supplies the half the other lacks. Erlang-A has the abandonment but reports no collapse, because a call-center agent learns of a hang-up at once, so wasted service is a footnote rather than a regime; the networking literature has the collapse but no human, its senders being protocol state machines whose patience is a timeout constant someone chose. A supervisory console inherits both at the same time, which is why the two remedies that work in Section~\ref{subsec:queue} and Section~\ref{subsec:band}, an explicit congestion signal and a retransmission backoff, are the two that literature already fields \citep{jacobson1988congestion}.

A console cannot discard work it cannot finish in time unless it knows when the time runs out, and that is an interface requirement rather than a modeling convenience. The requester's deadline has to travel with the request. That is the ordinary construction in protocols that face the same problem, where a message carries its own expiry and any server along the path may drop it once expired, and it is the assumption under which every \texttt{refund} and \texttt{admit} number in this paper should be read: a console attached to a request format that does not carry a deadline can implement neither, and the levers are worth nothing to it until the format changes. Whether a fielded console has that field is an empirical question, and it is a cheaper one to answer than any of the ones this simulator asks. The $D$ in $M/D/1$ is load-bearing for how large the lever reads. Service here takes exactly $1/\mu$ steps, so a console that decides at arrival is deciding against a perfect forecast of its own queueing delay, and \texttt{refund} and \texttt{admit} are therefore measured at the ceiling: they discard exactly the work that was going to expire and nothing else. A console facing variable service times would sometimes discard work that would have made it and sometimes keep work that did not, so the gains reported here are an upper bound on what the discard rule buys, and the gap between the two is a forecast-error cost this model sets to zero by construction. It is not a gap between deciding early and deciding late: with deterministic service and no cancellation a request's completion time is fixed the moment it is booked, so admitting at arrival and admitting when the console picks the request up are the same inequality evaluated at two different clock times.

Two thresholds follow in closed form, and Section~\ref{subsec:queue} shows the simulator obeying both. A reply fits inside the requester's patience only while $2\ell + 1/\mu \le P$, so above a one-way latency of
\[
\ell^{\star} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(P - 1/\mu\big) = \qLatCut{}\text{ steps}
\]
no request can be answered at all and the drop probability becomes inert. And in the collapsed regime every request abandons, so each asset runs a fixed cycle between requests: $P$ steps waiting the request out, $\tau$ steps of cooldown, and one step on which the next decision event fires. A \texttt{blind} operator still pays $1/\mu$ for every one of them. With $c$ consoles and two-way delivery probability $d = (1-p_{\text{drop}})^2$ the queue therefore sustains its own overload once the span exceeds
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nstar}
n^{\star} = \frac{c\,(P + \tau + 1)\,\mu}{d},
\end{equation}
which at the centerpiece's operating point ($\mu = \qServiceRate{}$, $P = \qPatience{}$, $\tau = \qDecisionInterval{}$) is $\qNStar{}$ assets on one console and $\qNStarTwo{}$ on two. The trailing step is not cosmetic. The cooldown counter is decremented on each of $\tau$ steps and the event fires on the step after it reaches zero, so the cycle is $P + \tau + 1$; dropping it understates the threshold by about two and a half percent. Measured directly in the collapsed regime the cycle is \qCycleMeasured{} steps against the predicted \qCyclePredicted{}. Note what is absent from Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}: the link enters only through $d$, and jamming \emph{raises} the threshold, because a dropped request is one the operator never has to work.

\subsection{Communications and EW ladder}
\label{subsec:comms}
A single \texttt{comms\_level} parameter indexes a C0--C5 degradation ladder, from clear (C0) to severe (C5), that raises tasking latency and message-drop probability and thins the shared picture by dropping relayed contacts (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The six rungs raise one-way tasking latency from 0 to 16 time steps and message-drop probability from 0 to 0.55, spanning a benign link to a heavily contested one; the rung values are set parameters, not measured link data. Because the ladder raises both together, it is one path through a two-dimensional space, and Section~\ref{subsec:queue} shows that latency and loss do not act alike along it: latency only ever hurts direct control, while loss can help it. The ladder also has fewer distinguishable rungs than levels for direct control, since C4 and C5 both sit above $\ell^{\star}$ and are therefore the same rung. The same latency $\ell$ and drop probability $p_{\text{drop}}$ gate both the direct-control round trip (Section~\ref{subsec:command}) and the air-relay path in sensing, so a single dial degrades both tasking and the shared picture. Because supervisory autonomy does not depend on the link while direct control does, the ladder is the axis along which the two modalities separate.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Every scenario carries a correct answer for the trial (which threats exist, which route survives, what outcome a design should produce), and every agent exposes dial-able error and confidence. This is what lets a study score a design against truth rather than against agreement with the model's own agents, and it is a prerequisite for the pattern-detection objectives the broader program pursues. Because each agent's error rate, confidence behavior, and version state are knobs rather than emergent artifacts, a study can hold the ground truth fixed and vary only the teaming design under test.

We study three scenarios drawn from the program's use-case library (Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios}; platform parameters in Table~\ref{tab:platforms}). All three share a 6~km $\times$ 3~km area in which a blue force spawns on the west edge and must move a surviving majority to an objective on the east edge, held by static anti-tank (AT) teams. UC-3 (route choice) sweeps a ground formation's \texttt{route\_bias} from a fast corridor to a covered defilade route. The centerpiece crosses control modality (direct, supervisory) with span of control (one operator to $\{2,3,4,5,6,8\}$ assets) and the C0--C5 communications ladder, in a fair fight where red strength scales with blue. UC-5 (sensor swarm) sweeps recon swarm size ($0$ to $\ucFiveSwarmMax{}$ aircraft) against the C0--C5 ladder, with the swarm cueing near-blind ground shooters against a defense in depth.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first set out the model's verification and validation, then report the three studies: route choice, the span-by-communications centerpiece, and the sensor swarm under EW.

\subsection{Verification and validation}
\label{subsec:validation}

\sandtable{} is low-fidelity, so it earns trust the way a combat-distillation model does, through a small battery of verification and validation checks rather than a single appeal to realism \citep{sargent2013verification}. Four apply here. \emph{Verification:} the evaluation is a pure function of its seed, so a run reproduces bit for bit and paired designs can share random numbers, the property that keeps the response surfaces smooth enough to optimize. \emph{Extreme-condition and degenerate-limit tests:} every added mechanic reduces to a known limit when switched off, and we check each numerically in Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}; suppression strength zero recovers the baseline exactly, an unbounded defender magazine converges onto the fixed-Pk curve, and the baseline is the degenerate kill web with no suppression, unlimited munitions, and perfect knowledge. \emph{Face validity:} the mission KPIs move in the direction and with the monotonicity a subject-matter expert expects as each design knob turns, the structure our sensitivity criterion demands and the studies below display. \emph{Analytic cross-check:} the attrition kernel matches a recognized closed-form combat law, developed next.

For that cross-check, \sandtable{} resolves fire as aimed fire, so when fire is distributed the kill rate on a side is proportional to the number of opposing shooters, which is the hypothesis of Lanchester's square law \citep{lanchester1916aircraft}; area or unaimed fire would instead follow a linear law \citep{deitchman1962guerrilla}, and the operations-research treatment of both laws with the search and detection that gate them is standard \citep{washburn2009combat}. Our per-shot kill probability and rate of fire are the primitives that map onto the Lanchester attrition-rate coefficients \citep{bonder1967attrition}, so the check is quantitative. For initial blue and red strengths $B_0 \ge R_0$ of equal per-unit effectiveness, the stronger (blue) side annihilates the weaker and its survivors are $\sqrt{B_0^2 - R_0^2}$. We isolate the kernel (no movement, no cover, mutual detection, forces intermixed so fire distributes) and compare the Monte-Carlo mean survivors to that closed form (Table~\ref{tab:lanchester}). Agreement is within \lanchMaxErr\%, biased slightly low. The bias is expected and conservative: simultaneous discrete-time resolution lets a unit about to die still return fire (an effect that vanishes as the per-step kill probability shrinks), and nearest-target assignment produces occasional overkill. Lanchester's laws are not a historical curiosity: they remain the standard closed form for aggregate attrition in current operations research, from irregular-warfare models \citep{kress2020lanchester} to heterogeneous-force extensions fitted to present-day equipment mixes \citep{hausken2025modeling}, and they have been checked directly against historical battle data \citep{lucas2004fitting}. Reproducing the square law to within \lanchMaxErr\% is the analytic leg of the checks above, not the whole of them.

\subsection{The moving optimum: span of control under EW}
\label{subsec:centerpiece}

The centerpiece study crosses control modality with span of control and the communications ladder. Figure~\ref{fig:cp_curves} shows the mechanism directly. Supervisory autonomy is nearly communications-invariant: at a span of one operator to two assets its success stays between \cpSupLoSpanMin\% and \cpSupLoSpanMax\% across the entire ladder (a spread of \cpSupLoSpanSpread{} percentage points). Direct control degrades along two axes at once. It falls with communications, from \cpDirClearLoSpan\% at C0 to \cpDirSevereLoSpan\% at C5 for the one-to-two span, and it falls with span, because the single operator saturates: at one-to-eight, direct control is already floored at \cpDirClearHiSpan\% even in clear communications, against \cpSupClearHiSpan\% for supervisory.

Figure~\ref{fig:cp_phase} collapses the full design space into a single decision aid. Each cell is the supervisory-minus-direct success difference; blue cells (bottom-left, low span and clear communications) are where a human in the loop wins, red cells everywhere else are where on-platform autonomy wins, the black contour is the measured crossover, and the green dashed contour is the queue's own unfitted prediction of it (below). The optimum \emph{moves}. At the smallest span the crossover sits near C$\cpStarLoSpan{}$: direct control leads at C0 and is otherwise within Monte-Carlo noise of parity across the near-clear cells, so its exact location is approximate. As span grows the crossover slides left until, at one-to-eight, supervisory autonomy dominates at every communications level (crossover C$\cpStarHiSpan{}$), by \cpDeltaHiSpanClear{}~percentage points even in the clear. Across the whole grid the supervisory advantage reaches \cpDeltaMax{}~percentage points, at span one-to-six under the heaviest degradation. The single most decision-relevant reading is the boundary itself: the more assets one operator must supervise, the less communications degradation it takes before control should be handed to the machines. \paragraph{The moving optimum is the collapse threshold moving.} The phase diagram and the queue mechanism of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} were produced by separate studies, and the second predicts the first. Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and the serviceable-latency ceiling $\ell^{\star}$ are given the ladder's latency and drop rate and the three queue parameters, and nothing else: no success rate, no fitted coefficient, no view of this grid. Their predicted operator goodput correlates with the measured supervisory-minus-direct advantage at $r = \cpQueueR{}$ across all \cpQueueCells{} cells, and its half-goodput contour is drawn on Figure~\ref{fig:cp_phase} beside the measured crossover. The discriminant matters as much as the correlation. The same prediction tracks the direct arm at $r = \cpQueueRDirect{}$ and the supervisory arm at only $r = \cpQueueRSup{}$, so what it has found is the operator's queue and not the scenario's difficulty, which both arms share. Two mechanisms carry the whole boundary. Direct control loses at high span because the queue collapses once $n$ passes $n^{\star}(d)$, and it loses at C4 and C5 at every span because one-way latency there is 8 and 16 steps against a ceiling of \qLatCut{}, so no answer can return inside the requester's patience whatever the span. The prediction is conservative: it keeps direct control viable about one span higher than the measurement does across C0 to C2, which is the queueing approximation of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} overstating the server's tolerance, and it agrees on the sign of the advantage in \cpQueueSign{}\% of cells. The practical reading is that a program does not need this simulator to locate its own crossover. It needs its operators' service rate, patience and consultation cadence, and its radio's round-trip delivery.

\subsection{Inside the optimum: an operator queue that pays for work nobody waits for}
\label{subsec:queue}

The moving optimum of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} is usually read as attention dilution: one operator divided among $n$ assets gives each of them less. That reading is incomplete. Holding the attention term fixed and changing only what the operator does with requests its clients have already abandoned moves mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qAdmitClear\% at the centerpiece's own operating point. The dominant term is not how thin the operator's attention is spread. It is that a \texttt{blind} operator spends a service slot on every request it receives, including the ones whose answer will arrive after the requester has given up and gone autonomous. Work that reaches nobody still occupies the server, which lengthens the queue, which makes the next answer later still. Past a threshold the queue sustains its own overload. Table~\ref{tab:queue_gate} lists the contrasts that establish this, each a seed-matched paired comparison at the full tier of 32 blocks by 64 replications.

\paragraph{The operator in this model is an oracle, and that is the point.} It never errs, never slows, and never tires; the ten-thousandth answer is exactly as good as the first, and the only thing that degrades under load is when the answer arrives. That is not a claim about people. It is what makes the mechanism argument work in one direction only: since the model has no attention term to spend, every pathology in this section is produced by the queue alone, so queueing is \emph{sufficient} for the span behavior usually attributed to operator overload. It says nothing about whether attention also matters, and the workload literature that measures a performance ceiling under high utilization is measuring something this model does not contain \citep{cummings2008predicting,schneider2011workload}.

The direction of the omission is worth stating because it is not neutral, and it favors the levers rather than the argument. An operator whose answers degrade as it saturates is an operator producing rework, and rework is offered load, which is the same quantity Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} thresholds, so a quality droop should move every threshold here to the \emph{left} and make admission control worth more rather than less. We test that rather than assert it. Taking the quality of a serviced decision down toward the autonomous baseline in proportion to the console's running utilization, across \drLevels{} settings up to an operator whose answers decay all the way to that baseline at full utilization, \drVerdict{} of the \drChecked{} orderings this paper ships reverses sign. The three families move differently, and the differences are the mechanism. The mission-metric gains SHRINK: admission control over a blind console falls from \drAdmitSuccZero{} to \drAdmitSuccFull{}, and refunding and the no-retry ordering lose \drRefundSuccShrink\% and \drNoRetryShrink\% of their value the same way. The queue-metric gain does not: admission control's goodput advantage runs \drAdmitGoodZero{} at zero droop and \drAdmitGoodFull{} at full. And the supervisory-over-direct ordering GROWS, from \drSupOverDirZero{} to \drSupOverDirFull{}. A degrading operator costs the policies that work by serving more, leaves the congestion effect they act on untouched, and strengthens the case for taking the operator out of the loop. Every conclusion here is conservative with respect to the oracle assumption rather than dependent on it, and this is a sensitivity analysis of an assumption rather than a model of a person.

\paragraph{The operator is also a metronome, and that assumption is load-bearing in a second way.} The droop check above varies what the operator's answers are worth. It does not vary how long they take, and the console's service time in this model is a constant: Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}, the fan-out floor, the latency ceiling and the capacity rule below were all derived against an exact $1/\mu$, and admission control is a feasibility test that compares a deadline against it. A person is not a metronome. Varying the coefficient of variation of the service time in the auxiliary queue model, holding the mean exactly so that anything that moves is variability rather than speed, the capacity rule's answer computed on the deterministic server stays inside the variable server's plateau in \QcapCvCells{} of \QcapCvCells{} cells up to $cv = \QcapCvMax{}$, which is an exponential operator; the argmax itself moves by up to \QcapCvShiftWorst{} capacities, but the argmax on this response is decided by rounding and the plateau is the readable statistic. The lever survives too, and loses value without approaching a sign change: bounding the backlog is worth \QcapCvBoundMetro{} served per step over the unbounded console at $cv = 0$ and \QcapCvBoundNoisy{} at $cv = \QcapCvMax{}$. Two consequences are worth stating because neither was predicted. First, an erratic operator is a much more expensive operator on a bad link than on a good one: the best achievable service falls \QcapCvLossJam\% across the same range at the C3 deadline and \QcapCvLossLong\% at a long one. Second, the age cap of the previous paragraph degrades far faster than the counter (\QcapCvAgeNoisy{} against \QcapCvBoundNoisy{}), and the reason is structural rather than incidental. Setting a cap at $P - 2\ell - 1/\mu$ embeds a point forecast of service the request has not yet received, and the counter forecasts nothing, so any admission rule that predicts remaining work inherits the variance of that work. That is a second and independent reason the recommendation is the counter.

\paragraph{Two things a program office can buy from an operator, priced against each other.} The
metronome assumption of the previous paragraph hides a second one that is more interesting and was
found by accident. When the service time is variable, a console applying admission control has to
compare the deadline against something, and there are two candidates. It can compare against the
mean, which is all a deadline field in the message could ever give it. Or it can compare against the
service time this particular decision will actually take, which is an operator who can tell a hard
request from an easy one before committing to it. Our implementation did the second while its
comment claimed the first, and the two are separated here because they are requirements of
completely different kinds. They coincide exactly when the service time is constant, which is why
every number published before this paragraph is unaffected by the distinction.

Priced at sixteen assets on a clear link, with the mean held fixed so nothing moves but variability
and discernment, the ordering is not the intuitive one. A metronome that cannot triage delivers
\FsMetro{} served per step. Give it a coefficient of variation of one half and it falls to
\FsNoisy{}, so consistency is worth \FsConsistency{} and the gate accepts it. Give the variable
operator the ability to triage and it rises to \FsSeeing{}, so discernment is worth \FsDiscernment{},
which is \FsRatio{} times what consistency is worth and also gated. Variance is an opportunity to
whoever can see it and a cost to whoever cannot, and the same dispersion that hurts one operator is
what gives the other something to select from. Discernment's value also rises with span, from
\FsDiscernSix{} at six assets to \FsDiscernTwelve{} at twelve, for the same reason: triage needs
something to triage away.

\paragraph{Perfect triage is a ceiling, so we measured the interior, and it is a straight line.} An
operator who sees each decision's exact duration before committing is an oracle, and a number
produced by one bounds what any real capability is worth without estimating it. The quantity a
human-subjects arm can estimate is the probability that an operator recognises a hard request at
all, so the console now carries that probability and treats the request as average otherwise, with
the same feasibility test either way. The curve it traces at sixteen assets is \AcZero{},
\AcQuarter{}, \AcHalf{}, \AcThreeQ{} and \AcFull{} served per step as the probability runs from zero
to one. Every step gates up with all thirty-two blocks agreeing, and the largest departure from a
straight line through the endpoints is \AcLinMax{} against a materiality floor of \AcFloor{}. There
is no threshold below which partial skill is wasted: a quarter of the capability buys
\AcQuarterGain{} served per step and gates up on its own. That is the answer to the question a
program office actually asks, which is not whether a perfect operator would be valuable.

And it costs the console nothing. Occupancy is statistically equivalent across every acuity at every
span, \AcBusyZero{} against \AcBusyFull{} at sixteen assets with $t = \AcBusyT{}$, while delivered
service on the same missions rises \AcGainPct\% at $t = \AcSrvT{}$. Two statistics, 0.01 and 274,
from one set of runs. The console that can triage does not work harder, and the console that cannot
is not idle: what changes is which decisions the same fixed effort is spent on. This is the effort
result of the previous paragraphs carried from a policy axis onto a capability axis, where it is
more surprising, and it is measured in console-time rather than in decisions completed because a
console that selects short decisions completes more of them without doing more.

\paragraph{How to tell triage from tightening, which is the objection this result has to answer.}
Every admission rule refuses work, and refusing work helps a saturated console whatever the rule is,
so a gain in delivered service is not by itself evidence of any capability. The test that separates
them uses the service-time accounting directly: length cannot be selected without seeing length, so
a console has difficulty information if and only if the mean service of the decisions it works falls
below the population mean of \AcPop{} steps. Four consoles that have the lever and not the
information were run against that test: a capacity bound, a rule that refuses a fixed fraction at
random, the honest deadline test against the mean, and a budget set at the 0.9 quantile of the
service distribution. All four work at population, spanning \AcFreeLo{} to \AcFreeHi{} across every
span, a spread of \AcFreeSpread{}. The two consoles that can see work \AcWorkHalf{} and
\AcWorkFore{}, a gap of \AcSelectGap{} steps. The instrument's noise and its signal differ by nearly
two orders of magnitude, and the quantile arm is the one to notice: a budget quantile looks like
cheap triage and is not, because it selects on the requester's slack rather than on the decision's
length.

The same accounting gives the mechanism a sentence it did not have. The honest console works
\AcWorkMean{} steps per decision and serves \AcServeMean{}; the foreseeing console works
\AcServeFore{} and serves \AcServeFore{}. Their served mixes are nearly the same. What differs is
that the honest console pays full service time for the long decisions it then fails to deliver
before the deadline, and the foreseeing one refuses them before the slot is spent. Triage and a
deadline test select almost the same jobs; the difference between them is who pays for the rejects.

The corollary is the one to act on, and its direction is the reverse of what we first drew from it.
Run the same comparison with a backlog bound in place and discernment almost stops paying: it is
worth \FsBoundDiscernment{} rather than \FsDiscernment{}, smaller by a factor of \FsBoundShrink{},
because a console holding $k^{\star}$ requests has very little left to choose between. We read that
as substitution, the cheap structural fix standing in for the expensive human one, and the numbers
underneath it do not say that. A bounded console that can triage delivers \FsBoundSeeing{} served
per step; an unbounded one that can triage delivers \FsSeeing{}. The bound does not reach what
discernment reaches, it forecloses most of it. What it does do is lift the console that cannot
triage, from \FsNoisy{} to \FsBoundNoisy{}. So the counter substitutes for the operator one did not
train and handicaps the one one did, and a program office that buys both gets less than it paid for.
That statement first shipped with a caveat, because the bounded arms carried a deadline test as well
as a counter and therefore priced the counter as an addition to admission control rather than as the
requirements ladder's counter-only rung. The rung has since been measured on its own. At sixteen
assets a counter alone delivers \AcBoundAlone{} served per step, a deadline field alone
\AcDeadlineAlone{}, the two together \AcBoundPlusTriage{} once the console can also triage, and
triage alone \AcTriageAlone{}. So the counter is worth \AcBoundHelps{} to a console that cannot
triage and \AcBoundHurts{} to one that can ($t = \AcBoundHurtsT{}$, agreeing in 32 of 32 blocks).
It does not merely fail to add: it changes sign. The measurement also closes the construction
argument the caveat rested on, since a counter-only console with and without the triage capability
switched on differs by \AcInertGap{} served per step, exactly and in every block, which is what it
means for a deadline test never to be reached.

\paragraph{What an admission rule has to know, priced rung by rung.} The consoles above differ in
whether they can see a decision's difficulty. A programme has to decide what to buy, and the four
things it could buy are separable and separately priced at sixteen assets. A console with no
admission rule at all delivers \BiRungNone{} served per step. Refusing a fixed fraction at random,
at the best fraction in the sweep, delivers \BiRungRandom{} and gates DOWN by
\BiRungRandomGap{}: admission tightness with no information in it is not a weak lever, it is a
negative one at every fraction and every span we ran. Budgeting a quantile of the service
distribution instead of its mean delivers \BiRungQuantile{}, worth \BiRungQuantileGap{} and gated,
and it needs no per-job information at all because it selects on the requester's slack. Seeing this
decision's own length is worth far more, \BiRungSeeing{}. And a console that sees the length and
also demands slack in proportion to it reaches \BiRungPriced{}. The last of those four is the one
that needed a control, because it can be produced either by an estimator that reads high or by a
rule that prices the slot, and the two are not the same recommendation.

The reason tightness alone is worthless is visible at matched refusal rate rather than at matched
parameter. The selective console turns away \BiRefuseSel{} of requests and delivers
\BiMatchedSel{}; the random one turns away \BiRefuseRand{} and delivers \BiMatchedRand{}. They
refuse nearly the same amount of work and differ by which work. The mechanism is length selection
and the instrument reads it: mean service of a SERVED decision falls from \BiSvcOne{} to
\BiSvcBest{} steps as the console tightens. It is not working more, it is working shorter. Two
boundaries make that concrete. At zero service variability the ladder is a step rather than a curve,
\BiFlatOne{} and \BiFlatBest{} and then exactly \BiFlatOff{}, with mean served service at exactly
\BiFlatSvc{} throughout: with every decision the same length there is nothing to select on and the
rule admits all or refuses all. And a noisy estimate cannot select at all: mean served service falls
from \BiSelClean{} to \BiSelCleanTop{} steps under an exact estimate and RISES from
\BiSelNoisy{} to \BiSelNoisyTop{} under a noisy one, which is why the peak value collapses from
\BiPeakClean{} to \BiPeakMid{} to \BiPeakNoisy{} as the estimate's spread grows while the peak's
LOCATION barely moves. For a specification that separates cleanly: the offset is a constant, the
spread is the requirement, and the value of getting either right scales with how variable the work
is, \BiCvLow{}, \BiCvMid{} and \BiCvHigh{} at coefficients of variation of a quarter, a half and
one.

The control that paragraph promised is the one that decides how the rung is written. An honest
console that sees each decision's true length and refuses anything longer than a fixed cap is
TOST-equivalent to the mis-estimating console at every span we ran, at \CapEffOne{},
\CapEffTwo{} and \CapEffThree{} served per step with a weakest one-sided statistic of
\CapTostWeak{}. Two mechanically different rules, one a bias in the estimate and one a truthful
limit on the slot, reach the same throughput, so there is no advantage to being wrong and the rung
is a statement about pricing the slot rather than about the estimator. Where they differ is in
what they do to the work they accept: at matched throughput the capped console serves the LONGER
decisions of the two, \CapSvcHonest{} steps against \CapSvcBias{} (both are means over served
decisions, so neither is referenced to a request count the treatments themselves move). We
recommend the capped form on two grounds the measurement does not decide, and say so. A mandated
bias lives in the estimator, which other consumers share, so a console specified to read
\(2.5\times\) high makes an accurate estimator fail its own admission test; and a cap degrades
more gracefully if the service distribution shifts, because it names a limit on the slot rather
than a multiplier on a forecast. Neither ground is a throughput claim and neither is measured
here.

Whether the offset is a design variable at all is a property of the load, and that question is
answerable where the peak's location is not. On a ladder this flat near its top an argmax is
ill-conditioned, so we report neither it nor the span at which it moves, and read two conditioned
quantities instead. The first is the tolerance band, every offset within one rate floor of the best
at that span, which is what a specification would have to state in any case. At four assets the
band is \CapBandLow{}, all \CapBandLowWidth{} settings we ran: nothing the console does to its
offset changes its throughput, so by this paper's own test the offset is not a design variable
there. The band narrows as load rises and by \CapBandNarrows{} assets it is \CapBandHigh{}, which
excludes unity. The second is a difference-in-differences on the ladder's corners, which tests the
tilt directly rather than inferring it from a peak: the gap between the highest and lowest offsets
is \CapTiltWide{} wider at sixteen assets than at four ($t = \CapTiltWideT{}$, agreeing in
\CapTiltBlocks{} blocks), and against unity rather than against the lowest rung it is
\CapTiltRef{} ($t = \CapTiltRefT{}$). So the ladder genuinely tilts with load, no offset separates
from unity below \CapGateFirst{} assets (\CapFineTwelve{}, reaching \CapFineSixteen{} at sixteen),
and where the optimum sits between those two facts is not resolved here. The cheap side of the
ladder is sharper. An optimistic console, one that under-demands slack, is
free at four assets (\BiOptGapFour{}, equivalent) and ruinous at sixteen (\BiOptGapSixteen{}). A
refusal costs the console nothing when there is something else to serve and costs it a delivery when
there is not, so what the offset is really pricing is the slot, and a slot is worth what the queue
behind it is worth.

Throughput bought this way is not free, and the bill is legible in a unit a sponsor cares about.
At sixteen assets the priced console serves \CapPayServed{} more per step ($t = \CapPayServedT{}$)
and abandons \CapPayAband{} more per step ($t = \CapPayAbandT{}$), which is about
\CapPayRate{} abandoned requests for each extra decision delivered, and \CapPayRateMid{} at
twelve. A more aggressive offset worsens the exchange rather than improving it, to
\CapPayRateBig{} abandonments per extra decision. An over-tight honest console is dominated on
both counts at once: it delivers \CapDomServed{} against a plain console, which the gate calls
equivalent, while abandoning \CapDomAband{} more per step. The favourable cell is the moderate
one. At twelve and sixteen assets the priced console gates UP on throughput while its per-vehicle
answer rate stays \CapPayAnswerVerdict{} (\CapPayAnswer{}), where the aggressive setting loses
\CapPayAnswerBig{} of it. So there is a setting at which the console delivers measurably more
without measurably lowering any vehicle's chance of being answered, and the whole of its cost
appears as abandonment instead of as denial. That exchange rate, not the offset, is the quantity to
specify.

One result here does not survive to the mission, and saying so changes the recommendation rather
than weakening it. None of the throughput gains above reach mission success at this tier: the best
cell is \BiMissBest{} at eight assets and the gate calls it \BiMissBestVerdict{}, with every other
cell equivalent. The losses do reach it. Over-tightening at four assets costs \BiMissHarm{} of
mission success and the gate accepts that one. So the asymmetry, not the throughput number, is what
belongs in guidance: pricing the slot is a console-level gain this study cannot show converting into
mission outcome, and over-tightening a console that is not busy is a mission-level loss it can. A
rule of this kind should be fielded with the span it was sized for attached to it.


\paragraph{Two thresholds, predicted then measured.} Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} puts the collapse span at $\qNStar{}$ assets on one console and $\qNStarTwo{}$ on two, and $\ell^{\star} = \qLatCut{}$ steps puts a ceiling on serviceable latency. Figure~\ref{fig:queue}(a) and Table~\ref{tab:staffing} are the staffing curve those numbers predict. On one console the operator's goodput, the fraction of its service time that reaches a requester still waiting, is \qGoodOneSix\% at six assets and \qGoodOneEight\% at eight, so the transition brackets $\qNStar{}$. On two consoles it is \qGoodTwoTwelve\% at twelve and \qGoodTwoSixteen\% at sixteen, bracketing $\qNStarTwo{}$. On three consoles, whose predicted threshold is $\qNStarThree{}$, the queue is still healthy at sixteen (\qGoodThreeSixteen\%). Those three readings are on the ratio, and the ratio is the metric this paper has had to correct twice, so they were re-run on the delivered rate. The brackets do not move: one console serves \seSrvOneNSix{} per step at six assets and \seSrvOneNEight{} at eight, two serve \seSrvTwoNTwelve{} at twelve and \seSrvTwoNSixteen{} at sixteen, and three are still rising at sixteen (\seSrvThreeNSixteen{}). Three consoles, three brackets, each containing its own prediction, with no parameter fitted to any of them. That the $c$-scaling survives on a metric with no denominator is the part worth having, because it is the one place in this paper where the equation makes a prediction about a knob the equation was not derived from. Adding a console does not raise goodput a little at every span; it moves a cliff. Staffing and admission control are the two levers the call-center literature puts on the same congestion \citep{ibrahim2018announcements}, and they act differently here: a second console doubles the span the queue can carry, while admission control on a single console converts the cliff into a slope, holding \qGoodOneEightAdmit\% goodput at eight assets where the blind operator has fallen to \qGoodOneEight\%. The latency ceiling shows the same discipline. Because C4 and C5 both sit above $\ell^{\star}$ at the default patience, they are one rung, not two: goodput is \qPatShortCfour\% at both. Raising patience to 30 steps lifts $\ell^{\star}$ past C4's latency and C4 separates (\qPatMidCfour\%) while C5 stays dead (\qPatMidCfive\%); at patience 45 both resolve (\qPatLongCfive\% at C5). The cut-off is a property of the requester's patience, not of the link.

\paragraph{Jamming can help, and admission control reverses that.} The counter-intuitive consequence of unrefunded abandonment is that dropping messages relieves the server. A request the link never delivers is a request the operator never works, so $d = (1-p_{\text{drop}})^2$ appears in the denominator of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} and jamming raises the collapse span. \sandtable{} shows exactly that: at eight assets under \texttt{blind}, moving from a clear link to C2 raises mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qBlindJammed\% (Table~\ref{tab:queue_gate}). Under teleoperation physics, where every asset consults the operator on every decision, the same move takes success from \qTeleBlindClear\% to \qTeleBlindJam\%. Turn on admission control and the sign flips: \texttt{admit} scores \qAdmitClear\% clear against \qAdmitJammed\% at C2, a small but gated advantage for the better link. This matters for how the ladder itself is read. C0 through C5 raise latency and loss together, so a curve drawn against it confounds two mechanisms that do not act alike. Separating them, in Figure~\ref{fig:queue}(b), shows latency monotone harmful, from \qDecoupClean\% goodput at zero latency and zero loss down to \qDecoupLatSix\% at six steps and \qDecoupLatEight\% at eight, where nothing is serviceable at any loss rate. Loss is hump-shaped: goodput rises from \qDecoupClean\% to a peak of \qDecoupPeak\% at a drop probability of \qDecoupPeakDrop{}, then falls to \qDecoupHiLoss\% at 0.55, because past the peak the link is destroying requests faster than the relief is worth. An EW dial that moves both at once cannot show this.

\paragraph{Which half of admission control does the work.} \texttt{admit} does two things at once: it frees the service slot, and it tells the requester. \texttt{refund} does only the first. Separating them at full tier says the slot is almost the whole story. Freeing it alone is worth \qRefundClear\% against \qBlindClear\% at the published operating point, which is \qRefundShare\% of what full admission control delivers, and \qTeleRefundShare\% of it under teleoperation. The notification is a second and much smaller lever, and its value is not constant: on top of a freed slot it adds \qNotifyPub{}~percentage points at the published request rate, which the gate rejects ($t = \qNotifyPubT{}$, 21 of 32 blocks), against \qNotifyTele{} under teleoperation, which it accepts ($t = \qNotifyTeleT{}$, 25 of 32). The asymmetry has a mechanism, and the operator's own goodput shows it. Freeing the slot helps the \emph{operator}, and the operator is the scarce resource in both settings. Telling the requester helps the \emph{requester}, by returning it to autonomy after one round trip instead of after its full patience. But a requester returned early also asks again sooner, so \texttt{admit} offers the operator more load than \texttt{refund} does, and its goodput is accordingly \emph{lower} at every span: \qGoodAdmitEight\% against \qGoodRefundEight\% at eight assets, and \qGoodAdmitTwelve\% against \qGoodRefundTwelve\% at twelve. That ordering is a ratio effect and should not be read as an efficiency loss. Goodput is serviced requests over requests made, and counting completions instead of fractions reverses it: neither policy ever claims a console for work it cannot finish, and \texttt{admit}'s operator completes \qServedAdmit{} useful decisions per step against \texttt{refund}'s \qServedRefund{} at the same span. Notification raises both the numerator and, by more, the denominator. What it buys is requester tempo, so its value should be the price of waiting, and that is directly testable: \texttt{q\_stall} is the control quality of an asset awaiting a decision, and raising it to \texttt{q\_fallback} makes waiting free. Sweeping it does not abolish the increment, it halves it. Under teleoperation the notification is worth \qNotifyTeleLow{}~points at \texttt{q\_stall} $=$ \qStallLow{} and \qNotifyTeleFree{} at \qStallFree{}, gated throughout ($t = \qNotifyTeleFreeT{}$ at the top of the sweep); under the published physics it is \qNotifyPubFree{} even at the cheapest wait and the gate rejects it there too ($t = \qNotifyPubFreeT{}$). So waiting is expensive in more than one way and \texttt{q\_stall} prices only part of it: it prices what a waiting asset can \emph{do}, not the fact that a teleoperated asset awaiting a decision does not \emph{move}. Crossing the two factors separates them without any arithmetic between response surfaces. Holding the behavior layer fixed and varying only whether a waiting asset stops (\texttt{teleop\_stop}) and only what it is worth while it waits (\texttt{q\_stall}) gives four cells, and the gate accepts the contrast in exactly the two where the asset is stopped: \qFacStopPub{}~points at \texttt{q\_stall} $=$ \qStallPub{} ($t = \qFacStopPubT{}$) and \qFacStopFree{} at \qStallFree{} ($t = \qFacStopFreeT{}$), against \qFacGoPub{} ($t = \qFacGoPubT{}$) and \qFacGoFree{} ($t = \qFacGoFreeT{}$) when it keeps driving. Splitting the effect further into main effects and an interaction is not supported at this tier: the point estimates order as the cells imply, but each term individually falls below the minimum practical effect the contract will report, so we give the cells and not the decomposition. The conditional they establish is the usable one. Notification earns its place where an asset that is waiting is an asset that is stopped, which is a property of the tasking loop rather than of the radio. The design consequence is that the load-bearing requirement is a console that does not spend service time on requests that will expire, which needs a discard rule rather than a better radio. How little that rule has to know is the subject of the next two paragraphs, and the answer is less than this one assumes. Notifying the requester is worth building too, but it should be justified by the tempo of the tasking loop, not by the headline number. There is also a structural reason the notification is the small half, and it is not about tempo at all. A refusal is a congestion signal in the sense of Floyd and Fall \citep{floyd1999congestion}, and in this model nothing consumes it: a refused asset returns to autonomy sooner and then consults again on exactly the same cadence, so the signal changes one wait and never once changes the offered load. In a congestion-controlled network the signal changes the \emph{sender's rate}, and that is the whole of why it works there \citep{jacobson1988congestion}. What the measurement above bounds, then, is the worth of a signal that no policy on the receiving end responds to, which is what a console that merely displays ``operator unavailable'' would deliver. Whether a requester that also asked less often would reorder the two halves is a question about tasking doctrine rather than about the console, and we leave it open.

\paragraph{The console has bufferbloat, and the right buffer size is already in this paper.} An operator that accepts every request into an unbounded backlog is holding work past the point where anyone still wants it, which is the failure Gettys and Nichols named in routers \citep{gettys2012bufferbloat} and the reason the fix there is to size or age the queue rather than to maximize it \citep{nichols2012codel}. The size falls out of the deadline algebra already used for Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}. A request standing at position $j$ in the waiting line is answered $2\ell + r + j/\mu$ after it was sent, where $r$ is the residual of the request in service, so with $\lambda = (P - 2\ell)\mu$ it makes its deadline with probability $\max(0, \min(1, \lambda - j))$ and the console should hold
\[
k^{\star} = \left\lfloor \lambda + \tfrac{1}{2} \right\rfloor - 1
\]
waiting requests and no more. This is a threshold rule in the sense of \citet{naor1969regulation}, phrased the way \citet{appenzeller2004sizing} phrase buffer sizing, and its drop-oldest victim rule is the packet management of the age-of-information line \citep{kaul2012real,costa2016age}. The composition is ours and none of the parts are. In \sandtable{} itself, at full tier, the argmax of delivered service over a capacity ladder from zero to ten is $k^{\star}$ at every span measured: 2 at \StQcapClearHit{} of \StQcapClearSpans{} spans on a clear link and 1 at \StQcapJamHit{} of \StQcapJamSpans{} at C3, where the extra round trip moves $\lambda$ from 3.00 to 1.80. Counting argmax hits is the weaker statement, though, and on this response it can be the wrong one, because an argmax read off a wide plateau is chosen by rounding rather than by the data. The count that carries information is over the cells where the measurement could have said otherwise. Calling a cell sharp when at most two capacities sit within one material effect of the best, \StQcapClearSharp{} of \StQcapClearSpans{} cells are sharp on a clear link and \StQcapJamSharp{} of \StQcapJamSpans{} at C3, and the rule is the argmax in every one of them. At C3 the discrimination is sharper still: in \StQcapJamSoleWidth{} of those cells the plateau is a single capacity, so $k^{\star} = 1$ is not merely inside the best set but alone in it. The one cell at C3 that is not sharp is the smallest span, where the console is not yet congested and \StQcapJamWidestPlateau{} of the capacities are indistinguishable, which is the expected shape rather than a failure: a rule for sizing a backlog has nothing to say about a console that has no backlog. The uncorrected form of the rule, without the term for where in the service cycle a request arrives, says 0 at C3, and 0 is gated WORSE there than 1 by \StQcapJamFloorGap{} served per step, so the correction is not cosmetic. The size of what the bound buys is the part a program office should hear: at sixteen assets on a clear link the derived capacity delivers \StQcapClearBest{} served per step against the unbounded console's \StQcapClearNone{}, a factor of \StQcapClearRatio{}, and \StQcapSuccGain{} points of mission success at eight (\StQcapSuccBound\% against \StQcapSuccNone\%). It is one integer in a configuration file. To test the rule's mechanism without the rest of \sandtable{} in the way we also wrote a second, independent implementation of the console queue alone, from the model description rather than from the simulator's source, which reproduces those argmaxes, and swept $\lambda$ over \QcapPoints{} operating points at three loads. Bounding the backlog at all is worth a median factor of \QcapUnboundedGainMed{} in delivered service over not bounding it. Sizing it exactly is worth a few percent on top of that, which is the ratio a program office should hear first.

\paragraph{The bound is a plateau, not a peak, and which way to err reverses with load.} Reading an argmax off this response would be reading noise: the set of capacities delivering within one material effect of the best is one capacity wide at $\lambda = 1.3$ and six wide at $\lambda = \QcapLamMax{}$. Scored against membership in that set rather than against the highest point, the rule above is outside it at \QcapHalfMiss{} of \QcapPoints{} points and the version without the correction term is outside at \QcapFloorMiss{}, every one of those on the degraded link. So the correction term earns its place and its exact value does not: two independent scans, one over load in the auxiliary model and one over the requester's patience inside \sandtable{}, disagree about whether $c$ is a third or a half and agree that the plateau is wider than the difference, so the honest form of the rule is $k^{\star} = \lfloor \lambda + c \rfloor - 1$ with $c$ between a third and a half and undetermined here. No single fixed number covers the range: a bound of two is inside the plateau at \QcapFixedTwoFrac\% of the points and fails everywhere below $\lambda = \QcapFixedTwoBadHi{}$. The direction in which to err is not fixed either. Averaged over the grid, one capacity too few costs \QcapUnderCostEight\% of delivered service and one too many costs \QcapOverCostEight\% at a load of roughly twice the console's capacity, where over-sizing is the worse error at \QcapOverWorseFracEight\% of points; by five times capacity the same two numbers are \QcapUnderCostTwentyfour\% and \QcapOverCostTwentyfour\% and over-sizing is worse at only \QcapOverWorseFracTwentyfour\%. The mechanism is the one the bufferbloat literature is about. A length cap has no fixed meaning in time. While the backlog is overflowing, every arrival evicts the oldest, so a request advances one place per removal at the arrival rate $\Lambda$ and the console picks it up about $k/\Lambda$ old; while the backlog is draining, advancement is by service alone, the age is $j/\mu$ at depth $j$, and the load does not enter. The measured age sits between those two bounds and what moves with load is the mixture weight. What survives either reading is the inverse dependence on $\Lambda$, and it is large: at a fixed cap of \QcapAgeK{} the console picks up work \QcapAgeLight{} steps old when requests arrive at \QcapAgeRateLight{} per step and \QcapAgeHeavy{} steps old at \QcapAgeRateHeavy{} per step, against overflow-limit values of \QcapAgeFlowLight{} and \QcapAgeFlowHeavy{}. The same cap is a different deadline at each load, which is why the plateau is narrow only in a window of load, roughly one to four times console capacity, and why any bound will do outside it.

\paragraph{What the console must know, and what the message must carry.} That last point is an argument for controlling the request's age instead of the backlog's length, so we built the age cap too and measured it against the counter. The three rungs differ in information, not in mechanism: bounding the backlog needs $P$, $\ell$ and $\mu$ and no change to the message; capping the request's age needs $P$ and $\mu$ and a send timestamp, which every protocol already has; refusing on the requester's own deadline needs a semantic field somebody has to define and populate, which is the \texttt{admit} policy priced earlier in this section. Across \QcapLinks{} link latencies at a fixed deadline, one age cap covers \QcapBestAgeCov{} of them within a material effect against \QcapBestCounterCov{} for the best single counter, and its worst shortfall is \QcapBestAgeShort{} against \QcapBestCounterShort{}. The counter is fine until the link is bad and then falls off a cliff, from \QcapCounterClear{} served per step on a clear link to \QcapCounterDeep{} at a one-way latency of six steps, where the age cap still delivers \QcapAgeClear{} and \QcapAgeDeep{}, or \QcapDeepGain\% more. That cliff is outside this study's ladder, which tops out at four steps at C3, and inside the ladder the extra machinery buys nothing: with the latency stepping between clear and C3 mid-mission, the fixed counter delivers \QcapSwitchCounter{} against the age cap's \QcapSwitchAge{}, a difference of \QcapSwitchDiff{} that is enormous in $t$ ($\QcapSwitchT{}$, paired on common random numbers) and below the minimum practical effect this paper reports. The recommendation is therefore the cheapest rung, and it is conditional on the link. Bound the backlog with a counter and do not change the message format \emph{while the link is good}. On a clear link at sixteen assets the counter delivers \KpClearSrvBound{} served per step against admission control's \KpClearSrvAdmit{}, a gap of \KpClearBoundVsAdmitSixteen{} that the gate rejects as immaterial, so the cheaper rung is free. At C3 it is not: the counter delivers \KpJamSrvBound{} against \KpJamSrvAdmit{}, a gap of \KpJamBoundVsAdmitSixteen{} that gates down at both twelve and sixteen assets, and the console's own accounting says why. Under the counter at C3 the console spends \KpJamLateBound{} served per step working requests whose answers arrive after the requester has gone; under admission control that bin is \KpJamLateAdmit{}. A length bound is a proxy for the request's age, and the proxy is good exactly while the queue is most of the delay. As the one-way latency grows, $2\ell$ eats the deadline and the residual budget the queue is allowed to consume shrinks until only a direct test against the deadline can size it. So the ladder does not have one answer: on a static good link, buy the counter; on a link that degrades, the semantic field earns its cost, and the crossover in this study is between C2 and C3. One consequence is worth naming because it retires a recommendation this section made earlier. Given the counter, the queue discipline stops mattering: at the jammed operating point $k^{\star} = \QcapDiscJamK{}$ and working the backlog newest-first is not a different policy at all, since a buffer holding one waiting request has no order to permute, and on a clear link, where $k^{\star} = \QcapDiscClearK{}$, the two orders differ by \QcapDiscClearDiff{} served per step. Inverting the queue and bounding it are substitutes, not complements, and the bound is the one with a closed form behind it.

\paragraph{One field, two readings, opposite fixes.} The ladder above is keyed to the link, and it has a second key that matters more to a requirement writer, because a program office can choose its link and cannot choose how unlike each other its vehicles are. Re-running the same four rungs on a fleet whose patience is two-point rather than uniform separates them where the uniform fleet could not, and the separation runs the wrong way for the intuitive reading. Reported adjacent-rung, because a program office buys the next rung and not the whole ladder: bounding the backlog at one is worth \LdMSrvBoundTwentyFour{} served per step at twenty-four assets (\LdMSrvBoundTwentyFourV{}) and takes exclusion, the share of vehicles that never get an answer at all, down by \LdMStarveBoundTwentyFour{} (\LdMStarveBoundTwentyFourV{}). Adding the deadline field on top is worth a further \LdMSrvFieldTwentyFour{} served per step (\LdMSrvFieldTwentyFourV{}), which is most of what the bound itself delivered, and it puts exclusion \emph{back up} by \LdMStarveFieldTwentyFour{} (\LdMStarveFieldTwentyFourV{}). Reading the same field a second time, to order the backlog rather than to refuse from it, adds \LdMSrvOrderTwentyFour{} served per step, which is \LdMSrvOrderTwentyFourV{}, and takes exclusion down by \LdMStarveOrderTwentyFour{} (\LdMStarveOrderTwentyFourV{}). In levels: the admitting console leaves \LdMExclField{} of its vehicles unanswered, the ordering console \LdMExclOrder{}, a factor of \LdMExclRatio{}, and the free bound that knows nothing at all leaves \LdMExclBound{}, so on this axis the informed console is several times worse than the ignorant one. The ordering is not free either: it costs \LdMRateOrderTwentyFour{} of mean answer rate (\LdMRateOrderTwentyFourV{}), which is the fairness-for-throughput trade this section reports elsewhere, reached independently on a different fleet.

The reason to put this in a requirements section rather than a queueing one is that both rungs are the same field. Same definition, same population effort, same integration cost, same line in an interface control document. What differs is which line of console code reads it, and the sign of the outcome turns on that. A requirement reading \emph{the console shall use request deadlines} is satisfied by a vendor who refuses on them, by a vendor who orders on them, and by a vendor who does both, and those three consoles differ by a factor of \LdMExclRatio{} in how many vehicles they abandon. The requirement has to name the rule and not the field. On a uniform fleet none of this is visible: the ordering rung is byte-identical to the refusing rung on \LdUEdfSame{} of \LdUEdfCells{} measured cells, because with equal deadlines there is nothing to order, and a test program that certifies a console against a homogeneous fleet will certify the wrong one. Two smaller results ride along. The second buffer slot is worse than the first on every metric at every span, \LdMSrvTwoTwentyFour{} served per step and \LdMStarveTwoTwentyFour{} exclusion, which is the direct backing for rounding $k^{\star}$ DOWN when it is uncertain. And on the uniform fleet the whole requirement is the bound: it is worth \LdUBoundEight{} of mission success at eight assets (\LdUBoundEightV{}) and every rung above it is \LdUFieldEightV{}, and the one place the whole-ladder gap is worth quoting is the collapse span, where the blind unbounded console delivers \LdCollapseBlind{} served per step against \LdCollapseBest{}, a factor of \LdCollapseRatio{}. Quoting that factor beside each rung, which is what a table against the blind console would do, would credit every later rung with what the first one delivered.

\paragraph{And the exclusion half does not need the field at all.} The paragraph above prices ordering as a second reading of the deadline field, which is how it was built. It is not how it has to be bought. Serving whoever has been served least reads nothing about the request, no deadline, no timestamp, no difficulty: only a counter the console already keeps about itself. On the same admitting console with the same mixed fleet, against arrival order, that free rule takes exclusion down by \RnRrExclTwentyFour{} at twenty-four assets (\RnRrExclTwentyFourV{}) where the deadline field takes it down by \RnEdfExclTwentyFour{}. Arrival order leaves \RnLevelFcfs{} of the fleet unanswered there; the free counter leaves \RnLevelRr{} and the field leaves \RnLevelEdf{}. Compared directly, the largest gap between them on exclusion at any span is \RnFreeVsFieldExcl{}, which is zero to four decimals. The gate cannot certify that agreement and should not be read as doing so: earliest-deadline order is exactly zero at four of the six spans, so a relative margin against it is undefined and the guard says so. What the runs support is the weaker and sufficient statement that the two rules leave the same number of vehicles unanswered.

Where they part is throughput, and there the field earns its cost: the free counter gives up \RnFreeVsFieldSrvTwentyFour{} served per step against the field at twenty-four assets (\RnFreeVsFieldSrvTwentyFourV{}) and \RnFreeVsFieldSrvSixteen{} at sixteen (\RnFreeVsFieldSrvSixteenV{}). So the requirement splits cleanly along the two currencies. \emph{Exclusion is fixed by not privileging arrival order, and the cheapest way to stop privileging arrival order is a counter that needs no message change at all. Throughput is bought by the deadline field, and only on a fleet whose deadlines differ.} A program office that cares about which vehicles get help does not have to define a semantic field; one that cares about how many requests get served does.

The precondition is absolute and it is the part most likely to be dropped in transmission. None of this survives on a console that cannot refuse. Running the identical four rules there, arrival order leaves \RnBlindFcfs{} of the fleet unanswered and the free counter leaves \RnBlindRr{}, the same number, while the deadline field leaves \RnBlindEdf{}, which is worse than doing nothing, and a coin flip at \RnBlindRand{} beats all three. Ordering is not a lever on a console that has no capacity to allocate; it is a way of choosing who is denied. The control that keeps this from being a story about our scenario is the case with no scheduling problem in it, where every rule reads at most \RnControlMax{} and none of them can win.

\paragraph{How much the console has to know is set by how unlike each other its vehicles are.} Two fleets are two points, and a requirement needs the curve between them, so we swept the fleet's patience spread directly at sixteen assets. The free bound loses \LdHBoundLoss{} of its throughput across that sweep, falling from \LdHBoundTwoSZero{} served per step on an identical fleet to \LdHBoundTwoSTwelve{} at the widest readable spread, while the deadline field loses nothing at all: \LdHAdmitsSZero{} to \LdHAdmitsSTwelve{}, flat to three decimals across the same range. That is the requirement in one sentence. The cheap rung and the expensive rung are equivalent on a fleet whose vehicles all want the same thing, and the gap between them opens in proportion to how much they differ, because a bound refuses by arrival order and arrival order is uninformative about a deadline exactly when deadlines vary.

Two things finish the argument, and the first closes the obvious escape. A program office reading the above will ask whether a better-sized bound would do, since sizing is free and a semantic field is not. Sweeping the bound over seven capacities on both fleets says no. The best capacity is \LdKUniStar{} on the uniform fleet and tightens to \LdKMixStar{} on the mixed one, which is the direct evidence for rounding $k^{\star}$ down, and the best achievable free bound on the mixed fleet delivers \LdKMixBest{} served per step against the field's \LdHAdmitsSTwelve{}. The bound cannot be sized out of its deficit: at its own optimum it gets \LdKMixShortfall{} of what the field gets. The second is a caution about where this sweep stops being readable. The widest spread in the grid, \LdHCensorSpread{}, puts the impatient half of the fleet at a patience of \LdHCensorPat{} steps against a mean service of \LdHCensorSvc{}, so half the fleet cannot be served by any console whatever it knows, and every repaired arm reads exactly one half excluded there. That is a floor set by the fleet and not a console result, so the numbers above stop at \LdHLast{}. At that last readable spread the ordering console excludes \LdHExclOrderLast{} against the refusing console's \LdHExclFieldLast{} and the free bound's \LdHExclBoundLast{}, which is the same reversal as before, now located.
\paragraph{Knowledge sets the ceiling; the rule decides who pays.} The paragraph above retired the
service order on a bounded console, and the retirement stands exactly where it was measured: at the
derived capacity the backlog holds one or two waiting requests, and a queue of one has no order to
permute. That is a statement about a console that has already bought the counter and sized it. It is
not a statement about the order itself, and the rest of this section is about a console with a real
backlog, which is what an admitting console without a capacity bound has. On that console the order
is the largest single lever in this paper, and everything below is measured there.

The question it answers is orthogonal to the ladder. Everything priced so far is about what the
console must be TOLD; this is about what it does with what it already has. Every console number in
this paper up to this point is a mean, and a mean cannot see exclusion. The fleets these consoles
serve are not uniform: a vehicle in contact and a vehicle in a march column ask the same operator
for help with very different amounts of time to spare. We therefore added three readings that split
the same answers rather than adding them up. The first divides the fleet at its median deadline and
reports answers per step to each half, so its ratio is one when service is proportionate and rises as
the console favors those who can wait. The second counts the vehicles that asked and never received a
single answer over the whole run. The third is the interquartile range of the per-vehicle answer
rate. None of them changes the simulation; they change only what is recorded, and the same runs that
produced the aggregates produce these.

Table~\ref{tab:fair} prices seven service orders at sixteen assets on a clear link. Every row reads
the same message, sees the same job, and has exactly the same information; only the order differs,
so this is not another rung of the ladder above but a separate axis crossing it. The ordering
everything defaults to is the worst one on the page. First come, first served answers the patient
half of the fleet \FrLvFcfsRatio{} times as often as the impatient half and leaves
\FrLvFcfsStarv{} of its vehicles with no answer at all; working the same queue in deadline order
takes the ratio to \FrLvEdfRatio{}, which is proportionate service, and the exclusion to
\FrLvEdfStarv{}. Against first come, first served the deadline order moves the ratio by
\FrEdfRatio{} ($t = \FrEdfRatioT{}$), exclusion by \FrEdfStarv{} and the spread by \FrEdfIqr{}, and
it does this while \emph{also} raising the mean answer rate by \FrEdfRate{} and throughput by
\FrEdfSrv{} and lowering abandonment by \FrEdfAband{}. It is not a fairness-for-efficiency trade. It
dominates on every column measured.

The result that was not predicted is in the second row. Serving the queue in \emph{random} order
recovers most of the harm, moving the ratio \FrRandRatio{} of the deadline order's \FrEdfRatio{},
and so does shortest-job-first at \FrEasyRatio{}. Randomness is not a policy anyone would field, and
it has no information at all, so a rule that a coin flip beats by that margin is not merely
suboptimal: first come, first served is actively regressive, and the mechanism is that arrival order
is correlated with slack. The one order that is worse is the one that sounds most considerate.
Serving the most patient requester first moves the ratio \FrPatRatio{} in the wrong direction and
loses on throughput as well.

\paragraph{What is prior here and what is not.} Most of the mechanism in the table above is old, and
saying so is what makes the part that is not old defensible. That a size-based rule shortens the
queue for short jobs is \citet{kleinrock1964timeshared}; that shortest-first minimises mean waiting
at the price of starving the large job is the standing concern of the class-based fair-queueing
literature \citep{khayari2005classbased}; that earliest-deadline-first is optimal for a capacitated
server with heterogeneous due dates and a penalty for non-completion is
\citet{xu2016duedate}, whose model is this console with the names changed. Most elementary of all,
under first come, first served with reneging a request is served if and only if its wait falls
inside its own patience, so a longer deadline is a higher service probability by arithmetic rather
than by any emergent property \citep{barrer1957ordered}. Deadline order beating arrival order here is
a known result reproduced in a new setting, not a discovery.

Three things are ours, and they are narrow. First, the axis rotates. Classical starvation is harm
along an axis of the WORK, the long job waits; here it is harm along an axis of the CLIENT, the
impatient vehicle is never helped, and the plain console has no size rule at all, so classical
starvation is absent exactly where client starvation peaks. Second, the sign of size-based control
flips with the axis: in the scheduling literature a size rule causes starvation, and here a service
cap removes it. Third, and this is the part with a program consequence, the same deadline field
supports two rules that gate in opposite directions on exclusion at matched aggregate performance,
which means a service-level objective written as a percentile budget and one written as an ordering
rule are not two encodings of one requirement. The ex ante framing is
\citet{liu2023segmentation}'s: a vehicle's deadline is a property it arrives with and this console
never observes it, so the stratification cannot be attributed to a rule that discriminates.

\paragraph{How much of it is available without the semantic field.} The ladder above prices what a
console must be told; the table prices what it does with what it has. Crossing the two gives the
question a program actually faces, which is how much of the repair survives if the expensive rung is
not bought. Most of it. Every row of the table runs on the same admitting console, so what varies is
the information the DISCIPLINE consumes and nothing else. Measured as a fraction of the movement
deadline order achieves on each reading, working the queue in random order, a discipline that reads
no field at all, recovers \FxRandStarv\% of the exclusion, \FxRandRatio\% of the stratification
and \FxRandIqr\% of the spread. Sorting instead on the length of the job, which requires the
console to see what it has been handed, is indistinguishable from the coin flip on all three
(\FxEasyStarv\%, \FxEasyRatio\% and \FxEasyIqr\%). Refusing on that same estimate with a service
cap does better, \FxCapStarv\%, \FxCapRatio\% and \FxCapIqr\%, and still does not close it.

So the semantic field is not what buys the fairness. It buys the last increment of it and, separately,
the aggregate improvement: the deadline order is the only rule in the table that raises throughput and
answer rate while compressing the distribution, and every cheaper rule pays a little of one for the
other. The recommendation splits cleanly along that line. A program that cannot change its message
format should still stop working its queue oldest-first, and will get most of the distributional fix
for nothing; a program that can define a deadline field gets the rest of the fix and a throughput
gain with it. Neither recommendation depends on knowing the span the console will be flown at.

\paragraph{Two rules, one field, opposite signs.} A console that cannot see the job it has been
handed can still read a deadline, and there are two things it can do with that number. It can budget:
refuse any request whose deadline is shorter than a quantile of the service distribution, which is
the natural way to protect a saturated server and is what a percentile service-level objective asks
for. Or it can order: work the shortest deadline first. Both read the identical field. At sixteen
assets, against a console budgeting on the mean, an eightieth-percentile budget moves the
stratification ratio by \FqQEightyRatio{} and exclusion by \FqQEightyStarv{} while its mean answer
rate is \FqQEightyRateV{} and its throughput is \FqQEightySrvV{}. It is regressive at matched
aggregate performance, which is exactly the failure the aggregates were blind to. Raising the
percentile makes it worse: a ninety-fifth-percentile budget moves the ratio \FqQNinetyfiveRatio{}
and leaves \FqLvQNinetyfiveStarv{} of a mixed fleet with nothing, against \FqLvMeanStarv{} for the
mean budget and \FqLvEdfBStarv{} for deadline ordering. This is the sixth instance in this paper of
one measurement supporting two rules that gate in opposite directions, and it is the cleanest,
because here the two rules read the same number out of the same message.

\paragraph{What the deadline order actually spends the console on.} The console's own effort
accounting says why the gain is not a reshuffle. Decomposing the slots at a degraded link, the
deadline order works \FeLate{} more served-equivalents per step in the bin whose answers arrive after
the requester has gone ($t = \FeLateT{}$), and the bin lost to the link is \FeLostV{} at \FeLost{}.
The third bin, work spent on requests whose vehicle has already asked again, is EMPTY in both arms
rather than equal between them: a console that refuses at the head never carries a superseded record
long enough to reach it, so \FeStale{} there is a structural zero and not a measurement. It is worth
saying which of those two things a bin is, because a contrast drawn against a reference that is
exactly zero has no relative margin and would gate on any difference at all; the survey now refuses
such a cell out loud, and this is the sentence it corrected. What the decomposition shows is that
deadline order is not avoiding doomed work. It is spending more of the console on requests that were about to expire, which is the
same trade a triage nurse makes and the opposite of the one a first-come queue makes.

\paragraph{Two qualifiers, both load-bearing.} The dominance above holds for a console that can see
the job in front of it. Where it cannot, deadline ordering stops being free: against the same
mean-budget reference, ordering by deadline without job sight costs \FqEdfBRate{} of mean answer
rate and \FqEdfBSrv{} of throughput to buy its compression. The recommendation is therefore welded
to a precondition, and the precondition is the same discernment requirement priced earlier in this
section. Second, the two levers do not add. Adding deadline order to a capped console is a strict
improvement: \FcAddEdfRatioFull{} on stratification, \FcAddEdfRateFull{} on mean answer rate,
\FcAddEdfAbandFull{} on abandonment, with throughput \FcAddEdfSrvFull{} and immaterial. Adding a cap
to a deadline-ordered console is a trade rather than a gain: \FcAddCapSrvFull{} of throughput bought
with \FcAddCapAbandFull{} more abandonment and no fairness movement at all, and at eight assets the
same trade loses on both. \textbf{Adopt the discipline first and unconditionally; the cap is a
throughput-for-abandonment trade laid on top of it, and one a program should take deliberately or
not at all.}

\paragraph{The same behavior, on a different knob and a uniform fleet.} The stratification above is a
property of a mixed fleet, and the obvious objection is that a fleet with one deadline cannot be
stratified. It can still be excluded. Running the five backlog levers of Section~\ref{subsec:queue}
on a fleet in which every vehicle is identical, a plain first-in, first-out console with an unbounded
backlog leaves \FmBandFifo{} of its vehicles with no answer at all across the whole run while working
a backlog of \FmFifoBack{} requests. Last-in, first-out holds a \emph{larger} backlog, \FmLifoBack{},
and excludes \FmLifoStarv{}, because it answers the request a vehicle just made rather than the one
it has already given up on. The number replicates to three decimals on all three maps
(\FmBandFifo{}, \FmChokeFifo{}, \FmOpenFifo{}), which makes it a console property and not a terrain
one. Different knob, different fleet, same behavior: working the oldest request first is the most
harmful single thing this console does, and it is the default everywhere.

\paragraph{The console works exactly as hard under every policy, and that is the result.} Delivered
service is only one of four ways a claimed slot can end, and \sandtable{} counts all four: the answer
reaches a requester still waiting, or the requester has already given up and asked again, or the
reply is lost on the way back, or the reply arrives after the deadline. Adding them gives the total
work the console did, as distinct from the work that was worth doing, and running the five levers in
one allocation makes the two directly comparable. At sixteen assets on a clear link the total is
between \KpClearEffortMin{} and \KpClearEffortMax{} served-equivalents per step for every one of the
five, a spread of \KpClearEffortSpread{} against a materiality floor of \LvMPE{}. Delivered service
over the same five spans \KpClearSrvSpread{}, a factor of \KpClearRatio{}. The console is saturated
in all five cases and it is saturated to the same degree; nothing any of these policies does makes
the operator work harder or less hard. What they change is the composition of a fixed quantity, and
the whole of the span-of-control result in this section is a statement about that composition rather
than about capacity. It follows that working the operator harder is not available as a lever, which
is worth saying plainly to a program office that has been offered training as the answer.

Three scope conditions belong on that sentence, because without them it says more than the
measurement does. It is about INTENSITY and not about capacity: how fast the console works is a
different parameter, it is a lever, and this paper prices it at one discipline change. It is about a
SATURATED console: below the collapse span the console is demand-limited and idle time is real, so
the invariance is a property of the regime the span-of-control question lives in rather than of the
model. And the four bins count decisions, which is a faithful measure of effort only while every
decision costs the same. Once service times vary, a console admitting on a deadline test finishes
short decisions and refuses long ones, so decisions per busy step rises with nothing added to the
console; time cannot be selected that way, and the invariance is stated here in the unit that
cannot. The constant-service consoles this section reports are the case where the two units agree.

The same accounting names a loss that no lever in this paper touches, and it is large where the
paper's advice is weakest. At C3 the best-performing console spends \KpJamLostAdmit{} served per
step, roughly a fifth of its total effort, on decisions it completed and whose replies the downlink
then dropped. That slot is gone, the requester is not served, and no queue discipline, bound or
admission rule can recover it, because the loss happens after the console has finished. One modeling
choice sets the size of that bin and should be stated with it: replies in \sandtable{} are sent
once, with no retransmission and no cache, so the number is the cost of a link with no reply-side
protocol at all. A console that resent a dropped reply would pay for it in service time rather than
in lost decisions, which moves the loss between bins instead of removing it, but the split is not
one this study measured and the bin should be read as an upper bound on what reply transport costs. It is the
reason the C3 ceiling in Figure~\ref{fig:queue} sits where it does, and it is a communications
requirement rather than a teaming one. Separating it out matters for how the section's advice should
be read: the levers priced here divide up the console's time, and at a bad enough link the binding
constraint has moved off the console entirely.


\paragraph{Four levers, one mechanism, and the control that shows it.} The levers of this section
were built one at a time against different failures, and read together at full tier they turn out to
be the same lever. At sixteen assets on a clear link the blind console has collapsed to \LvSrvFifo{}
useful decisions per step, against a capacity of \LvMu{}. Refusing work at the door recovers
\LvSrvAdmit{}; bounding the backlog at $k^{\star} = 2$ recovers \LvSrvBound{}; working the backlog
newest-first recovers \LvSrvLifo{}. Three policies with different information requirements, within
\LvMPE{} of each other, which is this paper's materiality floor. The natural reading is that
oldest-first is simply a bad order and that anything else is better, and the control arm refutes it:
service in \emph{random} order recovers \LvRandGain{} against newest-first's \LvLifoGain{}, which is
\LvRandShare\% of it and is equivalent to zero at the same span. Breaking first-come-first-served is
not the mechanism. That the mechanism is the deadline rather than the order is not a new claim, and
the prior art is closer than any of the supervisory-control literature this paper otherwise engages
with. \citet{doshi1983hybrid} analyses an $M/G/1$ queue whose server takes the oldest customer whose
delay is under a threshold and, failing that, the youngest customer already past it, and compares it
against last-in-first-out on exactly the quantity used here, the throughput of successfully served
customers. He states the condition that makes such a discipline worth having in one clause, that the
success or failure of a service depends on the delay in providing it, and the optimality side of the
same problem is \citet{ross2015sequential}, where impatient jobs carry rewards and the list policy
maximising expected return is derived. What is new here is the console \emph{below} that information
level. Doshi's server reads each customer's current delay; ours cannot tell that a requester has
given up at all, which is the premise of the collapse. Newest-first is therefore not an
approximation to the optimal policy but the best rule available to a server that knows only arrival
order, and the reason the bound is preferred to it is that a counter is less than an order. What the three working levers share is visible in the console's own accounting.
The fraction of the console's requests that it eventually works after the requester has given up and
asked again is \LvStaleFifo\% under oldest-first and \LvStaleRand\% under random order, against
\LvStaleLifo\% under newest-first and \LvStaleAdmit\% under admission control. The console's capacity
is fixed; every step of it goes either to an answer somebody is holding for or to one nobody is, and
the three policies that work are the three that stop spending it on the second kind. Random order
does not, because drawing uniformly from a backlog that is mostly stale mostly draws stale work.

That reduction is what makes the three levers comparable, because it says they cannot be stacked and
must instead be priced. \sandtable{} prices them at the same operating point. Admission control needs
a semantic deadline field in the message and leaves a mean backlog of \LvBackAdmit{} requests. The
counter needs no change to the message and bounds the backlog at $k^{\star}+1$ by construction.
Newest-first also needs no change to the message, and its backlog is \LvBackLifo{} requests against
oldest-first's \LvBackFifo{}: it is \emph{larger} than the policy it beats. That reads as a cost and
we first reported it as one, which was wrong, and the correction is worth stating because the wrong
version is the intuitive one. A mean backlog counts requests that never leave. It does not count
requesters who never get an answer, and under newest-first those are not the same set, because a
requester whose first request is buried asks again and its new request lands on \emph{top} of the
pile it was buried under. Measured directly, as the fraction of assets that asked at least once and
were never once answered, oldest-first abandons \KpClearStarveFifo\% of the force at sixteen assets
and random order abandons \KpClearStarveRand\%, while newest-first, the bound and admission control
between them strand at most \KpClearStarveFair\%. The harm arrives with the collapse and not before:
no policy strands anybody through six assets, and at eight oldest-first is already stranding
\KpClearStarveOnsetHi\%. Jamming does not reorder the column, it only sharpens it, to
\KpJamStarveFifo\% against \KpJamStarveFair\% at C3. The backlog under newest-first is memory, not
harm, and the honest statement of its price is that it holds a large dead queue rather than that it
starves a tail.

That correction generalizes past this one policy, and the generalization is the more useful half of
it. One allocation at sixteen assets admits three readings, all from the same runs. Operator
utilization puts oldest-first at \KpClearUtilFifo{} and newest-first at \KpClearUtilLifo{}, which is
the same console to four figures. Mean backlog puts them at \KpClearBackFifo{} and
\KpClearBackLifo{}, which favours oldest-first. The fraction of its own requests that a vehicle ever
gets an answer to puts them at \KpClearAnsFifo{} and \KpClearAnsLifo{}, a factor of
\KpClearAnsRatio{}. Two of the three readings say the two policies are equivalent or that the wrong
one is better, and the third says one of them answers \KpClearAnsRatio{} times as much of what its
force asked for. The two that fail are the two referenced to the console: utilization is measured against the console's clock and
backlog against the console's queue, and neither has the requester in it. The one that works is
measured from the vehicle's end. This is not a defect of those two statistics, which are correct
about what they measure. It is that a metric cannot audit a decision that sets its own reference,
and a scheduling discipline sets both the clock's occupancy and the queue's contents. The
substitution is
measurable rather than argued: once the console can refuse, reordering what it accepted is worth
\LvSubEight{} served per step at eight assets and \LvSubSixteen{} at sixteen, negative at every span
in the sweep and equivalent to zero at every one. Two levers aimed at the same waste do not add.

Two boundaries belong with the recommendation. The first is that over-sizing the counter is not a
soft error at this operating point: at sixteen assets the ladder runs \LvCapTwo{} served per step at
the derived bound, \LvCapThree{} one above it, \LvCapFour{} two above, \LvCapSix{} four above and
\LvCapTen{} at a bound of ten, which is already indistinguishable from the unbounded console's
\LvSrvNone{}. A generous buffer is not a conservative choice. The second is that the ordering lever
is a congestion remedy and not a communications one, and the ladder dates it precisely: newest-first
is worth \LvJamClear{} served per step over oldest-first on a clear link, \LvJamOne{} at C1,
\LvJamTwo{} at C2, \LvJamThree{} at C3 and exactly \LvJamFour{} at C4, where nothing in the backlog
is serviceable inside its requester's patience and there is no order in which to work an unanswerable
queue. Every lever in this section stops paying at the same rung, and it is the rung where the
latency ceiling $\ell^{\star}$ is crossed rather than any property of the console.


\paragraph{Fan-out cannot warn of the collapse, whichever way the wait is booked.} The standard supervisory-control statistic for operator capacity is fan-out, $1 + NT/IT$, the ratio of neglect time to interaction time \citep{olsen2004fanout,cummings2008predicting}. Measuring it inside \sandtable{} while the queue collapses gives a negative result, and the negative is sharper than a bare failure to track capacity. Fan-out is load-dependent rather than a platform constant: it falls from \qFanTwo{} at two assets to \qFanTwelve{} at twelve. Across the interval where delivery actually fails it is nearly flat, \qFanEight{} at eight assets against \qFanTen{} at ten, while vehicles delivered over the same interval fall from \qArrivedEight{} to \qArrivedTwelve{} at twelve.

That floor has a closed form, and it explains why. Counting the whole held period as interaction, measured neglect is pinned at $\tau + 1$ across the entire span sweep while every bit of the load dependence sits in the interaction term, which is not interaction at all but the request sojourn $W(n)$, saturating at $P$. So the statistic is $1 + (\tau+1)/W(n)$, and past the cliff, where $W \to P$, it converges to
\[
1 + \frac{\tau + 1}{P} = \qFanFloor{},
\]
a ratio of two \emph{client} timing constants. The service rate and the console count have vanished. It is not that fan-out fails to see this cliff; at the cliff the quantity has stopped being about the operator at all.

Booking the wait differently does not rescue it, and we report the alternatives because a referee can compute them from the same run. Excluding the wait entirely, the classic reading, gives \qFanClassic{} at the collapsed operating point, wrong in the other direction. Counting the wait as neglect instead gives \qFanUtil{}, which does land near $n^{\star} = \qNStar{}$, but only at saturation: it reads the capacity correctly at the moment capacity has already run out, and below the cliff it reads low. No booking of the duty cycle predicts the cliff in advance. Fan-out measures how long an asset can be neglected before it needs help; it does not measure whether help arrives, which is exactly the failure unrefunded abandonment produces. An operator-capacity number computed from neglect and interaction times should not be used to size a span without a queueing check alongside it, and the closed form of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} is the only forward-looking instrument here.

\paragraph{The queue reaches into terrain and route choice.} Operator demand is not exogenous. When the request rate is tied to trafficability, so that an asset in difficult ground asks for help more often (\texttt{terrain\_demand}), the collapse threshold moves left: at six assets, goodput falls from \qTerrainFlatSix\% on easy ground to \qTerrainHardSix\% under the strongest coupling, which is the behavior the flat-ground model shows only at eight. The route-choice study of Section~\ref{subsec:uc3} changes character as a result. There, more cover was monotonically better, and the best route bias was full cover. Under direct control with terrain-correlated demand the covered lane is both slower and harder ground, so it costs attention the operator does not have, and success peaks at a bias of \qRouteDirectArgmax{} (\qRouteDirectBest\%) before falling to \qRouteDirectFull\% at full cover. The interior optimum needs both halves of the coupling: on flat ground the same direct arm still prefers full cover (\qRouteFlatFull\%), and on the same difficult terrain so do supervisory control (\qRouteSupFull\%) and admission control (\qRouteAdmFull\%). Route planning under direct control is therefore an operator-load decision as much as a survivability one, and the interior optimum is an artifact of the human in the loop rather than of the ground.

\paragraph{What the deadline does, and does not, do.} One correction belongs in the record because a cheap robustness check overturned a stronger reading. At the centerpiece's \qDeadline{}~s mission window, \texttt{blind} direct control delivers \qArrivedSix{} vehicles at six assets, \qArrivedEight{} at eight, and \qArrivedTwelve{} at twelve, which reads as a capacity cliff at the predicted span. Re-running the same configurations at a \qLongWindow{}~s window removes the cliff: the same twelve-asset case then delivers \qArrivedTwelveLong{} vehicles and ten delivers \qArrivedTenLong{}, and delivery becomes monotone increasing in span again. What the queue actually imposes is a tempo penalty, and the deadline is what converts tempo into failure. The \texttt{admit} arm is identical at all three windows tested, which is the cleaner statement of the result: admission control removes the dependence on how long the mission is allowed to run. The claim the model supports is about tempo under a deadline, not about a throughput ceiling, and the window has to be named whenever the delivered-vehicle numbers are quoted.

\paragraph{Every number above was found on one map, and two more say which half of it travels.}
\sandtable{}'s terrain until now was a single profile: a lateral Gaussian band of cover with no
longitudinal structure, so a route choice is a choice of latitude. Two further profiles were built to
attack the queue results from opposite sides. \texttt{open} has no cover anywhere and is the negative
control for anything terrain is claimed to do; \texttt{chokepoint} is a transverse ridge with one gap,
so a dispersed force converges where the map chooses rather than where the operator does. Re-running
the section's main lever on all three separates two things that a single map holds together, and they
travel differently. On the console's own metric the lever is very nearly map-invariant: at sixteen
assets admission control is worth \MpBandSrvFull{} served per step over the blind console on the
band, \MpChokeSrvFull{} on the chokepoint and \MpOpenSrvFull{} in the open, a spread of
\MpSrvSpread{} against a materiality floor of \MpMPE{}. On mission success the same lever is worth
\MpBandSucFull{}, \MpChokeSucFull{} and \MpOpenSucFull{} percentage points on the same three maps,
which is a factor of nearly five. The mechanism is headroom and it is visible in the baseline: the
blind console already reaches \MpBandBlindFull\% success at sixteen assets on the band and
\MpOpenBlindFull\% in the open, so on the easy map there is almost nothing left for a console fix to
win. At the collapse span, where no map has headroom to spare, the mission-level lever converges
again: \MpBandSucColl{}, \MpChokeSucColl{} and \MpOpenSucColl{} points.

The capacity rule survives the same treatment, with one qualification that is worth more than the
result it qualifies. On delivered service its argmax is $k^{\star} = 2$ on all three maps at all
three spans, and the shape of the error is map-invariant as well as the answer:
across \MpCapCells{} cells the derived bound beats one below it at \MpCapLoWin{} of them, by at most
\MpCapUnderWorst{} served per step, which never reaches the materiality floor, and beats two above it
by a material margin at \MpCapHiWin{} of \MpCapCells{}, by at least \MpCapOverWorst{}. Under-sizing by
one is free on every map and over-sizing by two is costly on every map. The closed form carries no
terrain term and the measurement says it does not need one. The qualification is that on mission
success, the metric a sponsor will actually read, the rule is not located that sharply at all. There
the derived bound beats one below it at only \MpSucCapLoWin{} of \MpCapCells{} cells and the largest
gap either way is \MpSucCapLoGap{}~percentage points, so the argmax flips between them by map and is
not measuring anything. Two above remains worse at \MpSucCapHiWin{} of \MpCapCells{}. Read on the
mission, then, the rule's content is not that the bound is two rather than one; it is that the bound
exists and is small. That is a weaker claim than the console metric supports and it is the one to
put in front of a program office, because it is the one that survives being measured on the quantity
they care about.

There is a general point here about how a console result should be measured, and it is the reason
this paragraph reports the console metric first. A lever that acts on the operator is a lever on the
operator whatever the ground looks like, but its consequence for the mission is bounded by how much
of the mission was at risk. A study that measures only the mission metric, on a map where the mission
succeeds anyway, will report that a real fix does not matter, and will be wrong for a reason that has
nothing to do with the fix. That is not a caveat about our maps. It is a claim about what any
span-of-control result means when it is quoted without the scenario it was measured in, and it is the
same failure the fan-out result of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} exhibits one level down.

\subsection{What the span result is about: the sign reverses when the enemy scales too}
\label{subsec:attrib}

Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} sweeps span against a fixed defending force, which is how span-of-control studies are usually run and which confounds two things. Adding assets to one operator loads the console, and it also improves the force ratio. A result read as teaming may be a result about arithmetic. The test is to sweep the same span with the defenders scaled so the ratio never moves.

The sign reverses. From \atSpanLo{} to \atSpanHi{} assets at a fixed defending force, mission success rises \atFixDir{} ($t = \atFixDirT$). Over the identical span with the ratio held, it falls \atHeldDir{} ($t = \atHeldDirT$), from \atDirLo{} to \atDirHi{}. The difference in differences is \atDid{} at $t = \atDidT$ with \atDidBlocks{} of \atDidN{} seed-matched blocks agreeing. A reversal of this size is not a confound to be controlled away and reported in a limitations paragraph. It is the identification: whatever the free force ratio contributes, the held-ratio arm has none of it, and what remains is a team that gets worse as it grows.

\paragraph{Three channels, and two of them can be bounded.} The reversal admits three readings, and they carry different consequences for a program office. The console may be congesting, which is the mechanism of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} and is a teaming result. Holding the ratio scales the defending force, which deepens the defending column on one lane rather than widening the engagement, and that is a scenario fact. Or \texttt{success} may be moving without anything underneath it moving, because it is the single bit $\text{arrived} \geq \tfrac{1}{2}n$ and a binomial gets sharper as $n$ grows, so a step in the reported metric can be a step across the cut rather than a step in the fraction.

Supervisory control has no console at all, so its held-ratio step bounds the second and third channels together: whatever it still loses cannot be the queue. It loses \atHeldSup{} ($t = \atHeldSupT$) against direct control's \atHeldDir{}. The console therefore owns about \atConsoleShare\% of the collapse and everything else about \atScenarioShare\%. Keeping the console but refunding the slot of a requester who has already abandoned recovers \atRefundShare\% of the direct step, which places admission policy inside the console's share rather than beside it.

The third channel is ruled out directly rather than bounded. The same runs report \texttt{arrived\_frac}, the fraction of the assault force that reaches the objective, which is the continuous quantity \texttt{success} thresholds on the failing side of that threshold and, for a reason worth stating, not on the other side. The run stops as soon as enough vehicles have arrived, so a mission that succeeds records \texttt{arrived\_frac} at the threshold and not above it. The metric is therefore uncensored where the mission fails and pinned where it succeeds, which has two consequences for how the contrasts below are read: a difference between two arms that both succeed reads as zero on it whatever the arms actually did, and a difference between a failing arm and a succeeding one is a LOWER bound on the separation rather than an estimate of it. Every contrast reported here is of the second kind. On the direct held-ratio arm it falls monotonically at every rung, \atArrHeldDir{} in total ($t = \atArrHeldDirT$, \atArrBlocks{} of \atArrBlocksN{} blocks), and the difference in differences on it is \atArrDid{} at $t = \atArrDidT$. Nothing was sitting still while the threshold moved. The attribution repeats on the mediator at \atArrConsoleShare/\atArrScenarioShare{} against the \atConsoleShare/\atScenarioShare{} above. The agreement should be read for what it is: \texttt{success} is a deterministic function of \texttt{arrived\_frac} on the same missions, so the two are not independent measurements, and what the second establishes is that the threshold amplifies a real move rather than manufacturing one. Refunding is the one place they disagree, recovering \atRefundShare\% of the direct step on \texttt{success} and \atArrRefundShare\% on the mediator, which is the signature of a policy that acts on the tail: it moves vehicles onto the objective more readily than it moves whole missions across the half-force line.

\paragraph{An independent model found this interaction and could not explain it.} The reversal is not an artifact of our scenario, and the strongest evidence for that is a study that reported it before us without a mechanism. \citet{giachetti2012agent} build an agent-based model of an Army human-robot team searching an urban area, unrelated to this one in scenario, implementation, and metric, and cross team size with how centralized the team's decision authority is. They find a significant interaction ($F(1,12) = 9.18$, $p = 0.010$): under high centralization small teams outperform large ones, while under low centralization the difference disappears. They call the result counterintuitive, since more robots search more sectors, and they resolve it from their simulation logs, reporting that large centralized teams spend more time waiting and that the subteams are waiting on a commander who has become a bottleneck. Their reported operator capacity under those conditions is a single robot, which they note matches an experimental finding that a second ground vehicle is at best marginally useful.

What agrees is the sign, and it is worth being precise about how little else does. Their commander allocates search sectors a few times per subteam per mission; our console answers per-vehicle control requests on a \qDecisionInterval{}-step cadence. Those are different queues with different arrival processes, and a two-level contrast of centralized against decentralized cannot separate the two ways a shared decision-maker produces waiting: simple serialization, which is linear in team size at any utilization, from congestion, which is nonlinear and has a threshold. Their design was not built to, and they do not claim it. So we take this as an independent observation of the interaction's sign in an Army team model that shares none of our choices, and take no quantitative weight from it. What the closed forms add is the part a two-level design cannot supply: which of the two mechanisms is operating, and where it begins, as a quantity computable before the experiment rather than recovered afterward from output logs.

\paragraph{A human experiment recorded the interior optimum and read it as a question about subtasks.} The closest external test of the span claim is not a simulation. \citet{lewis2010choosing} had operators run a multirobot foraging task at 4, 8 and 12 robots, either performing the whole task or one of its two subtasks with the other automated. Full-task yield was \lwFullFour{} victims at four robots, \lwFullEight{} at eight, and \lwFullTwelve{} at twelve: an interior optimum, with an $N \times$ task interaction at $F(\lwInterDf) = \lwInterF$, $p = \lwInterP$. The pairwise comparisons are what make it a threshold rather than a trend. Four against eight is $p \lwFourEightP$ and eight against twelve is $p = \lwEightTwelveP$, but four against twelve is $p = \lwFourTwelveP$ and does not reach significance. Twelve robots performed like four.

We registered that shape as a prediction before obtaining their Results section, because it is where the two accounts of span separate. An operator whose finite attention is divided among competing robots should lose per-robot quality as soon as the robots compete, giving a monotone decline from four. A server whose clients abandon and re-ask should give away nothing until it saturates, and then give away a great deal. What their data shows is a return, not a decline.

Their own coverage numbers make the mechanism harder to attribute to the operator's attention than the framing suggests. Area explored in the full-task condition rose from \lwAreaFullFour{} to \lwAreaFullTwelve{} square metres between four robots and twelve, a factor of \lwAreaRatio{}, while victims found over the same range moved \lwYieldDelta{}. The fleet more than doubled the ground it covered and the mission output did not move. Whatever the operator lost at twelve robots, and they did lose something, since exploration-only operators covered \lwAreaExplTwelve{} against the full task's \lwAreaFullTwelve{} at $p = \lwAreaTwelveP{}$, it was not enough to stop the robots covering twice the ground. None of that extra ground converted. The loss is in the conversion, and conversion happens one interaction at a time at a shared server.

The condition that removes one class of console interaction removes the collapse with it. Operators doing perceptual search alone, with navigation automated, found \lwPercFour{}, \lwPercEight{} and \lwPercTwelve{} victims: monotone in team size, no interior optimum, $F(\lwPercDf) = \lwPercF$ against the full task. That is the same intervention as this paper's supervisory arm, run on human operators, and it produces the same qualitative result. We take the shape and not a capacity number, since the study reports no interaction or neglect time and no $n^\star$ is computable for their setup. What it establishes is that the interior optimum is not an artifact of simulating an operator, because it appears when the operator is a person.

\paragraph{Why reported spans disagree.} Single-operator capacities in the literature are not close to each other. Fan-out arithmetic at \sandtable{}'s own interaction time puts the ceiling near \provMagicFanout{} platforms (Section~\ref{subsec:validation}); workload modelling of multi-aircraft control puts it near three, and says so explicitly of \emph{benign} operations \citep{schneider2011workload}; and wait time alone has been measured to consume up to two thirds of an operator's capacity \citep{cummings2008predicting}. Some of that spread is task and interface. But a capacity measured with no opposing force cannot have a threat-scaling rule, because there is no threat to scale, so the question this section asks does not arise for most of the studies that report a number. It arises for every contested one, and the measurement above says the answer's sign depends on it. A span-of-control figure quoted without the rule that governed the opposition is not a quantity another study can be compared against.

The practical reading is narrower than the headline and more useful. Growing a team helps when the opposition does not grow with it, and that part is arithmetic that needs no simulator. Hold the opposition proportional and growth hurts, three quarters of it at the console. Span of control is not a property of the operator's attention alone; it is a property of the queue the operator's clients form, and it is the part of the problem a program can actually staff, schedule, or automate away.

\paragraph{Why the backoff has to be random, not merely long.} The floor of Section~\ref{subsec:band} sets how long a rebuffed vehicle should wait. It says nothing about whether every vehicle should wait the \emph{same} long time, and the two questions have different answers. Holding the mean hold fixed and widening only its jitter, so that the arms differ in nothing but the spread of their return times, is worth \jcSuccBest{} points of mission success at \jcSuccSpanNum{} assets ($t = \jcSuccBestT{}$, \jcSuccBestBlocks{} of \jcSuccBestN{} blocks). Same cadence, same mean hold, different phase. How that number was arrived at, including two readings of it that were published and withdrawn, is in the companion report on arrival-process measurement; what belongs here is the lever and its size.

What a randomized hold DOES to the arrival stream needs a control that says what crowding a fleet would have without any retries in it at all, and the natural one is a fleet whose rebuffed vehicles never come back. Against that control, a fixed hold's arrival crowding is \nrGeFixLoPct\% above a fleet that never retries at \nrGeSpanLo{} assets and \nrGeFixHiPct\% above it at \nrGeSpanHi{}, rising at every step of the sweep, gated in \nrGeFixGated{} of \nrGeFixCells{} cells at $t$ from \nrGeFixMinT{} to \nrGeFixMaxT{}. On the memoryless link the same contrast runs \nrMemFixLoPct\% to \nrMemFixHiPct\%, also rising at every step. \emph{The jittered hold of the same mean gates nowhere against that control on either channel}: on the bursty link the largest discrepancy at any span is \nrGeJitMaxPct\% at $|t|$ of \nrGeJitMaxT{} with between \nrGeJitMinBlocks{} and \nrGeJitMaxBlocks{} of \nrGeBlocks{} blocks agreeing, which is a coin flip. So the statement is not that a randomized hold reduces the cohort. It is that a randomized hold removes it: a fleet retrying on a jittered schedule presents the console with an arrival pattern statistically indistinguishable from a fleet that never retries at all, at every span we measured and on both channels, while the same fleet on a fixed schedule presents one more than twice as crowded at the largest span.

The superlinear growth in span is the reason the recommendation matters at all: at four assets a fixed hold costs \nrGeFixLoPct\% of extra crowding and at ten it costs \nrGeFixHiPct\%, so the intervention is nearly free where the console is idle and decisive where it is not, which is the same shape as every other queue result here.

That collapses two recommendations into one mechanism. Contending senders that retransmit on a fixed schedule collide, back off identically, and collide again, which is why Ethernet randomizes its backoff rather than merely lengthening it \citep{metcalfe1976ethernet}. Section~\ref{subsec:band} borrowed that literature for the length of the hold. It turns out to govern the spread as well, and for its original reason rather than a new one. The operational statement is a single sentence: a vehicle that has gone unanswered should wait at least \qBandFloor{} steps, and should not wait exactly \qBandFloor{} steps.

\paragraph{The cheapest change in this paper is the one that cannot be bought on its own.} Every lever above is on the console. The one fleet-side lever we tested changes how long a vehicle that gave up waiting stays autonomous before asking again. It touches no console, needs no message, no field and no coordination, and is a constant in the vehicle's own software, so on cost alone it should be first on any list. Its sign is set by a console policy the vehicle cannot observe. Read on blue loss, because slowing the fleet changes how often vehicles ask and so moves the denominator of any answered-fraction metric, an eightfold hold is worth \FlBlindEight{} on the console that cannot refuse (\FlBlindEightV{}), costs \FlBoundEight{} on the console bounded at $k^{\star}$ (\FlBoundEightV{}, and it gates the wrong way at \FlBoundMoves{} of four spans), and on the admitting console does exactly \FlAdmitEight{}: \FlAdmitMoves{} of four spans move at all, because that console overrides the vehicle's hold with its own refusal hold and the knob never reaches the code that would apply it. The dose-response says the same thing with a recommendation attached. Sweeping the hold from one to sixteen, the best hold is \FlHoldBlind{} on the blind console and \FlHoldBound{} on the bounded one and \FlHoldAdmit{} on the admitting one, which is to say: back off twice on a console that is collapsing, and do not back off at all on a console that has been fixed.

The mechanism is isolated by a control rather than argued. Give the console enough service rate that it is not load-limited, and two things happen at once. The lever becomes pure cost, \FlFastCost{} of blue loss at four assets (\FlFastCostV{}), and the console policy stops mattering: blind and admitting are byte-identical there on every cell, because a console with no queue never refuses anything. So the fleet-side change buys congestion relief and nothing else, and where there is no congestion there is only its cost. The consequence for a program office is a substitution rather than a lever. Damping demand at the vehicle and refusing it at the console do the same job, and doing both is worse than doing either, which means the fleet-side change cannot be specified without naming the console it will meet. It is the cheapest item in this paper and the only one whose sign is somebody else's decision.

\paragraph{A congestion signal is worth nothing until something consumes it.} The notification analysis above has a gap that the networking literature makes obvious once the analogy is drawn. A refusal \emph{is} a congestion signal in the sense of \citet{floyd1999congestion}, and nothing in the model consumes it. A refused vehicle returns to autonomy sooner and then comes back on exactly the same standing cadence, so the signal changes one wait and never once changes the offered load. In TCP the signal changes the sender's rate \citep{jacobson1988congestion}. That is the disanalogy, and it is a missing mechanism rather than a finding.

Adding the missing half changes the answer. Let a refused vehicle halve its own request rate and add back an eighth on each answered request, floored at a quarter of the standing cadence, which is additive-increase multiplicative-decrease on the vehicle rather than on the console. At twelve assets on a clear link the operator's goodput runs \aiGoodClearBlindTwelve{} under \texttt{blind}, \aiGoodClearRefundTwelve{} under \texttt{refund}, \aiGoodClearAdmitTwelve{} under \texttt{admit}, and \aiGoodClearAimdTwelve{} when the vehicle responds. On that metric notification alone appears to cost \aiGoodClearTellTwelve{} there ($t = \aiGoodClearTellTwelveT{}$), and adding a response rule appears to be worth \aiGoodClearGainTwelve{} over \texttt{admit} ($t = \aiGoodClearGainTwelveT{}$, \aiGoodClearGainTwelveBlocks{} of \aiBlocks{} blocks). The gain is \aiGoodClearGainEight{} at eight assets and \aiGoodClearGainTen{} at ten, and on mission success it reaches \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten ($t = \aiSuccClearGainTenT{}$).

That reading is contaminated, and for this policy the direction of the contamination is knowable in advance, though it is not knowable in general: the burst interaction of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is a site where the same ratio hides an effect instead of manufacturing one. \texttt{operator\_goodput} is serviced over requested, and every policy here changes the requesting: a refusal returns a vehicle to autonomy sooner so it re-consults sooner, and a rate rule suppresses re-consultation by construction. Read on \texttt{served\_per\_step}, which has no denominator to move, the rule serves \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} at twelve assets on a clear link ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveT{}$) and \rtServedAimdOverAdmitJamTwelve{} under jamming ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitJamTwelveT{}$). The console does \rtAimdWorkVerdict{} work, not more. What collapses is the asking: requests fall \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} ($t = \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveT{}$, \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelveBlocks{} of \rtBlocks{} blocks).

The finding survives the correction and is better stated after it. Mission success under the rule still gates positive, \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten assets on a clear link and \aiSuccJamGainEight{} at eight under jamming. So the console is asked forty percent less, does a few percent less work, and wins more missions. Stating that as a throughput gain both misdescribes the mechanism and undersells it, because a lever that improves outcomes while REDUCING operator load is a different kind of recommendation from one that improves outcomes by extracting more work from the same operator. The same correction rehabilitates plain \texttt{admit}, whose goodput appears to collapse against \texttt{refund} at wide spans: it in fact serves \rtServedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} at twelve assets, ungated, while being asked \rtAskedAdmitOverRefundClearTwelve{} more often. The console was never overloaded by it. The metric was.

That leaves the rule with an outcome gain and no account of where it comes from, which is a story rather than a mechanism, so we registered one and tested it: the service the rule gives up is service that was going to be wasted. It is testable on two absolute rates measured on the same runs, and it holds by a wide margin. At twelve assets on a clear link the rule serves \abClearNTwelveSrv\% less and the console's abandonment falls \abClearNTwelveAba\% ($t = \abClearNTwelveAbaT{}$, \abClearBlocks{} of \abClearBlocks{} blocks), and abandonment falls faster than service in \abClearRelMore{} of \abClearRelCells{} spans. Priced as an exchange, at sixteen assets the rule removes \abClearRateHi{} abandoned requests for every one it costs the console in service. Under jamming the same exchange is smaller and the same way round, \abJamRelMore{} of \abJamRelCells{} spans. A congestion signal consumed by the vehicle suppresses requests that were going to expire, which is why the console can do less work and win more missions.

The registered falsifier named a second metric, \texttt{wasted\_frac}, and the gate now refuses it. Its denominator is the request count and the rule moves the request count by construction: it reads \abWfMove\% at twelve assets on a denominator that has itself moved \abWfDenom\%. The limb is unreadable rather than failed, and we say so here rather than quietly substituting the metric that carries the same question with no denominator to move.

The two console-side levers together are worth stating on the two metrics no policy can inflate, and they agree. Against a console running no policy at all, admission control plus the response rule raises mission success by \aiStackClearEight{} percentage points at eight assets on a clear link ($t = \aiStackClearEightT{}$, \aiStackClearEightBlocks{} of \aiBlocks{} blocks), \aiStackClearTen{} at ten and \aiStackClearTwelve{} at twelve, and \aiStackJamEight{}, \aiStackJamTen{} and \aiStackJamTwelve{} at the same spans under jamming. Over the identical runs it cuts the fraction of the blue force lost by \rtLossStackOverBlindClearEight{} points at eight assets ($t = \rtLossStackOverBlindClearEightT{}$), \rtLossStackOverBlindClearTen{} at ten and \rtLossStackOverBlindClearTwelve{} at twelve. The two are separate measurements rather than one restated: \texttt{success} thresholds the assault, \texttt{blue\_loss\_frac} counts the force that does not come back, and both are fractions of what was fielded rather than of what was requested, so neither can be moved by a policy that changes the asking. They peak at the same span, gate at the same spans, and are null at the same one. Two changes to software, neither adding an operator, a console, or a radio.

Below the collapse span it costs nothing, which is the property that makes it a recommendation rather than a tuning parameter. At four assets the difference from plain \texttt{admit} gates on nothing: \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearFour{} on the rate served ($t = \rtServedAimdOverAdmitClearFourT{}$), \rtLossAimdOverAdmitClearFour{} points of blue force lost ($t = \rtLossAimdOverAdmitClearFourT{}$), and \aiSuccClearGainFour{} on mission success ($t = \aiSuccClearGainFourT{}$). A vehicle that is never refused never halves anything, so a fleet operating inside its console's capacity pays nothing for carrying the rule, and that now rests on three metrics rather than on the one a policy can move. Like the backoff floor of Section~\ref{subsec:band}, and unlike a staffing decision, it needs no estimate of the span, the delivery rate, or the service rate, because the console's own refusals carry that information already. What it does need is the console to emit them, so this lever sits on top of admission control rather than beside it.

We had predicted something weaker and got it wrong in an instructive direction: that notification would become the majority share of admission control's gain once consumed. It does not, and the share statistic was the wrong instrument, because past the collapse span the unconsumed notification is negative and a ratio whose numerator changes sign cannot measure a share. The finding that replaces it is sharper. The value of telling a requester it has been refused is not small; it is negative, until the requester has a rule for what to do about it.

\subsection{When reliance closes the loop: a span at which the team has two answers}
\label{subsec:band}

Every queue result above assumes a vehicle's request cycle does not depend on whether its last request was answered. That assumption is what gives the model one threshold. It is also not obviously true of an operator-reliant vehicle, which may ask again sooner after being left unanswered than after being served, and the difference matters more than it looks: it makes offered load a function of the state the queue is already in. We add the smallest mechanism that does this. A fraction $\pi$ of abandoned requests return after a short backoff $\rho$ instead of the standing decision interval $\tau$, and everything else is unchanged.

The threshold then splits. Deep in collapse every request abandons, so the mean cycle is $P + 1 + \tau - \pi(\tau - \rho)$ and the collapsed state sustains itself above $n_c$. In the healthy state only a fraction $a_h$ abandons, so only that fraction returns early, and the healthy state survives up to $n_h$:
\begin{equation}
n_c = \frac{c\,\mu\,(P + 1 + \tau - \pi(\tau-\rho))}{d}, \qquad
n_h = \frac{c\,\mu\,(P + 1 + \tau - a_h\,\pi(\tau-\rho))}{d},
\label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
n_h - n_c = \frac{c\,\mu\,\pi\,(\tau-\rho)\,(1 - a_h)}{d}.
\label{eq:bandwidth}
\end{equation}
Between $n_c$ and $n_h$ both states are self-sustaining. The fold itself is old: a retrial queue, in which a blocked customer returns from orbit rather than leaving, is a standard object \citep{falin1990retrial}, and the same feedback makes slotted ALOHA bistable, where a collision provokes the retransmission that provokes the next collision \citep{carleial1975bistable}. What is new here is where it sits. The quantity that turns out to be bistable is span of control, which is a thing a program actually chooses, and the retry that closes the loop is a human-machine interaction rather than a protocol. Three things follow, and none of them is specific to this particular retry rule. The band is empty exactly when the return does not shorten the cycle, so any reliance loop that closes faster after a failure opens one. At the abandonment floor $a_h = 1-d$ the link cancels out of Equation~\ref{eq:bandwidth} entirely, so jamming relocates the band without widening it. And the width inverts into a design rule: holding a failed requester off for at least $\tau - 1/(c\mu\pi)$ steps, which is \qBandFloor{} at this operating point, leaves a band narrower than one vehicle, which is a band no staffing decision can land inside. The floor is a number, and a console cannot use a number: it does not know the span it will be flown at, still less the delivery rate. The rule that reaches the floor without one is older than the analysis. Doubling the hold after each unanswered request and resetting it on service is Ethernet's binary exponential backoff \citep{metcalfe1976ethernet} and the retransmit rule of TCP congestion control \citep{jacobson1988congestion}, and it pays nothing while the system is healthy because a requester that gets answered never accumulates a streak. The published caveat runs the other way and belongs here: unbounded exponential backoff is unstable in infinite-population models \citep{aldous1987instability,hastad1996backoff}. What makes it safe on a console is the bounded vehicle count and the cap at the standing decision interval, not the doubling. That rule is measured and not merely derived. Sweeping the backoff at five vehicles, the initial-condition gap runs \qFloorFour{} at a backoff of four steps and \qFloorTwelve{} at twelve, both of which the gate accepts, and falls to \qFloorSixteen{} at sixteen and \qFloorTwenty{} at twenty, neither of which it does. The predicted floor lies inside that interval.

The floor $a_h = 1-d$ is the wrong number to use on a clear link, where it is zero and Equation~\ref{eq:band} collapses back onto the single threshold. A request can also abandon because the queue was busy, and at C0 that is the only way it can. Reading the healthy abandonment off the retry-free arm instead (\qBandAbandon{} at six vehicles) puts the band at $(\qBandLo{}, \qBandHi{})$, so five vehicles sit inside it and six do not.

Whether an operator's load actually arrives in the clustered way this mechanism needs is an empirical question, and the field record says it does: across the MAGIC 2010 multi-robot reconnaissance competition the task operator averaged 4.25 interactions per minute but went as long as two minutes without interacting at all, intervening in prolonged bursts rather than in small steady adjustments \citep{olson2012magic}. That is the demand shape a fold band lives in, and it is burstier than anything this model generates: the request arrivals here are mildly \emph{under}-dispersed, because the jittered decision interval regularizes them. The band therefore appears under arrivals smoother than the ones a real operator faces, which makes the operating conditions reported below conservative rather than favorable. That is a testable prediction about initial conditions rather than about means, so we test it that way. Staggering the vehicles' first decision events is a healthy start and the synchronized default is not, and the two are compared at each span. Staggering also perturbs the random stream and has an effect of its own, so the quantity that isolates the loop is the difference of differences against the retry-free arm. It is null everywhere except at five vehicles (Figure~\ref{fig:band}a), where mission success moves by \qBandSuccess{} ($t = \qBandSuccessT{}$, 32 of 32 blocks) and operator goodput by \qBandGood{} ($t = \qBandGoodT{}$, 32 of 32). One span below it gives \qBandBelow{} and one span above it \qBandAbove{}. Filling the sweep in at full resolution leaves that reading intact: seven vehicles, a span the first sweep skipped and which had only been interpolated between six and eight, measures \qBandSeven{} on success and \qBandSevenGood{} on goodput, and nine measures \qBandNine{}. At five vehicles the model has two answers, and which one it gives depends on how the vehicles happened to start.

The operational content is in what closes the band. Freeing the slot does: the same contrast at five vehicles falls from \qBandBlind{} under \texttt{blind} to \qBandRefund{} under \texttt{refund} and \qBandAdmit{} under \texttt{admit}, a factor of forty-four and twenty. A console that discards work it cannot finish in time removes the bistability, not merely the average loss, which is a stronger reason to build admission control than the mean effect of Section~\ref{subsec:queue}. That \texttt{admit} closes the band less completely than \texttt{refund} is consistent rather than anomalous: notification returns a requester to service sooner, which raises the request rate, which is what the retry loop does. Admission control's second half is itself a small reliance loop.

The loop also decides the sign of the channel. A link that fades in bursts and a memoryless one that delivers at the same average rate are not interchangeable, and which of the two a console does better on depends on whether its clients come back. With the loop open, so that a rebuffed vehicle goes autonomous and does not re-ask, the bursty link delivers \emph{more} service at five assets: \grFlipROneOpen{}, \grFlipRTwoOpen{} and \grFlipRThreeOpen{} served per step at C1 through C3, because a request a fade destroys is a request the operator never has to work. Close the loop and all three comparisons reverse, to \grFlipROneClosed{}, \grFlipRTwoClosed{} and \grFlipRThreeClosed{}, because the vehicles that abandoned together during one fade come back together when it lifts. The difference in differences is gated at every rung, from \grFlipROneDid{} to \grFlipRTwoDid{} at $|t|$ between \grFlipMinT{} and \grFlipMaxT{} with \grFlipMinBlocks{} of \grFlipBlocks{} blocks agreeing, but the levels are the stronger statement: the same channel helps a console serving assets that give up for good and hurts one serving assets that return, so the crossing is in the delivered rate itself and not only in a contrast of contrasts. A link budget written for an autonomous fleet does not transfer to a supervised one, and the term that flips it is a human-machine interaction rather than a radio property.

\paragraph{What the recommendation costs when waiting is priced properly.} The model as published lets a vehicle whose operator link is unanswered keep driving at reduced speed, so a wait costs tempo only through the console. That is the setting in which advising a rebuffed vehicle not to re-ask is cheapest to give, and the obvious objection is that a wait ought to cost more where waiting is dangerous. We price it the other way and re-run the ladder: under \texttt{teleop\_stop} a vehicle with no answer coming back stops, which is the behavior a teleoperated platform actually has and which the published path reproduces exactly when the flag is off. The recommendation survives and roughly doubles. At six assets a four-step backoff costs \tpStopRZeroFourNSix{} points of mission success against not re-asking, which is the whole of it, where the same arm on the published physics costs \tpKeepRZeroFourNSix{} points (\tpKeepRZeroFourNSixPct\%). No retry arm gates above no-retry on either physics.

The mechanism is the interesting part, because the obvious reading of that result is wrong. Time spent stopped is the natural explanation and it is a good partial one: across the \tpLadder{}-point backoff ladder the hold fraction falls from \tpHoldHi{} to \tpHoldLong{} against \tpHoldLo{} with no retry, closing \tpHoldClosed\% of its range while mission success closes only \tpSuccClosed\% of its own, so it mediates most and not all. But on the physics the rest of this paper uses, hold fraction is identically \tpKeepHold{}, because nothing is ever held, and retrying still costs \tpKeepRZeroFourNSix{} points. Time in stall cannot be the mechanism where there is no stall.

So we tested whether the stop-dead cliff is a tempo budget, which would make it a closed form in route length, advance rate and mission clock and would owe nothing to the queue. Estimating the advance rate at one span and holding that span out of the test, the budget predicts the mission collapses at a hold fraction of \tpHStar{} everywhere. It does not. The collapse sits at \tpSepFive{} at five assets and \tpSepEight{} at eight, a shift of \tpSepShift{} against a tolerance of \tpSepTol{} declared before the run, and the direction is the one a shared server predicts: more vehicles on the console, collapse at a smaller hold. A budget in route and clock cannot know how many vehicles share an operator. The account is therefore withdrawn, and what it leaves is a stronger statement than it would have made. Two physics that disagree about what a waiting vehicle does produce the same advice for the same reason, and the reason is the queue.

Three limitations belong with this. The upper edge is not a constant: $a_h$ is itself a function of span, so Equation~\ref{eq:band} with a frozen $a_h$ brackets the edge rather than locating it, and the honest reading of $\qBandHi{}$ is an estimate at one operating point. Four vehicles sits inside the predicted band and shows nothing, which is a question about basins rather than about stability: the collapsed state is self-sustaining there, but a synchronized start is not a large enough disturbance to reach it, and we have not mapped the basin boundary. Nor does the band survive load arriving from another direction. Tying the request rate to trafficability shortens the decision interval, which lowers both edges, and the upper edge falls below the operating span first: the same contrast decays monotonically from \qBandGood{} on flat ground to \qBandGoodHard{} under the strongest terrain coupling, which is a threshold moving past a fixed span rather than a band widening around it. And $\pi$ and $\rho$ are declared rather than measured. We could find no published re-request rate for a vehicle whose request went unanswered, which is the number this section would need to become a prediction rather than a demonstration. What the field record does establish is that the retry itself is not an exotic assumption: the Demo~III experimental unmanned vehicles called for help only after several attempts to back up and replan had failed, and terrain was the typical trigger \citep{schlenoff2006rcs}, so retry-before-escalation is already how fielded ground autonomy behaves. Whether the same vehicle also retries \emph{after} escalating, and how soon, is the open measurement. Until it is made, the claim here is conditional: if a crew or a vehicle closes that loop faster than it would otherwise, a span exists at which the team's behavior is not determined by its parameters.

\subsection{Verification as signal detection, and what a checking operator costs}
\label{subsec:trust}

Everything above treats the operator as a server whose answers are right. The over-trust literature
is about the case where they are not, and it is usually posed as a dial: how often should the human
check the machine. That is the wrong shape for the question. Checking is a detection problem, so the
operator has two independent properties and only one of them is a capability. It sees a cue drawn
from $N(w d', 1)$, where $w$ is one when the automation is wrong, and it checks when the cue clears
a criterion. The discriminability $d'$ is how well it can tell a wrong answer from a right one; the
criterion is a policy, and the check RATE is that policy expressed in units a procedure can name.
A program office can buy the first and set the second, and conflating them is what makes the dial
framing mislead.

\paragraph{The instrument first.} Before any claim rests on the model, it has to recover what it was
given. Set $d'$ to \TdZeroSet{}, \TdHalfSet{}, \TdOneSet{} and \TdTwoSet{} and the run reports
\TdZeroGot{}, \TdHalfGot{}, \TdOneGot{} and \TdTwoGot{}. The separation of capability from policy is
the sharper check: holding $d'$ at one and moving only the criterion moves the fraction of answers
actually checked by \TdFracSpread{} while the recovered discriminability moves by \TdCritSpread{}.
The two knobs are measurably independent, which is what licenses treating one as a requirement on
the operator and the other as a requirement on the procedure. At $d' = 1$ the operator catches
\TdOneHit{} of wrong answers at a false-alarm rate of \TdOneFa{}; at $d' = 2$ it catches
\TdTwoHit{} at \TdTwoFa{}.

\paragraph{An operator who can be wrong is not automatically better than no operator.} Against a
supervisory force that consults nobody, an operator who never verifies is WORSE at four assets:
\ToNeverSuccLow{} mission success, \ToNeverSuccLowV{}, and it loses \ToNeverLossLow{} more of the
force (\ToNeverLossLowV{}). An operator who always verifies is better by \ToAlwaysSuccLow{}, and
that is \ToAlwaysSuccLowV{}. Across the whole span sweep and all four operator arms, the arm with an
operator loses more vehicles than the arm without one in \ToLossUpCells{} of the twenty cells. This
is not an argument against operators. It is an argument that a consulted operator has to be
verified, because the model's automation is wrong half the time by construction here and an
unverified answer is worse than the autonomy it displaces.

\paragraph{What the operator costs is the asset's time, not the console's.} The value of verifying
is large and it is spent on the wait. Removing what a waiting asset gives up, by letting it hold at
its own autonomous quality instead of stalling, takes the always-verifying operator from
\ToAlwaysSuccLow{} against no operator to \ToFreeAlwaysSuccLow{}, and that one \ToFreeAlwaysSuccLowV{}.
The same move takes losses from \ToAlwaysLossLow{} to \ToFreeAlwaysLossLow{} (\ToFreeAlwaysLossLowV{}).
Swept as a requirement rather than a contrast, the penalty for verifying shrinks monotonically as
waiting gets cheaper: always-verifying trails never-verifying by \TsLow{} when a stalled asset is
badly degraded (\TsLowV{}), by \TsMid{} in the middle (\TsMidV{}), and by \TsHigh{} when waiting is
free (\TsHighV{}). The requirement that makes verification affordable is therefore not on the
console at all. It is on what a vehicle is permitted to do while its request is outstanding.

\paragraph{The console's load decides the rate.} Sweeping the check rate against span and reporting
the TOLERANCE BAND rather than the argmax, because an argmax over a flat ladder is a coin flip
dressed as a finding, the best rate falls from \TbFourBest\% at four assets to \TbSixBest\% at six
and \TbEightBest\% at eight and above, and the band contains \TbFourWidth{}, \TbSixWidth{} and
\TbEightWidth{} of the five rates at those spans. Verifying everything is worth \TrLow{} at four
assets (\TrLowV{}, $t = \TrLowT{}$) and \TrColl{} at eight (\TrCollV{}, $t = \TrCollT{}$). The sign
turns over at exactly the span where Section~\ref{subsec:queue}'s queue collapses, and for the same
reason: a verification is a second draw on a server that has already run out. A second battery run
at the full tier on the harshest of the three prices puts the same three regimes on the record with
their gates: verifying everything is worth \DrLoadFour{} at four assets (\DrLoadFourV{}), costs
\DrLoadEight{} at eight (\DrLoadEightV{}), and at sixteen is \DrLoadSixteen{}, which is
\DrLoadSixteenV{}. The third regime is the one worth naming, because it is not a weaker version of
the second. Past the collapse span the verification policy stops mattering in either direction: the
console is so far behind that what it does with an answer is no longer what decides the mission.

\paragraph{Whether verifying pays at all is set by what a wrong answer costs, not by how often the
automation is wrong.} We registered the opposite and it did not survive. The registered claim was
that the best check rate is interior and rises with the automation's error rate, with a declared
falsifier of a tolerance band containing a corner in every cell. Across the \DrCells{} cells of
that grid, three ways of pricing a wrong answer by five error rates each, the number in which an
interior rate clears the better corner by the gate's own margin is \DrInterior{}, so the claim is
retracted rather than reworded. What the error sweep does show is a split by PRICE. When a
wrong answer leaves the requester with its problem, so that it comes back and asks again, verifying
everything costs \TpTimeLo{} at an automation error rate of 0.1 (\TpTimeLoV{}) and
gains \TpTimeHi{} at 0.9 (\TpTimeHiV{}), turning over near an error rate of
\TpTimeFlip{}. When the wrong answer is accepted and the requester stops asking, verifying
everything is a loss at every error rate tested: \TpWeakLo{} to \TpWeakHi{} in the channel where a
wrong answer merely fails to help, and \TpReflLo{} to \TpReflHi{} in the channel where it leaves the
vehicle worse off than its own autonomy. That last channel is the over-trust failure the literature
is named for, and verification does not pay there at any error rate we measured. Verification pays
when a wrong answer comes back, not when it is harmful.

\paragraph{Why not verifying wins, tested rather than asserted.} The obvious explanation for a
console that does better by shipping unverified answers is throughput: a verification costs a slot,
and a slot is what the console is short of. That explanation is cheap to state and it has a kill
test, so we ran it. If never verifying wins because a shipped answer costs only a fraction of a
slot, then charging a shipped answer a FULL slot must take the advantage away. It does. At a
compliance cost of \DrComplyLo{} of a slot, always verifying trails never verifying by
\DrComplyQuarter{} (\DrComplyQuarterV{}); at \DrComplyHi{} of a slot the sign has turned and the
gap is \DrComplyFull{}, which is \DrComplyFullV{}. The advantage is bought with console capacity
and disappears when the capacity is charged for, so the mechanism is throughput and not some hidden
property of the quality model. This is an audit rather than a finding, and it is reported because
an explanation that has not been given a chance to fail is not evidence.

\paragraph{The crossing is on the stakes.} Moving what a right answer is WORTH, by sweeping the
autonomous baseline under a fixed operator, produces the interior crossing the error sweep did not.
At the highest stakes tested, where a serviced decision is worth 0.45 of quality over autonomy,
verifying half the time gains \DrStakeHalfHi{} and verifying always gains \DrStakeFullHi{}, both
\DrStakeHiV{}, and both cut losses. Two rungs down the ladder the same policies have crossed over
and full verification costs \DrStakeFullMid{} (\DrStakeFullMidV{}), and at the lowest stakes it
costs \DrStakeFullLo{}. Half verification is the arm that survives furthest into the low-stakes
region, still \DrStakeHalfMidV{} where full verification has already gated down, which is what makes
a partial rate the robust setting near a crossing whose location a program will not know precisely.
The rule a requirement can carry is that the verification rate is set by the gap between a good
decision and autonomy, and that gap is a property of the autonomy rather than of the operator.

\subsection{Sensor swarm under electronic warfare}
\label{subsec:uc5}

UC-5 pairs a recon swarm with near-blind ground shooters against a defense in depth, then jams the link. Figure~\ref{fig:uc5} and Table~\ref{tab:uc5} give the result. With no recon the shooters cannot see the threats that out-range them and the assault fails, succeeding only \ucFiveSuccBlind\% of the time. Adding aircraft builds detection coverage with a clear threshold: coverage climbs steeply until the swarm is large enough to blanket the depth of the defense (near \ucFiveCovBestClear{} at the largest swarm in clear conditions) and mission success rises with it, to \ucFiveSuccBestClear\%. The sensor-to-shooter chain is the whole story: recon is what lets blind shooters engage, and swarm size buys the coverage that cues them.

Jamming erodes that chain. As jamming intensifies it thins the shared picture, and both coverage and success fall sharply at every swarm size, though they stay well above the no-recon baseline. At the largest swarm, success falls from \ucFiveSuccBestClear\% in clear conditions to \ucFiveSuccBestSevere\% under severe EW, and coverage from \ucFiveCovBestClear{} to \ucFiveCovBestSevere{}; a mid-sized swarm falls from \ucFiveSuccMidClear\% to \ucFiveSuccMidSevere\%, roughly halving. The design implication is that swarm size and communications resilience are complements, not substitutes: buying more aircraft raises the ceiling in clear conditions but does not protect the picture once the link is contested.

\subsection{Kill-web mechanics: suppression, munitions, and contested belief}
\label{subsec:killweb}

A review of the model raised three related points about its engagement kernel. Attrition should emerge from the stream of individual engagements rather than being posited as the primitive; each entity should carry the state that governs a modern kill web, at least suppression, a finite basic load of munitions, and a perception state; and fires should be allocated against what a shooter believes rather than against ground truth, so that jamming, spoofing, and decoys become first-order effects instead of probability-of-kill modifiers. The first point already holds: \sandtable{}'s engagement is event-based, each living shooter draws a per-shot Bernoulli kill against its nearest detected target every tick, so attrition is emergent and the Lanchester match of Section~\ref{subsec:validation} is an aggregate cross-check, not the mechanism. The other two we address by adding the missing state.

We add three mechanics, each opt-in and off by default. Suppression is a decaying state in $[0,1]$ that incoming fire raises on its target and that scales down a suppressed shooter's kill probability. Munitions are a finite basic load carried per shooter, decremented on fire, that gates engagement once a shooter runs dry. Belief replaces the single detected-or-not bit with a persistent \emph{track} per opposing entity, a believed position refreshed on detection and going stale with decaying confidence when detection lapses, plus optional false tracks for decoys and spoofing; fires resolve against the believed position and confidence, not truth. All three are byte-identical when off: each is gated so the disabled path draws the identical random stream, and every number in Sections~\ref{subsec:validation}--\ref{subsec:uc5} is unchanged. The baseline is their degenerate limit, a force with no suppression, unlimited magazines, and perfect knowledge. Figure~\ref{fig:tick} shows the per-step pipeline these mechanics plug into and the entity state they add.

\paragraph{Suppression.} Suppression makes a base of fire worth having. On UC-3, raising the
suppression strength from off to full lowers mean blue losses from \suppBaseLoss{} to \suppMaxLoss{} of eight and lifts mission success from \suppBaseSucc\% to \suppMaxSucc\% (Figure~\ref{fig:killweb}a). The attacking echelon out-guns the six defenders, so putting fire on them degrades their return fire faster than they degrade the assault, and the maneuver force crosses at lower cost. A fixed probability-of-kill table cannot express this, because the benefit is contingent on who is firing on whom rather than a property of the weapon. The off setting recovers the baseline exactly.

\paragraph{Munitions.} A finite basic load turns rate of fire into a sustainment ceiling. Sweeping
the defenders' load on UC-3 traces a monotonic curve (Figure~\ref{fig:killweb}b): with \muniScarceAmmo{} rounds each the six defenders cannot service every vehicle crossing their engagement area, so blue loses \muniScarceLoss{} platforms at \muniScarceSucc\% success, whereas at an effectively unlimited load blue loses \muniPlentyLoss{} at \muniPlentySucc\%, reproducing the fixed-Pk baseline. The fixed-Pk model is the infinite-magazine limit; between the two, the defender's magazine depth sets the attacker's loss rate, which is exactly the quantity a logistics-aware plan trades against.

\paragraph{Contested belief.} With fires resolving against tracks, deception and electronic warfare
act on the belief state directly. On UC-3, mean blue losses over \beliefNRep{} replications are \beliefBaseLoss{} for the baseline seen-bit model and \beliefCleanLoss{} for belief tracks in clear communications, so with fresh tracks the two nearly coincide. Adding decoys drops losses to \beliefDecoyLoss{}, because spoofed tracks pull fire off the real force, and severe jamming drops them to \beliefJamLoss{} by a different route, stale tracks whose believed positions drift from the moving truth. Neither effect is reachable by a modifier table, because each needs a belief that deception can corrupt, which is how engagement-level frameworks such as AFSIM allocate fires against imperfect tracks rather than truth \citep{tryhorn2021afsim}.

UC-7 combines these into one contested-belief scenario in the use-case library: eight vehicles advance against a denser, spoofable, and jammed defense, with both sides carrying the full kill web. \subsection{Emergent maneuver: personality-movement propensities}
\label{subsec:personality}

The route-choice study of Section~\ref{subsec:uc3} steers the assault force along a scripted lane set by one \texttt{route\_bias} knob. That knob names the route; it does not let the route respond to where the threat actually is. The combat-distillation models we build on take the opposite view: in ISAAC, EINSTein, and MANA a unit's next move is a weighted sum of local propensities, and the route is whatever those propensities produce \citep{ilachinski2000isaac,ilachinski2004artificial,lauren2002mana}. We make that inheritance concrete with an opt-in movement mode that replaces the scripted lane for blue ground with such a propensity sum: attraction toward the objective, repulsion from each detected enemy that falls off with distance, a pull along the local cover gradient, and a separation term that keeps the formation from stacking up. A single weight $w_e$ scales the enemy-repulsion term. The mode is off by default, and the disabled path is byte-identical to the scripted planner.

Table~\ref{tab:personality} runs the two movement models on UC-3 over \personalityNRep{} replications. With the enemy term off ($w_e{=}0$) the propensity sum is pure goal-seeking and recovers the scripted fast corridor exactly, \persStraightLoss{} losses at \persStraightSucc\% success: the reduction checks that the mode adds a behavior rather than a bias. Turning the enemy term on bends the route. At $w_e{=}1$ the force bows around the detected defenders and reaches the objective with \persAvoidLoss{} losses at \persAvoidSucc\% success in \persAvoidTime{}~s. The scripted planner has no setting that reaches that point. Its safe extreme, the covered route, also crosses at \persScriptCoverSucc\% but costs \persScriptCoverLoss{} losses and takes \persScriptCoverTime{}~s; the emergent route is as survivable and about \persTimeSavePct\% faster, because it commits to no fixed lane and instead skirts each threat as it comes into view, cutting back toward the objective once past. The route is chosen by the model, not by us.

Higher weight is not uniformly better. At $w_e{=}2$ the same force still crosses unharmed but takes \persOverTime{}~s, an emergent over-caution: the repulsion field now holds vehicles off approaches that were survivable at lower weight. The weight has an interior operating point, the kind of structure the sensitivity loop of Section~\ref{sec:results} is built to expose, reached here from local movement rules rather than a scripted route. This is one demonstration on one scenario, not a claim that emergent movement dominates a planner in general; what it shows is that \sandtable{} carries the combat-distillation movement model as a first-class, byte-identical-off option, and that on UC-3 it already finds a route the single scripted knob cannot.

\subsection{Drone-war mechanisms: EW-immune links and counter-UAS}
\label{subsec:drone}

The Russo-Ukrainian war has pushed small uncrewed aircraft and the electronic-warfare fight over their control links to the center of land combat, but its clearest lesson is that the effect is contingent: the same drone is decisive or inert depending on the countermeasures it meets, so that the game-changing effect ``depends on the game'' \citep{kunertova2023depends}, and air defense keeps pace with the drone in an enduring hider-finder competition rather than being overturned by it \citep{calcara2022drones}. Two of those countermeasures bear directly on the command and sensing axes \sandtable{} already models, so we add each as an opt-in mechanic that is byte-identical when off. The first is the answer to link jamming, of which the war shows two: shifting control onboard to autonomy so the drone completes with the radio link severed, and hardening the link itself, most visibly the fiber-optic tethers that jamming cannot touch \citep{kunertova2023boots,bondar2025autonomy}. The autonomy answer is already the supervisory mode of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece}, so here we model the other one: on that same command model we add an EW-immune command link that rides a fixed low latency off the C0--C5 ladder and ignores its drops, a fiber tether that keeps the operator in \emph{direct} control while removing the jamming penalty. The single operator remains a shared server, so span-of-control contention is untouched and only the link penalty is removed.

Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(a) contrasts the two links for direct (human-in-the-loop) control across the ladder, over \droneNRep{} replications. On the jammable link, direct control degrades from \fiberClearJam\% success in clear conditions to \fiberHeavyJam\% under heavy jamming (\fiberHeavyLabel{}), the collapse the centerpiece already documents. The EW-immune link instead holds near \fiberHeavyImm\% across the whole ladder, because jamming no longer touches the control path; its advantage is within Monte-Carlo noise when comms are clear (the fixed tether latency and unpaired seeds leave it indistinguishable from a perfect radio there) and grows to about \fiberHeavyGain{} percentage points at heavy jamming. The tether is the complement of the centerpiece's other jamming remedy: there, handing control to autonomy let the force keep functioning as the link degraded; here, an unjammable link lets it keep the human in \emph{direct} control instead. Both realize the same lesson from the war, that jamming-resistant completion is what restores effectiveness under EW; \citet{bondar2025autonomy} report the autonomy variant raising engagement success from roughly 10--20\% to 70--80\%, which we read as directional support for that lesson rather than a calibration of our direct-control rates.

The war's second lesson cuts the other way. Massed cheap drones are themselves attrited by layered air defense and counter-UAS fires, so swarm size is not a free good. The baseline UC-5 of Section~\ref{subsec:uc5} models no air defense, so recon aircraft cannot be engaged and swarm size carries no survivability cost; the unopposed response only climbs and saturates with swarm size, from \cuasOffSmallSucc\% at a single aircraft to \cuasOffLargeSucc\% at the largest, with no interior penalty for a bigger swarm. We add an opt-in counter-UAS/SHORAD layer in which red air defense engages exposed aircraft with a per-step hazard that rises with the committed swarm size, because a larger, more detectable formation is acquired and serviced faster; the hazard keys on the committed size, so it does not slacken as the swarm thins. The layer is deliberately coarse and should be read as a stand-in, not a modeled weapon system: the red units already in the scenario double as the shooters (on UC-5 those are the anti-tank teams, not a dedicated SHORAD element), an aircraft is exposed simply by coming within reach of any of them, and the per-drone hazard is set by the committed swarm size through a calibrated superlinear signature rather than by a radar, magazine, or defender-count model. Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(b) and Table~\ref{tab:drone} give the result. Under counter-UAS that climbing curve becomes an interior optimum: success peaks at \cuasPeakSize{} aircraft (\cuasOnPeakSucc\%) and falls to \cuasOnLargeSucc\% at the largest swarm, a \cuasOnDrop-point drop, because past the optimum the swarm is attrited faster than it can cue the ground force. Too few aircraft give thin coverage; too many are shot down before they deliver it. At one or two aircraft the opposed and unopposed curves differ only within Monte-Carlo noise, since the hazard is negligible for so small a force and the two runs desynchronize once counter-UAS draws its own random numbers. This is the survivability cost the earlier UC-5 result explicitly lacked, and it turns the sensor-swarm study from an upper bound into a tradeoff.

That optimum has a cost dimension, which is the third mechanic. Recon drones are cheap and attritable, a signature economic fact of the war \citep{kunertova2023boots}, so losing many is tolerable in isolation; what makes an oversized swarm expensive is the mission it fails. We attach a cost-exchange KPI, the stipulated value of red destroyed divided by the blue platform-cost lost (a recon drone costed at 1, an assault vehicle at 20, a destroyed defender valued at 15, illustrative relative units, not measured prices), reported on the same swarm sweep so that a value above one means the exchange favored blue. With the default unit costs this KPI reduces exactly to the loss-exchange ratio, so the weights above are a study-level stipulation, and the series in Figure~\ref{fig:drone}(b) and Table~\ref{tab:drone} is the one under counter-UAS; air defense is a cost the unopposed force never pays, so for oversized swarms the unopposed exchange sits higher, and the interior peak describes the shape of the opposed series rather than any gain from counter-UAS. The cost-exchange peaks at \cuasCostPeakSize{} aircraft (\cuasCostPeak{}) and collapses to \cuasCostLarge{} for the largest swarm: the oversized swarm not only bleeds \cuasLargeAirLoss{} drones but, by failing to cue the assault, spends the expensive ground force for no gain. Cheap mass pays only up to the size the defense can be made to over-commit against; past it, more drones buy a negative return. Both mechanics are opt-in and byte-identical off: the jammable-link rows reproduce the command model of Section~\ref{subsec:centerpiece} and the counter-UAS-off rows reproduce the unopposed swarm of Section~\ref{subsec:uc5}, exactly.

\subsection{Bayesian test and evaluation of a design}
\label{subsec:bayeste}

Each study above returns a mission-success rate per design, but a test-and-evaluation decision needs more than a point estimate: the uncertainty on that rate, the probability a requirement is met, and a rule for when enough has been tested. That is the Bayesian Decision Theory paradigm for test and evaluation, which keeps a posterior over a system's performance and uses a utility to weigh information against the cost of testing \citep{ferry2023edou,ferry2024paradigm}. \sandtable{}'s per-replication binary outcomes are exactly what the paradigm consumes, with the simulator standing in as the likelihood, so we demonstrate it on the centerpiece scenario with no new mechanics (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}, Table~\ref{tab:bayeste}).

\paragraph{Credible intervals over point estimates.} A design's success draws give a conjugate Beta
posterior on its success probability, starting from a neutral Jeffreys $\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ prior. For a direct-control design at span 1:2 over \bteN{} replications (\bteHits{} successes) the posterior 95\% credible interval is [\bteBetaLo{}, \bteBetaHi{}] and the probability the design clears an 80\% requirement is \bteComplyEighty{}, a statement a design gate can act on rather than a $p$-value. The frequentist Wald interval nearly coincides at this sample size, but it misbehaves where decisions are often made, on little data: from the first \bteSmallN{} replications (\bteSmallHits{} successes) its upper limit is \bteWaldHiSmall{}, above one, while the Beta interval stays inside the unit interval at \bteBetaHiSmall{}.

\paragraph{A decision chart and when to stop.} Bayesian Decision Theory turns that posterior into an
accept, reject, or keep-testing rule. We set a compliance gate at 60\% mission success (below the 80\% bar used above only to illustrate the compliance probability) and a utility that scores a confident accept at $+20$, a mistaken accept at $-10$, and a reject at $0$, where ``confident'' means the posterior places at least 90\% of its mass above the gate; each trial then costs a fixed amount. A backward recursion labels every (successes, trials) state (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}a). Run against the sim at a per-trial cost of $0.02$, the rule accepts the compliant design (success rate $0.80$) after \bteStopAccept{} replications, rejects a clearly failing one ($0.05$) after \bteStopReject{}, and spends the most evidence, \bteStopBorder{}, on the design nearest the gate ($0.45$), against the \bteN{} replications a fixed-budget study spends on every design. Raising the per-trial cost from $0.02$ to $0.20$ shrinks the keep-testing region, from \bteContinueCheap{} states to \bteContinueDear{}, so the rule stops sooner, the cost-aware behavior a test planner wants.

\paragraph{Comparing two designs.} The same posterior compares designs. At the high-span crossover
the supervisory control mode beats direct with posterior probability \bteProbSupBetter{}, a success-rate advantage of \bteContrastMean{} (95\% CrI [\bteContrastLo{}, \bteContrastHi{}]). One caveat we report rather than bury: common random numbers, which should sharpen a paired comparison, buy almost nothing here (paired outcome correlation \bteCrnCorr{}), because \sandtable{}'s single per-run random stream aligns two designs' initial conditions but desynchronizes once their trajectories diverge; realizing the variance reduction would need the stream split into per-purpose substreams.

\paragraph{The fast model as a prior for the slow one.} The paradigm's most useful move for a
two-tier program is to treat cheap model-and-simulation output as a discounted prior that sparse, expensive high-fidelity runs then update \citep{ferry2024adaptive}. We weight \sandtable{}'s \bteSimN{}-run estimate for the compliant design at \bteLam{} of its nominal sample and confirm it against an emulated high-fidelity truth, a bias-perturbed \sandtable{} standing in for a real high-fidelity model. The informed test reaches a confident compliance decision in about \bteRunsSaved{} fewer expensive runs than testing from scratch (Figure~\ref{fig:bayeste}b), because the prior starts the posterior already tight. The discount is the guardrail against a wrong prior: when the emulated truth instead sits below the gate (\bteConflictP{}), overturning the optimistic prior to a confident reject takes about \bteOverrideMain{} high-fidelity runs at \bteLam{} but only about \bteOverrideHalf{} at the lighter discount \bteLamHalf{}, so trusting the cheap model less overturns it sooner. This is model-and-simulation-informed operational testing, and it is the quantitative form of the claim that the fast tier feeds, not replaces, the slow one.

We stipulate the utilities here (mission value against per-run cost) rather than eliciting them from stakeholders, which the source work flags as the hard practical step, and framing \sandtable{} as the system under test is a stand-in for a real one. The discounted-prior demonstration is precisely what keeps that honest: the model informs the prior, it is not the truth.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three of this paper's findings are levers rather than observations, and they share a property worth stating before the rest of the discussion qualifies them: each is a change to software, none adds an operator, a console, or a radio, and all three act on the same mechanism. A console that refuses work it cannot finish in time takes mission success from \qBlindClear\% to \qAdmitClear\% at the centerpiece operating point, and the same discard removes the bistable span outright, taking the initial-condition gap from \qBandBlind{} to \qBandRefund{}. A vehicle that halves its own request rate when refused and eases back up when served cuts the console's offered load \rtAskedAimdOverAdmitClearTwelve{} at twelve assets over admission control alone while raising mission success \aiSuccClearGainTen{} at ten, and at four assets, where nothing is being refused, moves neither the rate served, the force lost, nor mission success by a gated margin. And where the console cannot be changed at all, doubling the hold after each unanswered request reaches the band-closing floor from the vehicle side.

A program office does not buy software in units of served requests per step, it buys it against people, so the equivalence is worth stating in consoles. Reading the same span sweep with one, two and three consoles alongside the software arms, and on the two metrics no policy inflates, admission control on a single console is worth \seClearSrvNEightShare\% of a second operator in delivered service at eight assets and \seClearSucNEightShare\% of one in mission success; at twelve the two figures are \seClearSrvNTwelveShare\% and \seClearSucNTwelveShare\%. The gap between the two percentages is the finding rather than either number: the software is worth less in throughput than in outcomes, because what it removes is service that was going to be discarded, and a program staffing consoles is buying the second. Stacking the vehicle-side rate rule on top closes the rest. At ten and twelve assets one console running both is not distinguishable from two consoles running neither (\seClearStackNTen{} and \seClearStackNTwelve{} points of mission success, $t = \seClearStackNTenT{}$ and $\seClearStackNTwelveT{}$, both null at \seClearBlocks{} blocks). A null is not evidence of equality, and in a simulator it is worse than that: failing to separate two arms gets easier the fewer replications we choose to run, so the claim would strengthen with carelessness. The affirmative form runs the other way, so we test it directly. Against the same margin the gate uses to call a difference material, which was fixed before any of these contrasts were run and is \seClearStackNTwelveMarg{} points of mission success here, both one-sided tests reject at every span measured: the two configurations are \seClearStackNTenEq{} at ten assets ($t = \seClearStackNTenEqT{}$ on the weaker side), \seClearStackNTwelveEq{} at twelve ($\seClearStackNTwelveEqT{}$) and \seClearStackNSixteenEq{} at sixteen ($\seClearStackNSixteenEqT{}$), and the same holds under jamming (\seJamStackNTenEqT{}, \seJamStackNTwelveEqT{}, \seJamStackNSixteenEqT{}). At sixteen the claim is stronger than equivalence and should be stated as the stronger thing: the single console with software is ahead by \seClearStackNSixteen{} points ($t = \seClearStackNSixteenT{}$), a lead too small for this paper to call material but not in any sense a deficit. That equivalence is on decisions delivered and on missions won, and it does not extend to what a requester waits, which we checked because two queues can match on throughput and differ entirely in their tails. On the 95th percentile of the request wait, the single console is EQUIVALENT to two at twelve and sixteen assets on both rungs, and is gated LONGER at ten: \seClearTailNTenOne{} steps against \seClearTailNTenTwo{} on a clear link and \seJamTailNTenOne{} against \seJamTailNTenTwo{} at C3. The magnitude matters more than the gate, because the scale is the requester's own patience of \qDeadlineP{} steps: with two consoles the ninety-fifth-percentile requester at ten assets still has slack, and with one it is at the edge of giving up. Two of the six registered cells failed, which is what its falsifier said would narrow the claim, so the claim is narrowed. The sentence a budget can act on is that at the span where the queue collapses, the two software levers together are worth about one operator in decisions delivered and in missions won, and are not worth one operator in how long the unluckiest twentieth of requests waits.

The second and third share the property that makes them deployable, and it is the same property that makes exponential backoff deployable in a network: neither needs to know the span it will be flown at, the delivery rate, or the service rate, because the console's own refusals and silences carry that information already. A staffing decision needs all three estimated in advance and is wrong whenever the estimate is.

The first of the two has an interaction worth reporting separately, because it runs the opposite way from how a mitigation usually behaves: admission control is worth more, not less, as the link gets worse. Read on the rate served, refunding is worth \grNEightBern{} served per step at eight assets on a memoryless link and \grNEightGe{} on a link that fades in bursts of the same average delivery, a difference of \grNEightDid{} ($t = \grNEightDidT{}$, \grBlocks{} of \grBlocks{} blocks); at ten assets the gap is \grNTenDid{} ($t = \grNTenDidT{}$), and it gates at \grGated{} of the \grSpans{} spans swept. Both channels deliver at the same rate by construction, so what separates them is the arrival pattern: a fading link hands the console a cluster at each recovery edge, the back of the cluster waits past its requester's patience, and a console that cannot detect abandonment serves it anyway. This site is also where the denominator bias reverses direction. The bias is computable for any one contrast and constant in neither size nor sign across contrasts, which is why the fix has to be a rule and not a habit of checking: refunding raises the asking \grNTenGeAsk\% on the bursty link against \grNTenBernAsk\% on the memoryless one, so the ratio divides the larger gain by the larger denominator and \emph{understates} the effect this paper claims, where at the two sites of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} it inflated one. On mission success the same difference is smaller and gates only at the span where the queue is already collapsing (\buDidSuccEight{}, $t = \buDidSuccEightT{}$ at eight assets), so the mission-level consequence is established at one span rather than across the sweep. The location of that span is itself a prediction rather than an embarrassment: a throughput gain converts into a mission gain only where the operating point sits near the threshold, and eight assets is where the bursty channel's shifted $n^{\star}$ falls, while at ten both channels are already past their own. We have two spans in the success sweep and cannot separate that account from a noise floor, so it is stated as the reading we would test rather than as one we have tested.

The route, control, and swarm studies make the same point in different settings: the best design is not fixed but \emph{contingent} on the contested-conditions axis, and \sandtable{} locates where it flips. Route choice prices tempo against survivability so a planner can pick a point on the frontier to match the mission. The centerpiece turns span of control and communications state into a single rule: hand control to autonomy sooner as either span or degradation grows, and the phase diagram says exactly when. The swarm study shows that sensing capacity and link resilience must be bought together, because EW attacks the chain that connects them. None of these is a claim about a single design; each shows how the right choice shifts as conditions change, which is the question a mission engineer actually has to answer. The drone-war mechanics of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} carry the same contingency into the countermeasure environment: the EW-immune link changes the outcome only once the link is contested, and the recon swarm's payoff turns from monotone to interior only once an air defense can engage it, so a drone's value is set by the countermeasures it meets rather than fixed \citep{kunertova2023depends}.

The queue result of Section~\ref{subsec:queue} changes what the centerpiece recommends. Read as attention dilution, the moving optimum says to buy autonomy, which is new capability on the platform. Read as congestion produced by undetected abandonment, it says a cheaper fix comes first: a console that does not spend service time on requests whose answers will arrive after the requester has given up. At the centerpiece operating point that is worth \qAdmitClear\% against \qBlindClear\% mission success, and under teleoperation it is the difference between a system that works and one that does not (\qTeleAdmitClear\% against \qTeleBlindClear\%). The decomposition matters for what to build. Almost all of it, \qRefundShare\% at the published operating point, comes from discarding the expired work rather than from telling the requester about it, and the notification earns its keep only when tasking is fast enough that an asset's wasted patience is a large share of its duty cycle. So the requirement is a discard rule and request timestamps, which is doctrine and software; the notification is a second increment, justified by tempo rather than by the headline. The guidance is therefore ordered: fix the admission policy, then give the rebuffed asset a randomized hold rather than a fixed one, then staff the consoles to the collapse span of Equation~\ref{eq:nstar}, and only then buy autonomy. The second of those is the cheapest item on the list and also the smallest, and both halves of that matter to a program office. It is a constant and a random draw on the asset side with no console change at all; it is worth \jcSuccBest{} points of mission success at the span where the queue sits near its threshold and nothing measurable at the console (Section~\ref{subsec:band}). It is placed second because it is nearly free rather than because it is large. Ordering does not belong on that list either, but for a reason we can only state about this model. Prioritizing requests by how difficult the ground under the requester is, against a randomized-order control and an inverted-priority control, moves mission success by \qTriageSpread{} percentage points at the span and terrain coupling where the effect would be largest. An earlier draft explained that null with an argument from queueing, that reordering clients who all carry the same patience changes which vehicle is helped and never how many. The argument is wrong, and it is worth saying why, because the wrong version is the intuitive one. Under a common deadline first-come-first-served already \emph{is} earliest-deadline-first, which is the order that maximizes the number of deadlines met, so a worse order does not merely reshuffle the beneficiaries: it strands earlier arrivals past their deadlines and serves fewer. Ordering can change how many, downward. The real reason the null is a null is a property of the simulator rather than of queues, and it is reported in Section~\ref{subsec:ledger} as an instrument: this operator commits a request to a service slot at arrival rather than holding a backlog to choose from, so a discipline can only permute requests that arrive on the same step, and it never sees a queue to order. That is measurable, not arguable. In the regime where same-step contention is common the console is already at capacity, and in the regime where it has capacity to spare, contention occurs in under an eighth of busy steps. Recording that as an obligation rather than a finding is what produced the console that does hold a backlog, and on that console ordering is a large lever: working the backlog newest-first is worth \LvLifoGain{} served per step over oldest-first at sixteen assets, which is \emph{most} of what refusal buys. So the corrected statement is that ordering was untestable on the published console and is a first-order lever on a queueing one, and the reason it does not enter the guidance above is different and better: given the bound, it is a substitute rather than an addition, and it is the one of the three that a program office cannot buy with an integer. The same reading changes what an EW dial means. Because loss relieves an unaware server while latency never does, a single ladder that raises both cannot say which one an adversary is actually buying, and the two need separate treatment in requirements and in test design. Finally, it puts a caution on fan-out. Fan-out is the standard number for sizing an operator's span, and across the interval where this model's delivery fails it barely moves; a capacity number computed from neglect and interaction times says nothing about whether help arrives.

Methodologically, the results argue for the two-tier workflow. \sandtable{} ran 44,100 replications in one to two core-hours and returned smooth, well-ordered response surfaces with tight confidence intervals, precisely the regions and boundaries a high-fidelity ProjectGL study should then confirm. The fast tier is not a replacement for fidelity; it is what makes fidelity affordable, by spending the expensive engine only on the designs the cheap one has flagged.


\sandtable{} is deliberately low-fidelity, and its conclusions inherit that. Movement is 2.5D kinematic, not physics-based; sensing and engagement are effect-based draws, not signal or signature models; terrain is a scalar trafficability-and-cover field, not a rendered environment; and line of sight is a flat-terrain stub. Tactical behavior is simplified too: red forces are static defenders that fire but neither maneuver nor react, so every finding is conditioned on a non-adaptive threat. The baseline scenarios model no air defense against the recon aircraft, so in them swarm size carries no survivability cost and the UC-5 upside is an upper bound; the opt-in counter-UAS layer of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} supplies that cost and the interior swarm optimum it implies, but its air defense is itself static, an addressed limit rather than a fully lifted one. Because that layer keys attrition to swarm size rather than to a finite-magazine defense, the interior optimum it produces should be read as a demonstrated mechanism, not a robust prediction of an optimal swarm size: a saturable defense that a large enough swarm can overwhelm would push the balance back toward mass, and which effect dominates in a given engagement is exactly the kind of question the high-fidelity tier exists to settle.

The bistability of Section~\ref{subsec:band} sharpens the same recommendation and adds a
second, cheaper one. A console that discards work it cannot finish does not merely raise the average;
it removes a span at which the team has two possible outcomes and the parameters do not say which,
which is the difference between a system that performs worse and a system that is not predictable
from its design. Where that console cannot be built, the backoff floor is a software change on the
vehicle rather than a change to the operator's station: hold a requester that went unanswered off
for at least $\tau - 1/(c\mu\pi)$ steps, \qBandFloor{} here, and the band narrows below one vehicle.
On difficult ground, where the model ties call-for-help rate to trafficability, lengthening the backoff past that floor cuts the terrain penalty from \qDefileShort{} to \qDefileLong{}, and it does so for a reason the band explains: a longer backoff shortens the request cycle less, which raises both edges, which puts the healthy state back above the operating span on ground that had removed it. Neither
recommendation asks for more operators. Adding a console scales both edges of the band by $c$, which
relocates the problem at the cost of people; the discipline removes it at the cost of software.

The admission-control recommendation is also narrower than it may read. It says the operator should refuse work it cannot finish in time, not that it should be clever about what it accepts. We tested the obvious next step, prioritizing by which requester needs help most, and found nothing, but the test was run on an operator supervising one formation on one route, where the vehicles stand on nearly the same ground by construction: the terrain difficulty spread across the supervised vehicles is \qTriageSeen{} on average against \qTriageMap{} available across the map, because the terrain's variation is almost entirely lateral and a formation samples one lane of it. Whether ordering pays for an operator whose clients are in genuinely different situations, on different routes or against different threats, is untested here and is a property of the command architecture rather than of the queue.

A saturating operator changes the magnitudes without changing any of the conclusions. Letting answer quality fall toward the autonomous baseline in proportion to console utilization, across five levels from the shipped oracle to a fully drooped operator, reverses none of the \drChecked{} orderings checked and moves at least \drBlocks{} of \drBlocks{} blocks in each. What it does move is size: the policy levers' success advantages shrink by roughly a fifth, while supervisory control's advantage over direct \emph{grows} by roughly a quarter, because a drooping operator penalizes the mode that consults it more often. The oracle assumption is therefore conservative for the paper's headline and optimistic for its policy magnitudes, which is the direction worth knowing.

Two of the queue findings are conditioned on choices worth naming. Abandonment semantics are a modeling choice rather than an observation: \texttt{blind}, \texttt{refund}, and \texttt{admit} bracket what a console might plausibly do, \texttt{blind} is the pessimistic end and the behavior of the model as first published, and which of the three a fielded system resembles is an empirical question this model cannot settle. Second, the delivered-vehicle counts that make the collapse look like a throughput ceiling are specific to the \qDeadline{}~s mission window. At \qLongWindow{}~s the same blind configuration delivers \qArrivedTwelveLong{} vehicles at twelve assets rather than \qArrivedTwelve{}, and delivery is again monotone in span. What the queue imposes is a tempo penalty; the deadline is what converts tempo into failure. Success rates under the published window are joint statements about the queue and the clock, and the window belongs with the number whenever it is quoted.

\paragraph{Which results are console properties and which are ground properties.} A mission-level
model is worth more if it can say which of its own findings depend on the map, so we replicated the
console levers on three terrain profiles built to disagree: a lateral band of cover, a transverse
ridge with a single gap, and open ground with no cover at all. Two classes come apart cleanly. The
console-level results are map-invariant to three decimals: first-in, first-out exclusion at sixteen
assets reads \FmBandFifo{}, \FmChokeFifo{} and \FmOpenFifo{} across the three, with the ordering of
all five levers and the span at which exclusion begins identical on every map. The mission-level
results are not. The same admission lever, read on the console, is worth \MpBandSrvFull{},
\MpChokeSrvFull{} and \MpOpenSrvFull{} served per step on the three maps, a spread of
\MpSrvSpread{} against a materiality floor of \MpMPE{}. Read on the mission it is worth
\MpBandSucFull{}, \MpChokeSucFull{} and \MpOpenSucFull{} points of success on the same three runs,
a spread of more than eleven points, and the reason is that the blind console already succeeds
\MpBandBlindFull\% of the time on the banded map and \MpOpenBlindFull\% on the open one, so on
the kind map there was almost nothing left for the fix to win. A study that measures only the
mission metric, on a map where the mission succeeds anyway, will report that a real fix does not
matter. The practical rule is that anything measured on the
operator's queue transfers, and anything measured on the objective travels with its map. That
boundary is the one a program can act on: a console requirement written from this work does not need
to be requalified for new ground, and a tactic does.

The reason the boundary falls there is visible in the mechanism. Every console-level result in this
paper reduces to how a capacity-limited server treats requests it cannot all satisfy, and terrain
enters that only through the rate at which requests arrive, which the span sweep already varies
directly. The mission-level results add the step from service to outcome, and that step is where
cover, chokepoints, and the geometry of the objective live. So the invariance is not an empirical
accident to be confirmed on a fourth map. It is a statement about which of the model's layers a
finding was produced in, and it is checkable for any new finding by asking which layer its metric
belongs to before running the replication at all.

The model's parameters are set, not measured. The command model is a capacity-limited-server abstraction of operator attention, calibrated to the qualitative findings of the supervisory-control literature rather than to human-subject data; and the lethality probabilities and the C0--C5 latency and drop values are hand-set, so they, not measured link or weapon data, fix where the crossover falls and how fast the sensor-to-shooter chain degrades. The studies also use fixed heuristic behaviors rather than learned or reinforcement-learning policies, a single operator with no handoff or dynamic span reallocation, and three scenarios with small teams; extrapolation to larger forces, multi-operator command, or adaptive policies is untested.

\paragraph{What this model can inherit from a controls result, and what it cannot.} One boundary is
worth reporting with a number rather than as a caveat, because the temptation to cross it is
specific and the literature invites it. Convoy and formation control optimise PATH ERROR, and the
sharpest empirical finding available to this project reports large reductions in it for a
behaviour-based follower under jamming \citep{cheung2022convoy}. Carrying that to mission outcome
requires station-keeping error to predict mission outcome, and in this model it does not. The
counterexample sits inside a single configuration. Disturbing a follower's ESTIMATE of where its
station is, and MOVING where the station is, produce the same metric on the same arm: at
\FoDisEstErr{}~m of mean station error from a stale estimate the mission succeeds \FoDisEstSuc{} of
the time, and at \FoDisCmdErr{}~m from a commanded spread it succeeds \FoDisCmdSuc{}. The larger
error is the harmless one at the other end too, \FoDisEstBigErr{}~m scoring \FoDisEstBigSuc{}
against \FoDisCmdBigErr{}~m at \FoDisCmdBigSuc{}. A commanded spread is a column designed to be
strung out, so the force arrives piecemeal and fights alone; an estimator error is a vehicle chasing
a wrong point along a trajectory that still tracks the route and closes when the track refreshes.
Mean position error is the same number for both and describes one of them.

\paragraph{Which reading of the same positions does predict it.} Reporting that a metric fails is
only half of a recommendation, so we drove both disturbance families across one grid and scored four
readings of the identical position data: the scalar distance from each vehicle to its commanded
station, its cross-track component, the RMS distance of the living assault force from its own
centroid, and the cross-track component of that. Ten arms, of which mission success separates
\DsLive{} from \DsDead{} at the gate, giving \DsPairs{} pairs a reading is entitled to be scored on.
The scalar orders \DsScalarInv{} of those \DsPairs{} pairs backwards, and the failure is visible in
two arms: a displaced formation reads \DsOutSc{}~m and succeeds \DsOutSuc\% of the time, while a
dispersed one reads \DsSpSc{}~m, less, and succeeds \DsSpSuc\%. The cross-track component, which was
our registered candidate on the reasoning that displacement along the axis of advance is harmless,
improves the ordering to \DsCrossInv{} inversions but still admits no separating threshold at all:
the live and dead arms interleave on it, with a formation reading \DsMidCross{}~m of cross-track
error scoring \DsMidSuc\% against the base formation's \DsBaseSuc\%.

\paragraph{And a reading from outside geometry beats all of them, for the price of one assumed number.} The four readings above are all distances. The resilient-consensus literature scores the same positions as a graph instead, and it supplies two quantities with established meanings: algebraic connectivity, the second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue, and exact vertex connectivity $\kappa$, which is the quantity the $2f+1$ tolerance result is stated in \citep{sundaram2011distributed,leblanc2013resilient}. We compute both on the disc graph of the living force, together with the faults it could tolerate, $\lfloor(\kappa-1)/2\rfloor$, evaluated per sampled step and then averaged. Scored by rank correlation against mission success over the \GrCells{}-cell design grid, restricted to the \GrCellsUsed{} cells where success is off its bounds and can order anything at all, the tolerated-fault count leads at $\rho = \GrBestRho{}$ against the best reading not referenced to the commanded station, \GrGeoBest{} at \GrGeoBestRho{}, and against the RMS spread this section recommended, at \GrWorstRho{}. Every reading carries the sign it was predicted to carry. The two station-referenced readings score higher than the geometric ones here and are excluded from that comparison rather than beaten by it, because the treatment on this grid IS the commanded geometry, which is the reference-set-by-treatment failure of Section~\ref{subsec:ledger} arriving in the one place it would look like a result.

Two objections have answers on the same runs. The first is that the fault count is a monotone function of connectivity and so cannot know more than it does. It is not: the floor is applied per sampled step and then averaged, so the mean of the floor is not the floor of the mean, and the two rank this grid at $\rho = \GrFaultsKappaRho{}$ rather than at one. The count answers how often the formation could survive a fault; the mean connectivity answers how connected it was. The second objection is the real cost. A graph reading needs a peer-link range assumed before it can be computed and a distance needs nothing, so the reading could be an artifact of a number we chose. Sweeping that range from \GrRangeLo{} to \GrRangeHi{}~m moves the readings themselves by an order of magnitude and moves what they RANK by very little: the fault count reproduces its own ordering of the arms at $\rho$ between \GrRangeWorst{} and \GrRangeBest{} across the whole sweep. The control that makes that readable is that mission success is identical to \GrRangeInert{} across the same sweep, because the assumed range is an observer's parameter and touches nothing in the simulation. So the price of the better reading is a number that has to be declared and does not have to be right.
Both readings that ignore the commanded formation separate the two sets cleanly, at
\DsSpreadInv{} and \DsFCrossInv{} inversions. They are then told apart by a family neither was
scored on. Sweeping the route bias moves the whole force off the line it was commanded to hold
without changing its internal geometry, which is precisely the nuisance a formation requirement must
not fire on. Against its own threshold of \DsFCrossThresh{}~m, fleet cross-track misclassifies
\DbFCrossMiss{} of \DbFCrossCells{} cells: a force strung out ALONG its axis of advance reads
\DbFCrossWideLo{}~m, comfortably inside the line, while its mission success in that cell is the worst
in the section. Undecomposed fleet dispersion misclassifies \DbSpreadMiss{}, with the tight
formation never exceeding \DbSpreadTightHi{}~m and the dispersed one never falling below
\DbSpreadWideLo{}~m. The reading to specify is therefore the RMS distance of the living assault force
from its own centroid, held under roughly \DsSpreadThresh{}~m at this column length, and it should
not be decomposed: each of the two decompositions tested here has a blind family the whole distance
does not. It also needs no knowledge of the intended geometry, which is what makes it writable into
a specification and recoverable from bare position tracks.

The single-span limit that number carried has since been paid off, and paying it moved the number
and added a condition. Re-running the same arms across \DkSpreadSpans{} column lengths, with the
recommended reading logged this time, the two station-referenced readings admit a separating
threshold at \DkScalarHeld{} and \DkCrossHeld{} of those column lengths: their live and dead arms
interleave almost everywhere, so neither is thresholdable at all once the column can change length.
Both readings that ignore the commanded formation hold at \DkSpreadHeld{} of \DkSpreadSpans{}. The
threshold that serves every column length is the intersection of the per-span admissible intervals
rather than the average of their midpoints, and for undecomposed dispersion that intersection is
\DkSpreadLo{} to \DkSpreadHi{}~m. The single-span figure of \DsSpreadThresh{}~m sits below it: at
sixteen assets the widest formation that still succeeds reads \DkWidestAlive{}~m, so a line drawn at
\DsSpreadThresh{} would forbid a formation that in fact arrives. The number to specify is
\DkSpreadThresh{}~m. Fleet cross-track also holds at every column length but on a window of
\DkFCrossLo{} to \DkFCrossHi{}~m, an order of magnitude tighter in relative terms, which is a second
reason to prefer the undecomposed reading over a specification that has to be met to within two
metres.

\paragraph{The requirement binds on long columns and on the covered route, not everywhere.} Running
the nuisance family at every column length turned a robustness check into a condition on the
recommendation. Route bias moves a force off the line it was commanded to hold without changing its
internal geometry, and the reading is properly blind to it: the tight formation never exceeds
\DkTightFourHi{}~m at four assets or \DkTightSixHi{}~m at sixteen, and the dispersed one never falls
below \DkWideFourLo{}~m and \DkWideSixLo{}~m. What is not invariant is the penalty the threshold is
there to prevent. On the fully cover-biased route at four assets, a commanded spread of 300~m costs
\DkCostFour{} in mission success (\DkCostFourV{}, $t = \DkCostFourT{}$, \DkCostFourBlocks{} of
\DkBlocks{} blocks): \DkTightFourCover\% against \DkWideFourCover\%, which is nothing. On the same
route at sixteen assets the same spread costs \DkCostSix{} (\DkCostSixV{}, all \DkBlocks{} blocks),
taking \DkTightSixCover\% to \DkWideSixCover\%. The dispersed sixteen-vehicle column does better on
the exposed route (\DkWideSixFlat\%) than on the covered one, which the tight column of the same
length does not, and that reversal is the requirement's mechanism showing through: a covered route is
a band of finite width, and a formation wider than the band cannot use it. Dispersion tolerance is
therefore a function of how long the column is and of whether the force intends to use terrain at
all. A short column that plans to run the corridor has no dispersion requirement worth writing.

One limit remains. The mechanism we registered for the penalty is not established. If a
dispersed force were punished because terrain funnels it, then removing the terrain should remove
most of the penalty; on a map with no cover at all the penalty is \DcWideRatio{} times the banded
map's on absolute success and larger still relative to what there was to lose there
(\DcWideRelOpen\% against \DcWideRelBand\%), against a registered falsifier of \DcNeeded{}. Dispersion
costs what it costs for reasons this study has not isolated, so the correlation may be specified and
the explanation may not.

The same warning arrives from the channel end of the stack, and there it involves three metrics
rather than two. Holding the mean drop rate fixed and changing only its arrival structure, from
independent losses to a Gilbert-Elliot chain with a long bad run, moves mean track error by a factor
of \FoBurstRatio{} at the worst rung, from \FoBurstTrkIid{}~m to \FoBurstTrkGe{}~m. Packet delivery
ratio, which is the statistic a link specification is written in, is identical across that pair by
construction. Mission success does not move either: all \FoBurstCells{} contrasts against the
independent channel are equivalent, the weakest carrying a one-sided statistic of \FoBurstTost{}.
So the channel metric cannot see the structure, the tracking metric reads it two orders of magnitude
loud, and the mission reads neither. The three are not measuring the same thing badly. They are
referenced to three different things: the packet stream, the commanded station, and the objective.

The consequence for the follow stack is that its channel is mission-immaterial here and its one
requirement is a scalar. Across the whole communications ladder, the two-by-two of refresh channel
against column topology is statistically equivalent to a link-following star in all \FoCells{}
contrasts, at station errors of \FoStErrClear{} to \FoStErrSevere{}~m; the weakest of those
equivalences carries a one-sided statistic of 1.73 against a threshold of 1.70, so the finding is
affirmative but not comfortably so. Within that band the on-platform follower's point estimate is
\FoGapClear{} on a clear link and \FoGapSevere{} at the worst rung, which is the opposite of the
sign the path-error literature reports; signs inside an equivalence band are noise and we draw
nothing from it beyond the failure to reproduce. What does matter is the platform's willingness to
stop trusting a stale track. Forcing the track age up directly, so that no channel model stands
between the disturbance and the measurement, mission success moves \FoCutSpread{} across a
sixteen-fold range of staleness while the cut-off is in place, and the fleet spends up to
\FoLostWorst\% of its time navigating by waypoint rather than following. Remove the cut-off and a
five-minute outage costs \FoCutGain{} of mission success ($t = \FoCutT{}$). The cut-off is inert
under every channel this study models and decisive against a severed link, which makes it a
zero-premium insurance scalar rather than a parameter to tune: sweeping its value is noise under
both the burstiest channel here and the forced disturbance.

That inertness is the one claim in this section that rests on a disturbance no channel produces, so
it was tested at \FoBrBlocks{} blocks rather than the standard tier. Under a Gilbert-Elliot channel
the cut-off is live: it hands \FoBrLost\% of the fleet's follower-time back to waypoint navigation,
in every one of those blocks. It is also worth \FoBrGap{} of mission success, equivalent to zero at
a one-sided statistic of \FoBrTost{}. The control is exact: under the independent-loss ladder, where
no bad run reaches the cut-off's age, the two arms are bit-identical at \FoBrCtrl{} in every block,
so the mechanism fires on track age and on nothing else. The forced sweep does not, however, forecast
the channel. Its own arithmetic predicted a gap an order of magnitude larger than the one measured,
and the reason is recorded in the ledger rather than argued away here: the deep-staleness fraction
was computed from a drop rate belonging to a different rung of the ladder.

Two of these findings are about things a program office buys, and putting them beside a third that it
does not clarifies both. Among the purchasable levers, admission control at the console is worth
\FoConsoleLever{} of mission success at eight assets against a console that cannot refuse
($t = \FoConsoleT{}$, agreeing in 32 of 32 blocks), while moving the follower from a commanded link
to an on-platform sensor, with the console fixed either way, is worth \FoPlatformLever{} and is
equivalent to zero at a one-sided statistic of \FoPlatformTost{}. So of the two acquisitions the
console dominates and the sensing chain does not move at all. The larger quantity on the same table
is not an acquisition: driving the same scripted path at commanded spreads from ten to nine hundred
metres runs mission success from \FoSpreadTight{} to \FoSpreadWide{}. That is a tactical choice
about how to array the force, and it belongs in a different column of a program plan than either
lever above. The comparison is still worth making, because it says the formation stack's own
literature is optimising the smallest of the three terms.

The absolute success rates should therefore be read as comparative, not predictive: the value is in the \emph{ordering} of designs and the \emph{location} of crossovers, which is what a search loop consumes, not in any single percentage. The moving optimum's exact crossover cells near the contour are within Monte-Carlo noise, so what the study establishes is the direction of the boundary, not the precise cell. The Lanchester check validates the aggregate attrition law but not the spatial or command dynamics. The stronger forms of validation this class of model admits are left open: docking the emergent behavior against an independently built model \citep{axtell1996aligning}, pattern-oriented validation that asks the model to reproduce several observed tactical patterns at once \citep{grimm2005pattern}, and empirical comparison against exercise or high-fidelity ProjectGL traces. This is why the intended role of the model is to feed, not replace, high-fidelity validation.

We built \sandtable{}, a fast, reproducible, pure-Python mission-level testbed, as the inner-loop companion to the Army's high-fidelity ProjectGL, and used it to produce three decision-relevant maps for human-machine teaming under contested communications: a moving direct-to-supervisory control optimum, a speed-survivability frontier for route choice, and the EW-induced collapse of a sensor-to-shooter chain. Tracing the first of those maps to its cause gave the sharpest result: the operator is a queue with impatient clients, an operator blind to abandonment sustains its own overload past a collapse span given in closed form, and admission control at the console recovers most of what high span costs direct control. On the same core we added an opt-in kill web, suppression, finite munitions, and a belief state that jamming and decoys corrupt, so attrition emerges from the engagement stream and deception and sustainment are first-order effects, with the fixed-Pk baseline recovered exactly when the mechanics are off. Drawing on the Russo-Ukrainian war, we further added an EW-immune command link that holds direct control under jamming, a counter-UAS layer that turns the sensor swarm's monotone upside into an interior optimum in swarm size, and a cost-exchange metric that prices attritable mass, again recovering the prior model exactly when off. The tool obeys a recognized attrition law, runs a full mission in about a tenth of a second, and plugs directly into a Bayesian-optimization and DOE orchestrator, so the next step is to search these design spaces with optimization rather than grids, and to confirm the flagged designs in ProjectGL.

\subsection{The registered-claim ledger}
\label{subsec:ledger}

Every quantitative claim in Sections~\ref{subsec:queue} through \ref{subsec:attrib} was produced by an unattended search loop under one discipline: a conjecture and a falsifier are written down before the run that tests them, the gate is a paired blockwise contrast at a fixed tier, and a claim that meets its falsifier is retracted rather than salvaged. A reader has no way to tell that loop from a post-hoc one by looking at the results that survived, because both produce surviving results. What distinguishes them is the denominator. Table~\ref{tab:ledger} is the denominator.

Of the \ldClaims{} registered claims, three thresholds held against falsifiers that could have fired and did not. One mechanism was retracted on its own falsifier while the shape it predicted survived, and is reported that way rather than restated to fit. One rule was refuted on both the metric it was derived for and the metric a sponsor reads, and its revival clause was written in advance and then closed. One dominance claim died and ships as the weaker claim it can support. One prediction was refuted while something better survived it, because the statistic chosen to test it could not measure what it was pointed at. One was refuted, and then unrefuted: the burstiness reduction it predicted was absent at the window we first read it in and present in every pair at the window a bench says the statistic works in, so what the falsifier caught was our instrument rather than our mechanism, and the entry is in the table with both readings because the first one was published. One conjecture was cut before it ran at all, on the ground that a null on a knob we wrote ourselves, with a range we chose, is not evidence. \ldInstr{} of the rows are instruments rather than claims, and they are in the table under a criterion that keeps the list from becoming a collection of war stories: a defect earns a row only if it can name the guard that now makes its recurrence impossible, and the ledger's generator refuses to build a table containing one that cannot. They were found by adversarial review and by us in roughly equal measure, one of them inside a guard written specifically to prevent the error it failed to catch, and the repository ledger now numbers \ldGenus{} instances of a single shape: a convention correctly derived for its first use and then carried into a second where it does not hold. A materiality threshold expressed as a fraction of capacity and applied as an absolute rate. A queueing correction derived for a server that draws its service times, applied to one that does not. A byte-identity assertion that was correct while every value equalled itself and stopped being correct the moment two ratios became honestly undefined. A utilization derived for a console that will not begin a service while it is busy, read off one that books its work forward without bound and returns 4.74 where a fraction was promised. A mission allowance divided by the six methods that existed when the line was written, still dividing by six once there were eight. The recurrence is the finding: the shape is frequent enough to be worth searching for on purpose, and cheap enough to search for, because in every instance the question that exposes it is the same one. Ask what the denominator counts, and then ask whether the arm being compared changed it. They are in the table for the same reason the review's are, which is that a paper reporting only the instruments somebody else caught is reporting a selection. The criterion has teeth in both directions: the generator refused the second-to-last row on this list, written by the author of the criterion, until it named its guard.

\paragraph{A null is only as good as the range the metric had left.} The last of those instruments is worth stating as a rule, because it is the one a reader of any test report can apply. On this scenario, with no treatment at all, mission success runs \CeSuccFour{} at four assets and \CeSuccTwelve{} at twelve and then sits at exactly \CeSuccSixteen{} at sixteen, twenty-four and thirty-two, while blue loss over that same saturated range keeps falling from \CeLossSixteen{} to \CeLossThirtyTwo{}. Read the second number carefully, because it is a fraction and the sweep is its denominator: absolute losses over the same range fall only from \CeDeadSixteen{} vehicles to \CeDeadThirtyTwo{}, a factor of \CeDeadRatio{} against the fraction's \CeLossRatio{}, so most of what the fraction reports is a larger force and not fewer deaths. The residual is still real and still invisible to success. Within a cell the caution does not apply and the fraction is the right comparator, because every arm there shares one force size; it applies to reading the sweep, and the two get confused because they are printed in the same column. Every equivalence verdict this paper reports on mission success is therefore read at eight assets or fewer, where the metric has \CeSuccEight{} of its range in hand, and every large-span claim is read on a console metric or on loss. The general form is that an equivalence result carries an implicit second claim, that the instrument could have seen the difference had there been one, and that claim is checkable and is usually not checked. A test program that reports a capability as making no difference at the top of its intended range owes the same check, and the check is cheap: run the baseline across the range and look at whether the metric still moves without any treatment at all.

\paragraph{And the rule was tested rather than asserted, by putting the ceiling back down.} The caution above is easy to state and easy to wave away, because a reader can always answer that the loss axis is simply more sensitive and the capability really did stop helping. That answer is checkable. Reading the free backlog bound against the unbounded console, mission success prices it at \LsSuccEight{} at eight assets and then reports it \LsSuccTwentyFourV{} at twenty-four, \LsSuccTwentyFour{} with \LsSuccTwentyFourBlocks{} blocks agreeing, while blue loss over the same contrast \LsLossTwentyFourV{} at \LsLossTwentyFour{} with $t = \LsLossTwentyFourT{}$. At thirty-two assets success reads exactly \LsDeadEff{} with \LsDeadBlocks{} blocks agreeing, which is not a weak null but an instrument with no range left to spend, and loss still gates at \LsAliveEff{}. Two axes, one contrast, opposite reports.

The test that separates the two explanations does not change the axis. It changes the scenario, by strengthening the opposing force until baseline success is no longer pinned, and then asks the same question of the same metric. Success recovers the effect at the span where it had gone silent: \LsLiftedTwentyFour{} at twenty-four assets, \LsLiftedTwentyFourV{} with $t = \LsLiftedTwentyFourT{}$ and \LsLiftedTwentyFourBlocks{} blocks agreeing, against \LsSuccTwentyFour{} and \LsSuccTwentyFourBlocks{} for the identical contrast with the ceiling in place. One caveat travels with that comparison and is not disposed of: the opposing force was lifted to a single stronger value rather than laddered, so the result establishes that some restoration of headroom restores the reading, not that the recovery is monotone in headroom, and a ladder across opposing strength at fixed span is the experiment that would close it. What decays with span is the baseline's headroom, not the requirement's worth: the uncensored axis gates at every span, and restoring headroom at a fixed span restores the success reading. The claim stops there on purpose. At four opposing units and twenty-four assets, where baseline success is pinned, ``the bound buys no mission success here'' and ``the instrument cannot see what it buys here'' are one fact described at two levels, and no experiment that holds that operating point fixed can prise them apart, this one included. What the lifted scenario rules out is the reading a program office would actually act on, that the requirement has a span above which it stops being worth writing, since the same contrast at the same span pays in full once the opposing force is strong enough for the metric to have somewhere to move. A test program reporting a capability as making no difference at the top of its intended range is making two claims and usually defending one, and the unstated second claim, that the test could have seen the difference had there been one, is the one that fails here.

\paragraph{And the same rule has a second half, which we did not have until an axis told three different consoles apart and reported them identical.} The check above audits the arm the contrast is measured against: it asks whether the baseline still had room to move. An axis can fail the other way round. On a blind console that works requests whose asker has already given up, three repairs were compared on the share of operator clock spent on those requests. Reading a counter the console already keeps and skipping them costs nothing and removes all of that clock. Bounding the buffer costs a number in a specification. Refusing work at the door costs an admission rule. On the waste axis the three are not merely close: their per-block vectors are identical, every value exactly zero, so all three carry the same effect of \GhStaleTwentyFour{} and the same $t$ of \GhStaleT{} with every block agreeing. On the axis that counts clock producing no answer by any cause, the free repair is \GhDeadDropsV{} at \GhDeadDropsEff{} and the admitting console gates down at \GhDeadAdmitsEff{}. The free repair is worth \GhPeakShare{} percent of what refusing the work is worth at its best span and \GhLastShare{} percent at the largest, because the dead clock is conserved rather than recovered: the late bucket rises by \GhLateTwentyFour{} as the waste bucket falls by \GhStaleTwentyFour{}. An axis named after a waste cannot price a repair, because a repair defined as removing that waste sets the axis to zero by its own definition. Only the axis named after the loss can.

\paragraph{This is a second genus, and it is the one that more replication cannot reach.} Every instance of the shape above produced a wrong number, and one question exposes all of them: ask what the denominator counts, then ask whether the arm changed it. Ask that here and the answer is nothing, because the denominator is innocent. The exposing question is a different one, it is answerable before any run, and it is about reachability rather than arithmetic: can the event this metric counts occur at all under this arm's definition? What makes the family worth separating is that its members are all accurate. The clock share really is zero, the effect really is \GhStaleTwentyFour{}, the $t$ really is \GhStaleT{}, and nothing anywhere is miscomputed. That is exactly why no amount of replication, range checking or benching touches it: the reading is a theorem, and theorems replicate perfectly, so more data makes the statistic larger while the information stays at zero. A saturated instrument is contingently blind and a different scenario restores it. An entailed one is necessarily uninformative and no scenario, seed or span changes it. The recurrence is again the finding, and it is not rare: sweeping every battery this paper rests on, an arm sits at a value fixed by its own definition in \PnPinned{} of \PnCells{} readings, about \PnPinnedPct{} percent, and in \PnSamePair{} of those two or more arms sit at the same such value and cannot be ordered against each other at all. That frequency is not an accident of these experiments. It is the shadow of a design choice we would make again, since every mechanism that can be switched off entails zeros on its own metrics whenever it is off.

\paragraph{And a third, which is a property of the decision rule rather than of the metric.} The gate this paper reads every contrast through asks for three things, and one of them is that \FlSign{} of blocks agree in sign. Mission success is zero or one per mission, so a block mean over \FlReps{} missions moves in steps of one over \FlReps{}, and a block can register a difference at all only if one of its missions flips. The fraction of blocks that can agree is therefore capped at $1-(1-p)^{r}$ for a per-mission flip rate $p$ over $r$ missions, and the sign requirement becomes a floor on that rate of $1 - \FlSign^{1/r}$, which at \FlReps{} missions per block is \FlFloor{}. Because the reported effect cannot exceed the flip rate, an effect under \FlFloor{} clears the gate only if the treatment is also moving a comparable number of missions the other way, which is the case where the difference is noise. The consequence is the part worth carrying away, because it points opposite to instinct: more blocks do not lower this floor at all, and only more missions per block do. At sixteen missions per block it sits at \FlFloorSixteen{}, at two hundred fifty-six at \FlFloorTwoFiftySix{}, at one thousand twenty-four at \FlFloorTenTwentyFour{}. A study that finds itself just short of a verdict and answers by running the sweep again has bought nothing.

The arithmetic needs no data, and the audit that prices it is unusually sharp. Of the \FlPublished{} contrasts that feed a number in this paper, \FlCounted{} are on mission success, which is the only metric here whose block means genuinely step in one over the mission count. Which metrics those are was settled by asking the logs rather than by reading names, since deciding it from a name would have been the first genus committed inside the check for the third: success steps that way in 98.8 percent of its readings, the loss fraction in 12.6 percent because its denominator counts vehicles and not missions, and delivered service in 1.4 percent. Among those \FlCounted{}, \FlGated{} gated, and the number of them sitting below the floor is \FlGatedBelow{}. The smallest effect that ever gated is \FlSmallestGated{}; the largest that read equivalent while under the floor is \FlLargestEqBelow{}, which is \FlMargin{} below the predicted \FlFloor{}. One counterexample would have refuted the account, and seventy battery logs hold none.

\FlEqBelow{} equivalence verdicts here sit under the floor, and most of them are unremarkable: the effect is small, the $t$ statistic misses too, and the null is honest on every count. The ones that matter are the \FlSignOnly{} where $t$ CLEARS the gate and the sign criterion alone produces the null. There, two of the three tests say the arms differ and the third cannot speak at all. The most extreme reads $t = \FlSignOnlyMaxT{}$ on an effect of \FlSignOnlyMaxEff{} and is reported as equivalent on \FlSignOnlyBlocks{} blocks agreeing, because at \FlReps{} missions a block that size can seldom register the difference even when it is there. Those \FlSignOnly{} are not verdicts about the world. They are verdicts about the block size, and at that block size no amount of replication would have changed one of them. The three genera now have three different exposing questions and they are asked at three different times. Ask of a number what its denominator counts and whether the arm changed it. Ask of a metric whether the event it counts can occur at all under this arm's definition. And ask of a verdict what the smallest effect its own decision rule could have told apart from zero is, and whether what was observed clears it. The last two are answerable at registration, before anything runs, from the arm definitions and the block size alone. The honest reason all three are in this paper is that we were asking only the first.

\paragraph{What it cost, and the part of this we got wrong first.} Because the frequency is high the audit had to be run rather than reasoned about. Of the \PnContrasts{} contrasts that feed a number printed in this paper, \PnCost{} compare two arms pinned at the same value, and both sit in a negative control whose entire purpose is to demonstrate that nothing happens where nothing should. No claim here rests on one. That outcome is not luck, and understanding why located our own error. Every contrast we computed was against a designated reference, and every one of those is sound; the unreadable comparison was a RANKING among three treatments that nobody ever computed, assembled by eye from three separate contrasts against a common baseline. Put that ranking through the same machinery and it is refused twice over, because a paired test on identical vectors returns zero by construction and an equivalence margin proportional to a zero baseline admits nothing. The tools already declined the claim. What was missing was the obligation to ask them, so the repair is not a new statistic but a rule about which comparison must be formed: admissibility belongs to a contrast rather than to an axis, and any reading across arms has to be computed as a contrast among those arms before it is believed. The rule stated at the head of this section, that the diagnosis comes from the reference arm alone, is the special case where the claim's reference is the battery's designated one. Stated without that qualifier it was a convention correctly derived for its first use and carried into a second where it has no referent, which is precisely the shape this section is about. The guard against the first genus contained an instance of it.

The point of the table is not that the loop was mostly right. It is that the loop was sometimes wrong, in public, on record, and before the numbers were seen. Nothing in Sections~\ref{subsec:queue} through \ref{subsec:attrib} would look different if we had quietly dropped the \ldFailed{} entries that failed.

\subsection{Extensions}
\label{subsec:extensions}

The cheapest gains come from hardening the findings the model already produces. A global sensitivity analysis over the operator parameters (service rate, attention capacity, and patience) and the C0--C5 latency and drop values, using Morris screening or Sobol indices, would report each crossover as a band with a variance decomposition that says which inputs actually move it. That converts the moving optimum from a point estimate, whose exact cells sit within Monte-Carlo noise, into a boundary with stated confidence, and it names the few parameters worth measuring. Two calibration steps would supply those measurements: fitting the command model to human-subject supervisory-control data rather than the qualitative anchor used here \citep{cummings2007capacity,chen2011supervisory,hocraffer2017meta}, and Bayesian calibration of the lethality and link parameters against high-fidelity ProjectGL runs \citep{stanko2025projectgl}, so the fast tier inherits the engine's numbers where they set the crossover.

Richer mechanisms would close the fidelity gaps that bound the present conclusions. Replacing the binary range gate and the flat line-of-sight stub with a range-dependent detection probability, true line-of-sight on the trafficability grid, and a false-alarm rate would sharpen the recon-cueing chain that drives the swarm study and the shared picture in the centerpiece. A reactive red force that maneuvers, seeks cover, and actively hunts the reconnaissance would lift every finding off the assumption of a static defender; the counter-UAS layer of Section~\ref{subsec:drone} already gives swarm size a survivability cost, and the natural next step is to let that air defense maneuver and deplete a finite magazine of interceptors rather than fire from fixed sites. A further contested-conditions axis the war has made central is satellite-navigation denial: modeling GNSS jamming and spoofing as a drift-and-bias process on each platform's own position estimate, degrading navigation and geolocation the way the C0--C5 ladder degrades the link, would let position uncertainty act as a first-order effect and compose with the EW-immune link and counter-UAS mechanics already in place. The single operator is now a bank of $c$ interchangeable consoles, which is what makes the staffing curve of Table~\ref{tab:staffing} and the $c$ in Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} meaningful, but the consoles are identical and the assignment is to whichever frees first. What remains is the part that makes a command post a command post: heterogeneous operators, dynamic span reallocation, explicit handoff, and a triage discipline that serves the request most likely to still be waited on rather than the one at the head of the line. Equation~\ref{eq:nstar} sizes the bank; it does not tell it how to work.

The largest extensions point the testbed at the program's learning and analytics objectives. Swapping the scripted heuristics for reinforcement-learning and imitation policies would test the direct-versus-supervisory tradeoff on adaptive teaming behavior rather than fixed rules, and would let the same policies transfer to the Learning Agents runtime inside ProjectGL. A deep-learning surrogate with uncertainty quantification, fitted to the response surfaces and queried by the Bayesian-optimization loop \citep{balandat2020botorch} with an acquisition rule that targets high-variance regions, would cut the number of simulator calls each search needs. Building on the kill-web mechanics of Section~\ref{subsec:killweb}, the entity state a full kill web still lacks, a command and connectivity graph and fuel-and-resupply sustainment, would let command-post disruption and logistics act as first-order effects the way suppression and munitions now do. Broadening the use-case library beyond the UC-7 deception case toward the remaining trust cases in the design, confident-but-wrong recommendations and thermal decoys, would exercise the engineered-ground-truth machinery for the trust-calibration metric and let pattern-mining over the run logs surface the success and failure signatures the program set out to detect.
